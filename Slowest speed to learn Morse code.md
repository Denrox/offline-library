# Slowest speed to learn Morse code

*Tags: morse-code · score 4*

## Question

I am trying to learn Morse code using online decoders (like LCWO and hamradio.solutions) for keying and a terminal program (a modified version of aldo) for copying. I am using the Koch/Farnsworth method, and I have aldo configured to key ten blocks of five characters each at 25wpm keyed/5wpm effective speed. I am copying directly into the terminal as I can type far faster than I can write.

The issue that I'm having is that I can't seem to keep my error rate low enough to progress. In keeping with Koch, I have to copy with 90% accuracy before the next character is introduced, and I can't keep my error rate low enough to make progress. I have covered the letters kmrsuaptlowi.n out of kmrsuaptlowi.njef0y,vg5/q9zh38b?427c1d6x. I can usually maintain 70%-80% accuracy over 50 characters, but it is hard to get beyond that.

I think the problem is that I am getting mentally fatigued after the first few blocks, and once I miss one character, I end up hesitating and missing the rest of the block. I also find that I start off practice sessions with an accuracy in the mid 80s, but it falls over time. I feel like I would benefit (at least in terms of progressing through the character set) by either setting the effective speed lower or reducing the number of characters per block. However, my concern is that, if I make it to easy now, I will have to do more work later when I inevitably have to listen to longer strings with proper timing. I have looked into recommended speeds for learning and found that Long Island CW Club starts at 8 wpm and the ARRL practice files start at 5wpm.

Does anyone know what the slowest recommended speed for initially learning Morse code is? Relatedly, is it beneficial to learn the character set at a much lower speed before moving on to decoding strings? I am also open to the idea that I am making some error in my learning method, so I welcome frame challenges as well. Unfortunately, I do not have easy access to my local ham club for guidance, so I am mainly teaching myself through online content.

## Accepted answer (score 4, by Ryuji AB1WX)

You can drop to 18 or even 15 wpm code speed and Farnsworth to 12 or 8 or 5 wpm if that's what you can copy at this time. A code speed of 15 wpm is probably the slowest I would recommend, but there is no hard rule about the limit. If you are counting dots and dashes, the code speed is too slow. If you are counting, you will develop copying skill that only works for very slow codes; the skill will not translate and become an obstacle in increasing the speed.

Blocks of five should be easy if you are writing down and the letter speed is slow enough, but if necessary, drop it to 4 or 3.

But if you can tolerate, keep the fastest letter speed and longest block length you can work with, and increase the space between the blocks. Increasing the inter-block space is more preferred way to "slow down" for practice purposes, rather than super slow Farnsworth speed.

Keep copying as long as you can, but if you feel fatigued, take a quick break. It is much better to practice when your brain functions fully and you can concentrate. Also, switch places/environment of practice. Studying at the same desk is the worst. (That is shown in neuroscience research many times.)

Alternating receive and send practices is another good approach, once you can copy 8 or 13 wpm range. Learning the same or related subjects in multiple different ways is also known to be an effective learning method in neuroscience research. I recommend using a single lever paddle, but a squeeze paddle or straight key is also a fine choice. Do not practice sending at a speed faster than you can copy comfortably. Focus on accuracy and quality of the code.

When you identify the patterns of confusion in receiving, you can practice sending those letters in particular at the same speed (but without Farnsworth, which is strictly for receive practice only) is helpful in removing the confusion faster, than just practicing receiving only.

Once things become easier, always stretch the parameters. This is more important than worrying about the slowest acceptable practice speed. One run of practice should stretch speed, while another run block size or the total duration. If you don't stretch, practice does not help. In other words, if you are getting near-perfect performance and feel comfortable with the speed, that is not really a practice.

Also, there are plenty of people who will call you back if you call CQ at 8 wpm or 12 wpm, or whatever speed you are comfortable with. Try around 7.114 MHz in the evening. Do not delay getting on the air. Screwing up on the air (which I did, although I thought I was prepared enough) is a good way to accelerate your skill acquisition.

Also, do not delay practicing head copying or "copy behind," not just writing down every letter. I can copy about 18-22 wpm using simplified Palmer-like script, but not much faster. In block letters, it'll be even slower. Head copying is faster and essential when you exchange more info than RST, OP name, and QTH. If I were to redo my Morse practice experience, I would limit writing down and switch to practice copy behind. Head copy practice is of course best to start with somewhat short blocks and lengthen the block as you become more proficient.

Another tip. When you practice using a computer or pre-generated MP3 files, you get clean signals without fading or noise or QRM. In real life, these things will distract you, and I had to ask for repeats many times. I recommend you take your Morse practice setup to a subway station, a nearby park, a bench on a busy street, and various places with lots of distractions. If you perform ok in those places, that'll help on the air. Another thing I did was to practice receive while playing a podcast and a music simultaneously. But practicing in noisy streets, train stations, etc. are more effective.

The distraction training is not so much about focusing attention 100% to the signal but to copy without using 100% of the mental bandwidth. Things become closer to automatic as you train. (This may be a topic for a few weeks down the road at this point.)

If you have a portable station setup, check out Parks on the Air. That's what I did for my CW practice. I did practice as above, and my first day of POTA activation, calling CQ POTA at 18 wpm, was my first day on the air. In POTA, the activator operating in a park calls CQ, and hunters call. You can call CQ at whatever speed you want, and everyone else needs to deal with it. Same thing in Summits on the Air. Few people exchange more than callsign and RST, but it is still a very useful practice in copying callsigns.

## Answer (score 2, by Caol)

Back in the day (1970's!) I learned Morse Code the old way. Flash cards to learn all the characters and then, starting at 5wpm, on a manual typewriter, with full messages. This included message formatting and structure and included plain language, 5 letter code and numbers and punctuation. 900 to 1200 characters per session.

As accuracy (90% minimum/95% good) went up, so did speed, usually in two or three WPM jumps. Almost everyone found "plateaus" where it seemed to take forever to get the accuracy at the new speed. Usually a small bump in the 8 to 12WPM zone and then again in the 16-20WPM area.

What's happening is that you are training your ears and your fingers. Your ears for pattern recognition and that is used to trigger a learned reflex with your fingers on the keyboard. The first plateau being more tied to pattern recognition and the upper plateau to training the finger reflex.

So, as webmarc mentioned, you may want to drop the character transmission speed down to where you're sure you've got the pattern recognition down pat then step up the characters/min and alternate character speed increases and characters/min and try and keep your accuracy.

The thing to remember, is everybody learns things like this slightly differently and it's not going to happen overnight. Keep on plugging!

Another thing to note is the audio frequency of the code. The internal resonance with your ears can make a difference. Usually somewhere 500-1000Hz (personally my favourite BFO offset was 650Hz)
