## Tactical Combat Casualty Care Overview

3-1. In accordance with DODI 1322.24, medical readiness training (also known as MRT) encompasses all facets of medical support across the competition continuum and forms the cornerstone of effective force health protection. TCCC serves as the DOD standard of care for both nonmedical and medical first responders. The TCCC-All Service member course supersedes previous trauma skills taught in self-aid and buddy aid courses, mandating that all Service members receive role-based TCCC training and certification aligned with their assigned skill level as outlined by the JTS. DODI 6040.47 designates the JTS as the DOD’s Center of Excellence for trauma management.

3-2. Service members performing self-aid, buddy aid, and CLS support prioritize their primary combat mission, rendering aid as a secondary mission when feasible. While these roles do not replace the specialized skills of combat medics, corpsmen, and healthcare technicians, they may assist in providing far-forward care and preparing casualties for evacuation if mission and time permit.

3-3. Self-aid, buddy aid, and CLS support are integral components of a comprehensive plan aimed at caring for wounded Service members. A significant advantage of individual Service members providing such support is their proximity to injured squad or crew members. This proximity allows for more immediate care compared to waiting for a combat medic, especially in situations where the medic may be occupied with treating other seriously wounded personnel. First responders and CLS personnel are trained to administer critical immediate care, including addressing severe bleeding and performing needle chest decompression for tension pneumothorax, potentially saving lives in the crucial moments following injury.

## Service Specific Skills for Tactical Combat Casualty Care

3-4. Individual medical readiness training is built upon a foundation of knowledge, skills, and abilities tailored to operational medicine. Operational medical readiness training focuses on developing a core set of validated, operationally unique skills and abilities necessary to accomplish mission objectives. These core competencies are expanded as needed to address specific Service missions, informing unit readiness reporting as part of medical specialty team readiness. DODI 1322.24, published by the JTS, outlines basic TCCC skills

for all Service members, allowing individual Services to augment these requirements based on their specific needs.

3-5. TCCC skills are categorized into four tiers, spanning from all Service members to credentialed providers, with each tier building upon the knowledge and skills of the preceding tier. Each tier represents a progression in the complexity and scope of medical interventions, enabling Service members to provide increasingly advanced care to injured personnel. The tiered approach ensures that personnel are trained to the appropriate level based on their roles and responsibilities within their respective units or specialties.

3-6. Tier 1, or All Service member skills, encompasses fundamental skills taught to all Service members, focusing on basic hemorrhage control and recognition of serious injuries. This tier aims to equip every Service member with essential lifesaving capabilities, enabling them to provide immediate care to injured comrades in the battlefield environment. Training in Tier 1 skills is critical for ensuring that all Service members are prepared to respond effectively to medical emergencies, regardless of their primary occupational specialties.

3-7. Tier 2, or CLS skills, involves more advanced care aimed at addressing the most common causes of battlefield fatalities. This tier emphasizes recognizing, preventing, and communicating life-threatening complications to medical personnel. CLS training provides select Service members with enhanced capabilities to stabilize casualties and facilitate their evacuation to higher levels of care, thereby improving overall survival rates in combat environment.

3-8. Tier 3 or Combat Medic and Corpsman skills contains the skills that are much more advanced and focus on invasive care. This tier of skills requires significantly more medical knowledge and skills than self-aid, buddy aid, or CLS.

3-9. Tier 4 or Combat Paramedic and Provider skills are for combat paramedics and advanced providers. This tier provides the most sophisticated care to keep wounded Service members alive and get them to definitive care.

CLS training equips Soldiers with necessary skills and knowledge to stabilize various types of casualties and to mitigate the worsening of injuries until medical assistance arrives.

Additionally, CLS skills should be exercised during deployment for training, such as rotations through combat training centers. This ongoing training ensures that CLS personnel remain competent, and ready to provide lifesaving care in diverse operational environments.

While exceptions exist, for The United States Army Special Operations Command surgeons, modifications to aid bags must adhere to validated mission requirements and receive approval from The United States Army Special Operations Command Defense Collaboration Services, Surgeon. In combat scenarios, where medical supplies may be rapidly depleted, emergency resupply options include combat medics, ambulances, BAS, or nearby MTFs. However, it's important to exhaust supplies from the casualty's first aid kit before tapping into resources from the CLS bag.

## Tactical Combat Casualty Care Phases

**3-36.** The TCCC framework encompasses three critical phases of care tailored to the dynamic challenges of battlefield medicine. These phases, structured to address varying levels of urgency and complexity, are Care Under Fire (CUF), Tactical Field Care (TFC), and Tactical Evacuation Care (also known as TACEVAC). Mastery of these phases equips military units and leaders with the proficiency needed to deliver prompt and efficient medical assistance to injured Service members in combat scenarios. Such skills play a pivotal role in preserving life, mitigating the potential for long-term disability, and optimizing the prospects of full recovery for wounded personnel.

### Care Under Fire

3-37. **_Care under fire_ is the first phase of care in tactical combat casualty care where very limited care is provided for the casualty while the enemy threat is still active. Also known as CUF or care under threat** . During CUF, the primary focus is on providing immediate life-saving care while still under enemy fire. This phase involves skills such as returning fire, moving the casualty to a safer location, and controlling severe bleeding using techniques like TQs. The goal is to stabilize the casualty quickly to prevent further injury or death. In CUF, available medical equipment is limited to what is carried by individual responders or casualties, and the only medical priority is addressing massive extremity bleeding due to the ongoing threat in a potentially chaotic and dangerous situation.

3-38. The order of actions during CUF is mission-variable dependent, with scene safety being paramount. For instance, if the threat is suppressed and the tactical situation allows massive life-threatening bleeding and extremity bleeding should be addressed prior to patient movement from the POI. However, each scenario is different, and the ordering of actions could vary accordingly. It's crucial to remember that in the combat environment, there is no "time-out" when casualties occur. Rapid medical and tactical decisions must be made simultaneously, as good medicine can sometimes be bad tactics. Potentially, endangering the lives of responders and jeopardizing the mission's success.

3-39. The tactical situation dictates the initial actions during CUF, with little time available for casualty care while under effective enemy fire. Prioritizing suppressing hostile fire and gaining fire superiority is crucial to minimizing the risk of injury to personnel and preventing further harm to the casualty. Wounded Service members should be directed to continue returning fire, moving quickly to nearby cover, and performing selfaid if able. It's essential not to become a casualty oneself, assessing the situation, suppressing enemy fire, and developing a plan before moving to care for a casualty under fire.

3-40. In CUF, early control of severe bleeding is the number one medical priority, as extremity hemorrhage is the most frequent cause of preventable battlefield deaths. Major vessel injuries can quickly lead to shock and death, making it crucial to identify and address life-threatening bleeding promptly. Only massive bleeding warrants intervention during CUF, with treatment of non-life-threatening bleeding deferred until the TFC phase. Despite the grave danger posed by enemy fire, applying a hasty TQ quickly and effectively remains essential in this phase (if this tactical situation allows) to save lives and prevent further casualties.

#### Scene Safety

3-41. CUF is defined as the aid rendered at the trauma scene while there is still an active threat. The scene in CUF is never safe, however, the active threat can be reduced. The responder can help others to reduce the threat (return gunfire, or firefighting) and to enable rescuers to reach the casualty and move the casualty to cover. The responder should not risk their personal safety or the safety of the casualty if the threat has not been reduced.

#### Control of Massive Bleeding

3-42. In the CUF phase, the primary focus is on controlling massive bleeding to prevent hypovolemic shock, a critical and potentially fatal condition. Effective management of severe bleeding is essential to stabilize the casualty and ensure survival until further medical treatment can be administered. For detailed strategies and techniques on controlling massive bleeding, see Chapter 4, which provides comprehensive guidance on the procedures and equipment necessary for this vital emergency intervention.

#### Casualty Movement

3-43. If a casualty is unresponsive or unable to move, a rescue plan should be devised and executed if tactically feasible. Do not put two people at risk if it can be avoided. If cover is not available or the wounded Service member cannot move to cover, they should lie flat and motionless. For more information on casualty movement techniques see chapter 2, paragraph 2-6 of this publication.

#### Proper Communication and Documentation

3-44. Communication is vital in providing effective care for casualties, involving interaction with the casualty, unit leaders, and on-scene medical personnel. Rescuers should communicate with the casualty as soon as they can safely reach them, by assessing their level of consciousness and orientation. It's crucial to reassure conscious casualties and request assistance from other responders or medics when available, as rescuers are rarely alone in such situations.

3-45. Documentation of all care provided to casualties is essential, primarily utilizing DD Form 1380. This form should include details such as the casualty's name, sex, time of injury, the nature of the injury, along with findings from rapid casualty assessments and the medical aid provided. This information is critical for the continuity of care and should be securely attached to the casualty or recorded directly on them if the form is unavailable.

3-46. Securing DD Form 1380 on the casualty, by attaching it to their belt loop or placing it in their pocket ensures it accompanies them to the next level of care. If the form is unavailable, pertinent information can be directly written on the casualty's body or on a strip of medical tape. This documentation is vital for ensuring comprehensive and effective care throughout the casualty's treatment process. (See figure 15-2 on page 154 for an example of a completed DD Form 1380).

#### Combat Lifesaver Role in Care Under Fire

3-47. In the context of CUF, the CLS assumes a role similar to individuals providing self-aid. Their primary objective is to suppress hostile fire to minimize the risk of injury to personnel and further harm to wounded Service members. The CLS utilizes available medical equipment, which is typically limited to what the individual responder, casualty's first aid kit, or CLS bag carries. It's emphasized that the first responder and CLS should prioritize the use of the casualty's first aid kit before resorting to other resources. See Appendix A, paragraph A-7 for further discussion on the CLS bag.

3-48. Given the urgency of the situation, the sequence of actions in CUF is dictated by the tactical environment. With limited time available for casualty care amidst enemy fire, suppressing hostile fire and gaining fire superiority are prioritized to protect both personnel and casualties. This may involve personnel assisting in returning fire instead of immediately attending to casualties, especially if the casualty can still engage in combat. Wounded Service members exposed to enemy fire are directed to seek cover, perform self-aid if possible, and continue returning fire while moving to safety.

3-49. In CUF, the primary focus is on recognizing and halting massive bleeding while seeking cover to shield oneself and the casualty from hostile fire. Signs of massive bleeding, such as pulsing or steady bleeding from wounds or traumatic amputations, must be promptly addressed. The immediate application of a hasty TQ is recommended if indicated, as injuries to major veins or arteries can lead to shock or death within minutes. Extremity hemorrhage, a common cause of preventable combat death, underscores the critical role of TQs in saving lives during CUF scenarios.

3-50. Prompt application of TQs to halt life-threatening bleeding not only saves lives but also enables injured Service members to continue fighting while awaiting further medical care and evacuation. It's emphasized that all Service members with access to a first aid kit should be trained in TQ use, highlighting the importance of preparedness and training in combat lifesaving techniques.

### Tactical Field Care

3-51. **_Tactical field care_ is the second phase of care in tactical combat casualty care. Care provided once the threat has been neutralized, the scene is safe, or the casualty has moved or been moved out of the immediate threat situation. Also known as TFC** . This phase permits more extensive medical interventions, including the assessment and management of airway, breathing, circulation, and other injuries. Essential skills such as wound dressing, pain management, blood or blood product resuscitation, and fluid resuscitation for burns are pivotal during this phase. TFC also encompasses scenarios in a combat or tactical environment not involving direct enemy fire, such as parachute injuries in a combat zone.

3-52. Transitioning from CUF, TFC provides the opportunity for a more deliberate approach to casualty assessment and treatment, given the time and relative safety available. However, the tactical situation remains dynamic, and the potential for reverting to a CUF scenario always exists. In TFC, both the casualty and the first responder or CLS should maintain cover and concealment. The primary management plan involves maintaining tactical situational awareness, triaging casualties as needed, and conducting a thorough casualty assessment.

3-53. Mission personnel are tasked with establishing a security perimeter according to unit tactical SOP or battle drills, maintaining security, and remaining vigilant for potential threats from hostile forces. Casualties in shock or under the influence of controlled substances pose risks to themselves and others. Hence, those with altered mental status should have their weapons cleared and secured, communications secured, and sensitive items redistributed.

3-54. During the TFC phase, responders have more time and relatively greater safety to administer further medical care. They may need to continue treatment until medical personnel arrive, assisting them with continued treatment and preparation for evacuation. However, the anticipated time for evacuation to the next level of care may vary based on the operational situation, requiring CLS to remain prepared to reengage the enemy and resume the mission.

3-55. While medical personnel prioritize casualty treatment, coordination between responders and unit leaders is crucial for requesting evacuation assets based on the operational situation. Despite the expanded scope of care in TFC, available medical equipment remains limited to what is carried into the field by the

casualty's first aid kit, the CLS, and other responders' first aid kits or supplies in a medic's aid bag. It's emphasized that supplies within the casualty's first aid kit should be used first before resorting to other available supplies.

### Tactical Evacuation Care

3-56. **_Tactical evacuation care_ is the third phase of care in tactical combat casualty care. Care provided during transportation from the battlefield to a medical treatment facility.** Tactical evacuation care is the phase of TCCC focused on preparing the casualty for evacuation to a higher role of care, typically an MTF. This phase encompasses both preparing the casualty for evacuation and executing the evacuation process while ensuring continued monitoring and treatment of the casualty's condition. Skills required during tactical evacuation care include managing the casualty's airway and breathing, maintaining circulation, and providing ongoing medical interventions as necessary.

3-57. The NATO term of tactical MEDEVAC refers to the intra-theater movement of patients between MTFs within the joint operational area. This process involves transferring patients from one MTF to another using ground, rotary, or fixed-wing assets, along the lines of communication within the joint operational area. In the U.S. Army, this includes MEDEVAC operations between different roles of care, from Role 1 through Role 3, supported by both ground and air ambulances. However, the term "TACEVAC" should not be used to describe the tactical evacuation care phase of TCCC and to prevent confusion between the evacuation process itself and a specific phase of TCCC.

3-58. The primary principle in tactical evacuation care is to continue monitoring and providing appropriate assessment and treatment until the casualty is transferred to medical personnel or reaches a higher level of care. During this phase, first responders or CLSs conduct a deliberate assessment to identify and treat previously unrecognized life-threatening injuries. While similar to TFC in many aspects, the presence of additional medical personnel and equipment on the evacuation asset may enable the provision of more extensive care and casualty management.

3-59. Non-medical first responders or CLSs are not expected to provide care during evacuation. However, if they do, the skills used in CUF and TFC still apply. Pre-evacuation procedures involve documenting all assessments and care provided during CUF and TFC on DD Form 1380. Additionally, an evacuation request using the 9-line format is initiated according to unit SOP to initiate MEDEVAC.

3-60. During staging, each casualty is reassessed, and their medical condition is monitored to ensure they receive necessary care while waiting for evacuation. This step is vital for maintaining the stability of casualties and adjusting medical interventions as needed before they are transported to a higher level of medical care. Communication plays a key role during this phase, as it helps reduce the casualties' anxiety and provides them reassurance about the care, they are receiving While, keeping the casualty informed and as calm as possible under the circumstances.

3-61. Overall, tactical evacuation care ensures that casualties receive continuous monitoring, assessment, and treatment during the evacuation process until they reach a higher level of care. It involves coordination between medical personnel, tactical leaders, and other responders to ensure a smooth and efficient evacuation process in challenging tactical environments.

3-62. The proper procedure for performing a 9-line MEDEVAC request is critical in tactical environments to ensure timely evacuation support for casualties. Here's a detailed outline of the steps involved:

- Collect Information: Gather all necessary information required for the MEDEVAC request, including the casualty's condition, location, and other pertinent details, using authorized brevity codes.

- Initiate Contact: Clearly announce "I HAVE A MEDEVAC REQUEST" over the radio frequency designated for communication with the unit controlling evacuation assets. Wait for one to three seconds for a response. If there is no response, repeat the statement.

- Transmit MEDEVAC Request: Contact the unit controlling evacuation assets using the proper call sign and frequency assignment as specified in the signal operating instructions from an automated net control device.

- Sequence and Format: Transmit the MEDEVAC information in the proper sequence, stating all line numbers clearly. Line numbers 1 through 5 must be transmitted within 25 seconds during the initial

contact with the evacuation unit. Lines 6 through 9 can be transmitted while the aircraft or vehicle is en route.

- Follow Procedure: Adhere to the procedure provided in the explanation column of the MEDEVAC request format, ensuring all required information is transmitted accurately.

- Radio Procedures: Pronounce letters and numbers according to appropriate radio procedures to ensure clarity and understanding.

- Use "BREAK": Use "BREAK" on long lines of information to break up the transmission and reduce its length, facilitating better reception.

- End Transmission: Conclude the transmission by stating "OVER" to indicate the completion of your message and readiness to receive further instructions or acknowledgment from the evacuation unit.

- Maintain Radio Communication: Keep the radio on and listen for additional instructions or contact from the evacuation unit, remaining available for any further communication regarding the MEDEVAC request.

3-63. By following these steps and adhering to proper radio protocols, Service members can effectively transmit a 9-line MEDEVAC request, facilitating prompt and efficient evacuation support for casualties in tactical environments. For additional information on requesting MEDEVAC and the MEDEVAC standard format refer to ATP 4-02.2.

## Tactical Combat Casualty Care During Operations

3-64. During operations, the battlefield is often widely dispersed and rapidly moving, posing challenges for the rapid acquisition of casualties and medical treatment. As a result, medical platoon personnel may face limitations in providing immediate, far-forward medical care. To address this, the plan is to integrate CLS into the system of providing care for wounded Service members. The three primary goals of TCCC are to treat casualties, prevent additional casualties, and complete the mission.

3-65. When a nonmedical Service member encounters an unconscious or injured casualty, they must quickly assess the situation and casualty's condition to determine if it's safe and if immediate first aid measures are necessary to prevent further injury or death. They should ascertain if trained medical personnel are available or direct others at the scene to call for or locate trained medical personnel. Once first aid is initiated, efforts should continue without interruption until medical personnel take over. Service members may also need to rely on their own first aid skills for self-aid. The CLS serves as a link between the self-aid or buddy aid skills taught to all Service members and the medical training provided to medical personnel.

3-66. Immediate medical care in far-forward positions is crucial in a dispersed and dynamic operational environment to prevent casualties from succumbing to wounds. In situations where medical personnel may not reach all wounded Service members promptly, the CLS plays a vital role in providing advanced lifesaving measures beyond basic self-aid or buddy aid. A well-trained CLS can stabilize various types of casualties and mitigate the deterioration of a wounded Service member's condition until medical personnel arrive. However, functioning as a CLS is a secondary mission undertaken when the tactical situation allows.

3-67. One significant advantage of the CLS is their proximity to injured unit members. While medical personnel may take several minutes or longer to reach casualties, especially when attending to severely wounded individuals, the CLS can provide immediate lifesaving care, such as controlling severe bleeding and performing needle chest decompressions for tension pneumothorax.

3-68. When not engaged in combat duties, CLS personnel may also assist medical personnel in providing care and preparing casualties for evacuation. The role and responsibilities of the CLS are delineated in different phases of care:

- In CUF, the CLS responds by suppressing hostile fire to minimize the risk of injury to personnel and prevent additional harm to previously injured Service members.

- In TFC, the CLS maintains security and situational awareness while attending casualties and preparing them for evacuation.

- In Tactical Evacuation, support the Combat Medic or Corpsman, as directed.

3-69. CLS personnel are specifically trained to tackle the primary causes of battlefield fatalities, which include massive hemorrhage and airway or respiratory issues. They possess the skills necessary to prevent complications and manage other injuries that, while not immediately life-threatening, could become serious

if left untreated. This training ensures that CLS personnel are well-prepared to provide critical first aid in combat situations, enhancing the survival chances of those injured in the field.

### Key Factors Influencing Tactical Combat Casualty Care

3-70. Prehospital care in the combat setting differs significantly from the civilian setting due to various factors that influence TCCC. These factors include:

- Hostile Fire: Combat environments are characterized by the presence of hostile fire, posing an immediate threat to both casualties and responders. This necessitates the integration of tactical considerations into casualty care protocols.

- Tactical Considerations: Tactical priorities and objectives often dictate the approach to casualty care. The safety of personnel and the completion of the mission take precedence, influencing how and when medical interventions are performed.

- Environmental Considerations: Combat operations can occur in diverse environments, ranging from urban areas to remote and hostile terrain. Responders must adapt their care techniques to address environmental challenges such as extreme weather conditions, limited visibility, and difficult terrain.

- Wounding Patterns: In combat, injuries are often caused by explosive devices, firearms, and other weapons unique to warfare. Understanding these specific wounding patterns is crucial for effective casualty management and prioritization of care.

- Equipment Constraints: Medical resources and equipment may be limited or unavailable in the combat setting, requiring improvisation and prioritization of essential interventions. Responders must work within the constraints of available resources to provide optimal care.

- Delays Reaching Higher Roles of Care: Evacuation to higher levels of care may be delayed in combat situations due to operational factors, such as ongoing enemy engagement or logistical challenges. This necessitates prolonged on-site care and management of casualties by first responders.

- Level of First Responder Training and Experience: The training and experience level of first responders, including CLS and medical personnel, significantly impacts the quality and effectiveness of prehospital care in combat. Adequate training ensures responders are proficient in TCCC principles and can adapt to dynamic battlefield conditions.

3-71. Overall, these factors shape the unique challenges and considerations involved in providing prehospital care in the combat setting, highlighting the importance of specialized training and protocols like TCCC for managing battlefield casualties.

### Fire Superiority in Tactical Combat Casualty Care

3-72. Fire superiority is paramount on the battlefield, serving as the best medicine to mitigate threats and protect personnel. In the midst of combat, all individuals should prioritize returning fire and seeking cover. The sequence of initial actions must align with the tactical circumstances at hand. Given the limited time available amid effective enemy fire, providing casualty care becomes a secondary consideration.

3-73. Suppression of hostile fire and establishment of fire superiority are critical objectives aimed at reducing the risk of harm to both personnel and casualties while accomplishing the mission. In certain situations, individuals may be required to engage in returning fire rather than immediately attending to casualties, including wounded personnel who retain combat capability. Those injured and exposed to enemy fire should be instructed to continue engaging the enemy, seek cover promptly, and administer self-aid measures if feasible. These actions are essential for maintaining operational effectiveness and safeguarding lives amidst combat conditions.

### Casualty Self-aid

3-74. The skills outlined in this publication equip Service members to administer initial medical care at the POI, sustaining casualties until further assistance arrives. Responsive casualties should be directed by the first responder or CLS to return fire, apply self-aid, re-engage, and seek cover if feasible.

3-75. If cover is inaccessible or the wounded Service member cannot move to safety, they should lie flat and remain still. If self-aid is beyond the casualty's capability or cover is unattainable, implementing a rescue

plan, if feasible, becomes imperative. A hasty TQ should be applied promptly and tightly to stop the bleeding (ideally within one minute), as the tactical situation dictates. The urgency of a TQ application is underscored by the potential for a casualty to succumb to exsanguination in as little as three minutes.

3-76. Initial medical care within the military context is administered by Service members leveraging their trained basic skills, encompassing self-aid or buddy aid. These skills encompass immediate life-saving interventions, as well as treatment for non-combat related injuries or conditions affecting functional capacity.

3-77. The CoTCCC has identified five critical life-saving skills, namely rapid casualty assessment, TQ application, wound packing with hemostatic dressing, application of pressure bandages, and basic airway maneuvers for airway management.

3-78. Vital to sustaining life is maintaining an adequate blood volume to facilitate oxygen transport to tissues. Halting bleeding and ensuring open airways are pivotal first aid measures, as oxygen deprivation swiftly precipitates death.

3-79. Shock denotes inadequate tissue and organ perfusion, which is left untreated, can culminate in fatality despite the non-lethal nature of the precipitating injury or condition. Preventing shock is a cornerstone of first aid, significantly enhancing casualty survival prospects.

3-80. Effective wound management entails safeguarding injuries against infection, which can impede recovery and exacerbate morbidity. Understanding basic first aid principles include proficiency in wound dressing to avert infection and minimize contamination.

3-81. TCCC serves as the benchmark in prehospital battlefield medicine. Rapid casualty assessment and application of life-saving skills are pivotal in preserving lives. During casualty response, Service members are urged to take decisive action, document provided medical aid for continuity of care and facilitate evacuation to expedite access to advanced medical treatment.

### Initial Encounter

3-82. In many cases, the initial care a Service member receives is provided through self-aid, buddy aid, or by CLS. The effectiveness of the actions taken during the initial encounter with a casualty, along with proper movement and transportation, are crucial elements in the successful application of TCCC. Sound decisionmaking and proficient execution of TCCC tasks have a significant impact on the health outcomes of casualties.

3-83. Upon first encountering a casualty, it is imperative for the responder to assess the situation, understand the nature and severity of the injuries, and determine the appropriate TCCC interventions required quickly and accurately. Accurate assessment is essential not only for the welfare of the casualty but also for the safety of the responder. For example, in the case of electrocution, the responder must avoid direct contact with the casualty to prevent becoming a casualty themselves. This initial assessment forms the foundation for all subsequent care and decisions in the management of battlefield casualties.

## Tactical Combat Casualty Care Clinical Practice Guideline

3-84. The JTS is dedicated to establishing the standard of care for all aspects of battlefield medicine, including prehospital care. It operates as a Department of Defense Center of Excellence for Trauma and is responsible for providing the CPGs and performance improvement initiatives for all levels of military trauma care. Under JTS, the CoTCCC is tasked with developing the TCCC CPG.

3-85. The TCCC CPG serves as the benchmark for contemporary battlefield medical care. While the guidelines serve as recommendations, they do not replace clinical judgment. Given that the guidelines undergo periodic review and updates, it is essential for healthcare providers to regularly review them and integrate any changes into their training. For more detailed information on the TCCC CPG, reference the deployed medicine website.

3-86. The content provided in this publication is accurate as of the 23 March 2026; however, standards of care may evolve over time. Continuous learning and training are crucial for Service members. The Deployed Medicine website and its accompanying mobile application, both endorsed by the DOD's JTS, serve as

authoritative resources for TCCC medical training. This application allows users to stay informed about the TCCC guidelines and updates.

### Triage or Sorting of Casualties

3-87. _Triage_ is the process of sorting casualties based on need for treatment, evacuation, and available resources (FM 4-02). It is performed in each role of medical care. Triage is the most effective means of establishing order and the best method for providing the greatest benefit to the greatest number of patients within the limitations of time, distance, and capability. Triage is a constant process as casualties move within and through the roles of medical care.

3-88. During incidents that generate mass casualties, the need to withhold treatment from a severely injured casualty, in-order to redirect lifesaving resources to casualties whose wounds are more survivable, is a difficult decision to make. While such decisions have been infrequent in recent conflicts, they may become more common during large-scale combat operations or situations where evacuation is difficult. The overarching goal of triage is, aiming to provide the greatest good to the greatest number and should guide these difficult decisions. The mission, immediate tactical situation, followed by medical necessity should prioritize resource allocation and should not be influenced by a casualty’s nationality or combatant status.

3-89. Triage, or sorting of casualties, is essential to maximize limited resources. It involves evaluating, categorizing, and prioritizing casualties for medical treatment and evacuation. Casualties are sorted into categories based on their treatment urgency: immediate, delayed, minimal, and expectant. The goal is to return the greatest number of Service members back to duty while preserving life, limb, and eyesight.

#### Immediate Category

3-90. Immediate injuries are those that require attention within minutes to two hours to prevent death or major disability. Examples include airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, uncontrolled hemorrhage, torso, neck or pelvis injuries with shock, head injuries requiring emergent decompression, threatened limb loss, collection of blood behind the eye, and multiple extremity amputations.

#### Delayed

3-91. Delayed injuries are those requiring surgery but permitting delay without significant risk to life, limb, or eyesight. Examples include blunt or penetrating torso injuries without shock, fractures, soft tissue injuries, facial fractures without airway compromise, eye injuries, and survivable burns without immediate threat of life (airway, respiratory) or limb.

#### Minimal

3-92. Minimal injuries are those that can care for themselves or need minimal medical care. Examples include minor lacerations, abrasions, small bone fractures, and minor burns. These casualties may assist with the movement or care of more severely injured individuals.

#### Expectant

3-93. Expectant injuries are those casualties that have injuries that would overwhelm current medical resources, at the expense of treating salvageable patients. These casualties should receive comfort measures and intermittent reassessment. Examples include casualties without vital signs or signs of life, open pelvic injuries with controlled bleeding and shock, life-threatening burns, high cervical spinal cord injuries affecting respiration, and destructive cardiac injuries.

### Casualty Assessment

3-94. When learning, training, or teaching emergency medical care for a medical condition the airway, breathing, and circulation is used to prioritize treatment and may still be applicable in some situations. Service members experiencing a trauma casualty often start with more problems than resources available. In these situations, casualty assessment and management in TCCC follows a deliberate framework to prioritize trauma care known as massive hemorrhaging, airway, respiration, circulation, and hypothermia (MARCH) - pain, wounds, antibiotics, and splinting (PAWS).

3-95. The implementation of TCCC in modern warfare reflects the military's commitment to enhancing the well-being and survival of personnel on the battlefield. TCCC provides a comprehensive approach to immediate medical intervention in combat environments, with a focus on the "MARCH" portion of the MARCH-PAWS treatment protocol. This protocol prioritizes the evaluation and control of:

- Massive Bleeding or hemorrhaging (the top priority) (see Chapter 4).

- Airway (see Chapter 5).

- Respiration (breathing) (see Chapter 6).

- Circulation (see Chapter 7).

- Hypothermia and head injuries (see Chapter 8).

3-96. The second portion of the MARCH-PAWS casualty assessment is PAWS, which is addressed after the MARCH portion. For instance, while treating a casualty, a first responder or CLS might identify a fractured arm during the circulation and second injury portion of the MARCH. However, splinting the fracture should occur during the PAWS portion of the assessment.

3-97. The primary focus of the casualty assessment is to swiftly identify and address life-threatening injuries before treating secondary injuries. For example, if a first responder or CLS were to treat a simple wound or splint a fracture before completing the full MARCH assessment, they might overlook life-threatening bleeding to a lower extremity. Although the simple wound or fracture would be addressed, the casualty would still be at risk of death due to the untreated life-threatening bleed. After addressing life-threatening injuries, the PAWS portion of the framework should be completed.

3-98. Following the MARCH-PAWS sequence guides Service members through the rapid casualty assessment and CLS through the trauma assessment. This involves either a rapid casualty assessment or a full tactical trauma assessment; both aimed at identifying and treating all life-threatening conditions and serious wounds. Rapid and accurate evaluation of casualties is crucial for providing effective first aid.

#### Rapid Casualty Assessment

3-99. When a casualty has transitioned from CUF into TFC, the Service member should treat all immediate and potential life threats discovered during the rapid casualty assessment. The rapid casualty assessment, performed by all Service members, is very similar to the tactical trauma assessment, which is performed by the CLS. Once the Service member has treated all immediate and potential life threats, the PAWS portion of the rapid casualty assessment should be performed, and all other injuries treated. These may include head injuries, eye trauma, fractures, and burns, among others.

#### Tactical Trauma Assessment

3-100. The CLS utilizes the tactical trauma assessment (a more in-depth assessment compared to the rapid casualty assessment) to reassess any hemorrhage control measures (hasty TQs) initiated in the CUF phase by first responders and identify any injuries that have not yet been treated. The tactical trauma assessment follows the MARCH-PAWS sequence, allowing the CLS to systematically evaluate and address any remaining life-threatening injuries and secondary injuries.

### Medical Equipment and Documentation for Tactical Combat Casualty Care

3-101. Service members carry a large array of equipment, and survival on the battlefield depends heavily on the ability to properly use that equipment. One piece of equipment that is critically important is the joint first aid kit (JFAK), which is a standardized medical assembling.

**_Note._** The Army uses the individual first aid kit (IFAK) instead of the JFAK.

#### Medical Equipment

3-102. The JFAK contains both medical and documentation materials. Essential medical supplies for TCCC are typically found in the Service member's first aid kit or Service-specific first aid kit. It's vital for all Service members to be familiar with the contents and location of these kits, as well as any maintenance procedures for trauma supplies required by their unit. See Appendix A for further discussion on first aid kits.

3-103. CoTCCC-identified equipment required to treat injuries and conditions to execute specific aspects of the MARCH-PAWS sequence and can be found in the following chapters—

- Chapter 4 Massive Bleeding.

- Chapter 5 Airway Control.

- Chapter 6 Respiration and Breathing control.

- Chapter 7 Circulation Control.

- Chapter 8 Hypothermia Prevention.

- Chapter 9 Secondary Assessment.

#### Medical Documentation

3-104. The JFAK includes documentation material, primarily the DD Form 1380. This form is the standard for documenting all assessment and treatment provided to casualties before evacuation or handoff to medical personnel.

3-105. Every piece of equipment in the JFAK is evidence-based, drawing from lessons learned in recent overseas contingency operations. The JFAK's contents support the provision of TCCC per current guidelines and enable first responders or CLS to address issues identified in various phases of casualty care. Each Service may have additional items in a Service-specific first aid kit. See Appendix A for Service-specific equipment.

#### Combat Lifesaver Aid Bag

3-106. The CLS carries a compact aid bag containing supplies for controlling bleeding, relieving tension pneumothorax, and performing other essential procedures. For a current list of supplies in the aid bag, refer to the medical materiel information portal website. CLS bags should be issued for training events and operational deployments.

3-107. It's the responsibility of each CLS to:

- Ensure their aid bag is stocked per the prescribed packing list.

- Verifying all stocked items are serviceable and not expired.

- Allocate aid bag supplies as needed.

**_Note._** All supplies and materials required for CLS training should be obtained through normal supply channels.

3-108. The CLS should prioritize using the casualty’s first aid kit before using supplies from the CLS bag if the casualty's kit is depleted.

3-109. Medical units should maintain stocks of medical materiel for resupplying CLS aid bags during operations. Resupply procedures during deployment mimic those during home station operations, involving bag inventories, supply requests, and emergency resupply from nearby medical units capable of helping.

### Maintenance and Resupply

3-110. Conducting precombat inspections or readiness checks is paramount for every piece of equipment carried or used by Service members. The readiness and serviceability of contents in first aid kits, CLS bags, or unit-specific kits can be a matter of life or death. Regular inspections before, during, and after training events and combat missions are crucial. Unit leaders should anticipate Class VIII resupply kits based on casualty estimates, and routine inventories must be conducted to ensure all required material is present.

#### Seals and wrappers

3-111. Replace items with broken or unsealed wrappers. If an item was vacuum sealed upon issuance but is no longer sealed during inspection, it should be replaced.

#### Check expiration dates

3-112. Medications and many medical-grade materials, such as hemostatic dressings, have expiration dates and lot numbers. Check all medications and medical-grade items for expiration dates and replace any expired items or those whose expiration dates do not extend beyond the expected deployment period. While items like TQs typically do not have expiration dates, ensure they are CoTCCC-approved, serviceable, reflect the current generation, and have not been replaced with newer models.

#### Unapproved or Fake Equipment

3-113. Only a few items in the first aid kit require specific recommendations from the CoTCCC. Ensure that kits are stocked only with CoTCCC-recommended or approved items and be wary of unapproved equipment. Beware of fraudulent manufacturers producing fake, misleading, or substandard medical equipment, especially TQs and hemostatic dressings.

### Caution

Avoid deploying with equipment that is missing, expired, or previously used in training.

#### Unit-Specific Evacuation Equipment

3-114. Unit-specific evacuation equipment may include ropes, harnesses, packing materials for mountain or extreme cold conditions, life jackets, or flotation devices. It's essential to incorporate precombat inspections of these unit-specific items into the unit's SOPs. After ensuring all specialized gear is accounted for, proceed to inspect litters for proper functioning and serviceability. Additionally, check the litter straps to ensure their locking functions and placements are correct. Inspect special evacuation equipment according to the manufacturers' instructions or unit guidelines and standards.
