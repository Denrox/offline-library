# Chapter 9: Secondary Injury Assessment Using Pain, Antibiotics, Wounds, Splinting and Mechanism of Injury, Injury Type, Signs and Symptoms, and Treatments

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Pain, Antibiotics, Wounds, and Splinting and Mechanism of Injury, Injury Type, Signs and Symptoms, and Treatments

9-1. As discussed in paragraph 3-96 on page 39, PAWS is the second part of the MARCH-PAWS casualty assessment and is conducted after the MARCH portion. The MIST reporting was introduced as a standard component of the MEDEVAC request during Operation Enduring Freedom in Afghanistan.

9-2. While not officially part of the NATO and U.S. standard MEDEVAC request, MIST reporting has become customary in some operations. Often referred to as the 10th line in the 9-line MEDEVAC request, the completed DD Form 1380 serves as the MIST report. However, the high volume of casualties expected during large scale combat operations may make it challenging to complete MIST reporting for each casualty.

9-3. The MIST report facilitates the transfer of medical information to the receiving health care provider by providing a structured approach to the casualty handoff. A MIST report delivers additional evacuation information that might be required by theater commanders and assists the receiving MTF in preparing for specific inbound casualties. However, transmitting a MIST report for each casualty during operations and mass casualty incidents may not always be timely or feasible.

### Management of Pain and Antibiotics

9-4. The combat wound medication pack (CWMP) is both the “P” (pain) and the “A” (antibiotics) in the MARCH-PAWS sequence. Pain is common with battlefield injuries. Some injuries and levels of pain can be treated safely by using pain medications (also known as analgesics) in the CWMP. These include fractures, burns, and eye injuries.

9-5. The CWMP is a prepackaged medication packet with drugs and dosages specifically chosen for use in combat casualty care. The CWMP contains drugs for mild to moderate pain (Meloxicam and Acetaminophen) and an antibiotic specific for penetrating wounds (Moxifloxacin). The CWMP should be used only for combat related injuries and is not for routine use. It may be procured through medical logistics systems as a prepackaged blister pack or, may be locally packaged by medical personnel.

9-6. For pain relief on the battlefield of mild to moderate pain that will not keep the casualty out of the fight, ensure they take their CWMP. The casualty should take all three medications in the CWMP. This can give significant pain relief and will not alter the casualty’s mental status. This is a good option when the casualty’s pain and wounds are not severe enough to keep them out of the fight.

**_Note._** If the casualty has wounds or pain severe enough to render them unable to fight, then medical personnel have other options for more effective pain relief. The medications given by medical personnel will generally require that the casualty be disarmed because the medications can alter the casualty’s mental status.

9-7. CWMP is a prepackaged pill pack containing CoTCCC-recommended medications for use in combat casualty care. The CWMP is found in the casualty’s first aid kit. It contains medications for pain and antibiotics that can be taken by mouth.

9-8. The medication in the CWMP should be taken as soon as possible after life-threatening conditions have been addressed. All medications administered should be documented along with the time given on the DD Form 1380.

#### 9-9. The CWMP contains the following components:

- Two 500mg caplets of acetaminophen (total 1,000mg) in extended-release form.

- One 400mg tablet of moxifloxacin.

- One 15mg tablet of meloxicam.

9-10. Each of the three medications in these dosages is contained in a blister pack.

**_Note._** The popular nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin interfere with platelet function and blood clotting and can significantly increase the risk of bleeding in combat casualties.

9-11. Acetaminophen and meloxicam do not alter platelet function and are safe and effective for use in combat casualties. Meloxicam does not alter platelet function and is the preferred Non-Steroidal AntiInflammatory Drug for personnel who may see combat in the next 7-10 days.

9-12. The CWMP should be given when the casualty:

- Is conscious and able to swallow.

- It has mild to moderate pain.

- Is still able to fight if needed.

- Has any penetrating wounds or break of the skin.

9-13. The CWMP should NOT be given if the casualty:

- Is unable to swallow or take oral meds, such as when the casualty is unconscious, or has severe facial trauma, or burns.

- Has known allergies to the CWMP medications.

- If the casualty is unconscious, refer them to medical personnel as soon as possible.

**_Note._** If the casualty has a break in the skin in a traumatic injury, they should take the CWMP. Otherwise, consult with medical personnel before giving it.

9-14. Battlefield wounds are often dirty and susceptible to infection. Early administration of antibiotics from the CWMP may reduce the chance of later infections. Wound infections can kill the casualty or delay their recovery. The CWMP should be given as soon as possible for wounds after life-threatening issues have been addressed.

### Management of Wounds

9-15. Beyond MARCH, there are additional or secondary injuries that should be identified and may need treatment. The secondary injuries identified in TCCC are organized by eye trauma, head injury, burns, and fractures. These secondary injuries are treated in TFC.

9-16. Wound management is the “W” in the MARCH-PAWS sequence. Once the casualty's hemorrhage has been controlled, earlier control interventions are completed, and wound management must be rechecked. All wounds must be frequently reassessed to ensure continued hemorrhage control.

9-17. The first responder or CLS should confirm all wounds have been addressed and reassess for re-bleeding under gauze or bandages to ensure bleeding has been controlled. The first responder or CLS must look for blood flowing around or under TQs, bandages, and dressings. If the bleeding has not been controlled, tighten the TQ or pressure bandage for that wound if possible, and redress any wounds as necessary. Also, check other wounds to make sure that:

- Eye injuries are shielded.

- Open chest wounds are accounted for.

- All wounds are wrapped.

- Fractures are splinted.

9-18. Wounds should always be monitored and frequently reassessed to ensure hemorrhage has been controlled. Any wounds that continue to bleed must be packed with new hemostatic dressing. Once the dressing has been applied with pressure for three minutes, carefully observe for blood continuing to flow

from under the gauze to determine if bleeding has been controlled. When the bleeding has stopped, a new pressure bandage should be applied over the hemostatic dressing.

9-19. Any previously untreated wounds should be dressed by applying (or packing) gauze with direct pressure. Non-life-threatening bleeding usually does not need hemostatic dressing. If dressings or gauze are not available, use clean, dry cloth such as torn clothing, or a cravat if available.

9-20. Minor wounds include minor lacerations and abrasions, such as road rashes. Other wounds that may need to be dressed include major wounds that are no longer bleeding, such as:

- Amputation stumps.

- Gunshot wounds that required a TQ.

- Major lacerations.

- Shrapnel wounds, possibly with shrapnel still in place.

- Impaled objects.

9-21. Applied pressure bandages must be continuously reassessed for:

- Increased pain.

- Skin color.

- Loss of the pulse.

9-22. If any of the above develops, it might indicate an emergency. Ensure that the applied bandage isn't too tight and loosen it as needed while keeping the bleeding controlled.

**_Note._** Pulses are reassessed after dressings are placed to ensure that they are not too tight. Do not ever apply a dressing and forget it!
