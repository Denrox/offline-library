# Chapter 16: Bites and Stings

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Bites and Stings

16-1. Bites and stings are common occurrences that can happen in almost every environment. From pesky mosquito bites to venomous snakebites, these encounters with creatures big and small can lead to a range of discomfort, pain, and even potential danger. Understanding the various types of bites and stings, their causes, and proper treatment is crucial for minimizing the associated risks and ensuring a swift recovery.

16-2. Poison and venom are terms often used interchangeably, but they have distinct differences in their mode of delivery and the way they affect living organisms. A poison is a substance that is harmful or lethal when ingested, inhaled, or absorbed through the skin. It is typically produced by plants, animals, or microorganisms as a defensive mechanism. Poisonous substances can be found in various forms, such as toxins in certain plants, chemicals in household products, or even certain medications when taken in excessive amounts. The key characteristic of poison is that it needs to be ingested or absorbed into the body to cause harm.

16-3. Venom is a specialized secretion that is actively injected into another organism. It is typically produced by certain animals, such as snakes, spiders, scorpions, and some insects. Venom is delivered through specialized organs, such as fangs, stingers, or spines, and is primarily used for hunting or self-defense. Unlike poison, venom is actively injected into the victim's bloodstream, tissues, or body cavities, often leading to localized or systemic effects, which can range from pain and swelling to paralysis or even death. The main distinction between poison and venom lies in their delivery method. Poison is harmful when ingested or absorbed, while venom is specifically injected into another organism through specialized organs.

16-4. If bites and stings are not treated promptly and correctly, they may cause serious illness or death. The severity of a snakebite depends upon whether the snake is venomous or nonvenomous, the type of snake, the location of the bite, and the amount of venom injected. Bites from humans and other animals, such as dogs, cats, bats, raccoons, and rats, can cause severe bruises and infection and tears or lacerations of tissue. Awareness of the potential sources of injuries can reduce or prevent them from occurring. Knowledge and prompt application of first aid measures can lessen the severity of injuries from bites and stings and keep the Service member from becoming a serious casualty.

## Human or Animal Bites

16-5. First aid for human or animal bites aims to prevent infection and provide initial care for the wound. To perform first aid for a human or animal bite:

- Remove yourself from further harm. If dealing with an animal bite, ensure that you are no longer at risk of being bitten again.

- Control bleeding. If the bite wound is bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or sterile gauze to stop or minimize the bleeding. Elevating the wound, if possible, can also help reduce bleeding.

- Clean the wound. Rinse the bite wound thoroughly with clean water or saline solution to flush out any dirt, bacteria, or saliva that may have entered the wound. Gently remove any debris from the wound using mild soap and water.

- Apply an antiseptic. After cleaning, apply an antiseptic solution or ointment to the wound to help reduce the risk of infection. Follow the instructions on the product label and ensure it is suitable for use on open wounds.

- Cover the wound. Use a sterile dressing or clean bandage to cover the wound. This will protect it from further contamination and promote healing. Change dressing regularly to maintain cleanliness.

- • Seek medical attention. It is important to seek medical evaluation for all animal bites, especially those from unfamiliar or wild animals, deep wounds, or those that become red, swollen, or increasingly painful. A health care professional can assess the severity of the bite, prescribe antibiotics if necessary, and administer tetanus prophylaxis if required.

16-6. If someone gets bitten by an animal, whether known, unknown, wild, or domestic, the primary concern is rabies. Unless the animal is someone's pet and verified to be up to date on rabies, the individual who has been bitten must get the rabies post-exposure vaccine. Seemingly minor bites can lead to infection, so it is essential to consult a health care professional to determine the appropriate course of action and receive any necessary medical treatment.

**_Note._** If unable to capture or kill the animal, provide medical personnel with any information that will help identify it.

### Marine (sea) Animals

16-7. Except for sharks and barracuda, most marine animals will not deliberately attack. The most frequent injuries from marine animals are wounds by biting, stinging, or puncturing. Wounds inflicted by marine animals can be very painful but are rarely fatal.

16-8. Wounds from sharks, barracuda, and crocodilian can involve major trauma because of bites and lacerations. Bites from large marine animals are potentially the most life-threatening of all injuries from marine animals. Major wounds from these animals can be treated by controlling the bleeding, preventing shock, giving basic life support, splinting the injury, and by securing prompt medical aid.

16-9. Turtles, moray eels, and corals normally inflict minor wounds. Treat by cleansing the wound(s) thoroughly and by splinting if necessary.

16-10. Jellyfish, anemones, and others marine animals inflict injury by means of stinging cells in their tentacles. Contact with the tentacles produces burning pain with a rash and small hemorrhages on the skin. Shock, muscular cramping, nausea, vomiting, and respiratory distress may also occur. Gently remove the clinging tentacles with a towel and wash or treat the area. Use diluted ammonia or alcohol, a meat tenderizer, and talcum powder. If symptoms become severe or persist, seek medical assistance.

16-11. Spiny fish, urchins, stingrays, and cone shells inject their venom by puncturing the skin with their spines. General signs and symptoms include swelling, nausea, vomiting, generalized cramps, diarrhea, muscular paralysis, and shock. Deaths are rare. Treatment consists of soaking the wound(s) in hot water (when available) for 30 to 60 minutes. This inactivates the heat sensitive toxin. In addition, further first aid measures should be taken such as controlling bleeding or applying a dressing. This should be carried out as necessary.

### Caution

Be careful not to scald the casualty with water that is too hot because the pain of the wound will mask the normal reaction to heat.

### Snake Bites

16-12. Snakebites can occur unexpectedly during outdoor activities or encounters with snakes in their natural habitats. Understanding how to identify snakes, knowing the proper steps to take after a snakebite, and having a grasp of basic first aid techniques can significantly increase the chances of a positive outcome in such situations. Snakes are categorized between nonvenomous and venomous snakes.

16-13. Snakes can be categorized into various groups based on different characteristics. One common classification system categorizes snakes into four main categories of colubrid snakes, constrictor snakes, nonvenomous snakes, and venomous snakes. These categories may overlap, and some snakes may possess traits from multiple categories. Understanding the different categories of snakes can aid in snake identification, assessing potential risks, and knowing how to respond appropriately in various situations.

#### Colubrid Snakes

16-14. Colubrid snakes represent the largest snake family and include a wide range of non-venomous species. These snakes have diverse habitats, sizes, and feeding habits. Common colubrid snakes include rat, king, gopher, and water snakes.

#### Constrictor Snakes

16-15. Constrictor snakes are non-venomous snakes that subdue their prey by coiling around them and squeezing until they can no longer breathe. Constrictors then swallow their prey whole. Well-known constrictor snakes include boas, pythons, and anacondas.

#### Non-venomous Snakes

16-16. Non-venomous snakes include snakes that lack venom glands or fangs and pose no direct threat to humans. Non-venomous snakes play an essential role in controlling rodent populations and maintaining ecological balance. Examples of non-venomous snakes are boas, pythons, corn, and garter snakes.

16-17. There are approximately 130 different varieties of nonvenomous snakes in the U.S. They have ovalshaped heads and round eyes. Unlike venomous snakes, discussed below, nonvenomous snakes do not have fangs with which to inject venom. Figure 16-1 depicts the characteristics of a nonvenomous snake.

#### Venomous Snakes

16-18. Venomous snakes possess venom glands and specialized fangs to inject venom into their prey or as a defense mechanism. It is important to exercise caution and seek immediate medical attention in the event of a venomous snakebite.

16-19. It is important to note that the distribution of venomous snakes may vary within states, and some species may be specific to certain regions. When spending time in areas known to have venomous snakes, it is crucial to be aware of the local species, understand their habitats and behavior, and take appropriate precautions to avoid encounters and bites.

16-20. Venomous snakes in other parts of the world include sea snakes, the Fer-De-Lance, the Bushmaster, tropical rattlesnake in tropical Central America; the Malayan pit viper in the tropical Far East; the cobra in Africa and Asia; the mamba (or black mamba) in central and southern Africa; and the krait in India and Southeast Asia.

16-24. In the U.S, there are several species of venomous snakes. The following are some of the venomous snakes commonly found in different regions of the country:

- Rattlesnakes: Rattlesnakes are a group of venomous snakes known for the rattling sound they produce by shaking their tails. Species such as the eastern and western diamondback, timber and Mojave rattlesnakes are found in various parts of the U.S.

- Copperheads: Copperheads are venomous snakes with distinctive copper-colored heads. They are commonly found in the eastern and southeastern regions of the U.S.

- Cottonmouths or Water Moccasins: Cottonmouths or Water Moccasins are venomous semi-aquatic snakes primarily found in the southeastern U.S. They are known for their aggressive behavior and are often encountered near bodies of water.

- Coral Snakes: Coral snakes are venomous snakes with colorful bands of red, yellow, and black. The Eastern and Texas Coral Snakes are found in specific regions of the southern U.S.

- Various Pit Vipers: Pit vipers include venomous snakes that possess heat-sensing pits between their eyes and nostrils. Along with rattlesnakes and copperheads, this category includes species such as the eastern and western Massasauga Rattlesnakes, and the Mojave Desert Sidewinder.

16-25. The casualty’s condition provides the best information about the seriousness of the situation and how much time has passed since the bite occurred. Pit viper bites are characterized by severe burning pain. Discoloration and swelling around the fang marks usually begins within five to ten minutes after the bite. If only minimal swelling occurs within 30 minutes, the bite will almost certainly have been from a nonvenomous snake or possibly from a venomous snake, which did not inject venom. The venom destroys blood cells, causing a general discoloration of the skin. Blisters and numbness in the affected area follow this reaction. Other signs, which can occur, are weakness, rapid pulse, nausea, shortness of breath, vomiting, and shock.

16-26. The venom of viper bites affects the circulatory system and destroys muscle. Severe pain, swelling and change in skin color usually develop within five to 10 minutes and slowly spreads. After several hours, the following signs and symptoms will occur:

- Further swelling and changes in skin color.

- Bleeding blisters.

- Numbness of the affected area.

16-27. Other signs and symptoms which may occur include:

- Weaknesses.

- Rapid pulse.

- Nausea.

- Vomiting.

- Shortness of breath.

- Sweating.

- Shock.

16-28. Elapids are a family of venomous snakes known for their fixed front fangs and potent neurotoxic venom. They generally have slender bodies and smooth scales. Famous elapid snakes include cobras, coral snakes, kraits, and mambas. Corals (figure 16-4), cobras (figure 16-5), kraits, and mambas all belong to the same group even though they are found in different parts of the world. All four inject their venom through short, grooved fangs, leaving a characteristic bite pattern.

16-29. To identify a coral snake, a common phrase used in the U.S. is “Red or yellow, kill a fellow; red on black, venom lack.” This phrase refers to the color pattern on the snake's bands and serves as a mnemonic device to help distinguish coral snakes from non-venomous snakes with similar markings, such as milk or scarlet kingsnakes. Coral snakes have red bands touching yellow bands, while non-venomous snakes have red bands touching black bands. It's important to remember that this phrase is specific to the U.S. and may not apply to coral snake species found in other regions. When encountering any snake, it is advisable to maintain a safe distance and seek professional assistance if unsure of its venomous nature.

16-30. The venom from corals, cobras, kraits, and mambas, which belong to the elapid family, notably affects the central nervous system, producing a distinct set of symptoms that diverge from those caused by pit vipers. Initially, these bites may result in only minimal pain and swelling, leading many individuals to underestimate the severity of the bite. Typically, the onset of neurological symptoms occurs within one to seven hours postbite, including blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, drowsiness, and an increase in salivation and sweating.

16-31. If the bite is not addressed quickly with appropriate medical intervention, more severe symptoms can manifest, such as nausea, vomiting, respiratory difficulties, shock, and eventually more critical conditions like paralysis, convulsions, and coma. This progression underscores the necessity of recognizing the potential danger of elapid bites early and seeking immediate medical treatment to mitigate these serious outcomes.

16-32. While most rear-fanged snakes are non-venomous, some species within this family possess venomous rear fangs. These fangs are located toward the back of the mouth and are used to subdue their prey. Rearfanged venomous snakes include boomslangs, mangrove, and vine snakes.

16-33. Burrowing asps are a family of venomous snakes primarily found in Africa and parts of the Middle East. They have small, fixed front fangs and are adapted for burrowing in sandy or loose soil. Examples include the stiletto snakes and mole vipers.

16-34. Sea snakes are a group of venomous snakes that have adapted to life in aquatic environments. They have paddle-shaped tails and can be found in the coastal waters of the Indian and Pacific Oceans. Sea snakes have highly potent venom but are generally not aggressive towards humans.

16-35. Sea snakes (figure 16-6 on page 165) are found in the warm water areas of the Pacific and Indian Oceans, along the coasts, and at the mouths of some larger rivers. Their venom is VERY venomous, but their fangs are only ¼ inch long.

##### _Venomous Snakebite_

16-36. Venomous snakes DO NOT always inject venom when they bite or strike a person. However, all snakes may carry tetanus (lockjaw); anyone bitten by a snake, whether venomous or nonvenomous, should immediately seek medical attention.

16-37. Venom is injected from the venom sacs through grooved or hollowed fangs. Depending on the species, these fangs are either long or short. Pit vipers have long hollow fangs. These fangs are folded against the roof of the mouth and extend when the snake strikes. This allows them to strike quickly and then withdraw. Cobras, coral snakes, kraits, mambas, and sea snakes have short, grooved fangs. These snakes are less effective in their attempts to bite, since they must chew after striking to inject enough venom (poison) to be effective.

16-38. In the event you are bitten, do not attempt to identify and or kill the snake. Freshly killed snakes can continue to bite due to reflexive action. Snake identification is not critical. Medical personnel will treat symptoms by geography and not someone’s best guess at a species.

16-39. The venoms of different snakes cause different effects. Pit viper venom (hemotoxin [blood toxin]) destroys tissue and blood cells. Cobras, adders, and coral snakes inject powerful venom (neurotoxin [nerve toxin]) which affects the central nervous system, causing respiratory paralysis. Water moccasins and sea snakes have venom that is both hemotoxic and neurotoxic.

### First Aid for a Snakebite

16-40. In the event of a snakebite, prompt first aid is crucial to minimizing the potential effects of the venom. To perform first aid for a snakebite:

- Stay calm and call for medical assistance immediately. Provide medical personnel with information about the snake, if possible, but do not attempt to capture or kill the snake as it may increase the risk of further bites.

- Keep the bitten Service member as still and calm as possible. Restrict movement and avoid unnecessary exertion, as it can accelerate the spread of venom in the bloodstream.

- Remove any constricting items, such as jewelry or tight clothing, from the affected area, as swelling may occur.

- Position the affected limb slightly below the level of the heart, if possible. This can help slow the spread of venom.

- Cleanse the wound gently with mild soap and water to reduce the risk of infection. Do not apply ice or use a TQ, as they are not effective and can cause additional harm.

- Apply a sterile bandage or dress over the bite, ensuring it is snug but not too tight. This can help immobilize the area and reduce the spread of venom.

- Encourage the person to remain calm and still, while waiting for medical help to arrive. Monitor their vital signs, such as breathing and heart rate, and be prepared to administer CPR if necessary.

16-41. The above steps are general guidelines and may vary depending on the snake species and the region. It is crucial to seek medical attention immediately after a snakebite, as they can provide appropriate antivenom treatment and further evaluate the situation.

### Caution

DO NOT attempt to cut open the bite or suck out the venom. If the venom should seep through any damaged or lacerated tissues in your mouth, you could immediately lose consciousness or even die.

16-42. If accompanying the casualty on CASEVAC to an MTF, observe the casualty carefully for signs and symptoms of an allergic reaction. Continue to monitor the airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs (for first aid purposes; pulse, and respiration), and distal pulse of the bitten extremity, treating for shock, and conserving body heat.

## Insect (arthropod) Bites and Stings

16-43. An insect bite or sting can cause great pain, allergic reaction, inflammation, and infection. If not treated correctly, some bites or stings may cause serious illness or even death. When an allergic reaction is not involved, first aid is a simple process. In any case, medical personnel should examine the casualty at the earliest possible time. It is important to identify spiders, bees, or creatures that cause bites or sting, especially in cases of an allergic reaction.

16-44. The insects found throughout the world that can produce a bite or sting are too numerous to mention in detail. Commonly encountered stinging or biting insects include the brown recluse spiders, black widow spiders, tarantulas, scorpions, urticating caterpillars, bees, wasps, centipedes, conenose beetles (kissing bugs), ants, and wheel bugs. Upon being reassigned, especially to overseas areas, take the time to become acquainted with the types of insects to avoid.

16-45. Arthropods are segmented invertebrates with jointed antennae and legs. They include insects, arachnids, centipedes, and millipedes. See figure 16-7 for examples of venomous arthropods.

16-46. Of all deaths due to venomous stings or bites in a year, most are caused by arthropods. Most injuries resulting from venomous arthropods can be prevented by doing the following:

- If you are allergic to certain stings or bites, be prepared with the proper medication.

- Ask the local inhabitants about the local venomous and harmful arthropods.

- Avoid touching unfamiliar arthropods or allowing them to touch you.

- Watch where you put your hands and feet.

- Before using it, check places where harmful arthropods may hide.

   - Closets, wall spaces, doorways, and eaves.

   - Outdoor privies and latrine boxes.

   - Bed clothing, beds, and sleeping bags.

   - Shoes, boots, and clothing.

   - Logs, felled trees, and leaves.

   - Rocks, boards, and debris.

- Avoid beehives, ant and wasp nests, and spider webs.

- Wear foot protection (shoes, boots, and sandals) always.

- Hang up clothes.

- Practice good sanitation by removing debris, trash, and board piles.

- Avoid wearing deodorants, colognes, perfumes, or cosmetics with fragrances.

### Insects

16-47. An insect is a small invertebrate animal characterized by having a segmented body, three pairs of legs, and often one or two pairs of wings. Insects are a diverse group, with over a million known species, and they play important roles in ecosystems as pollinators, decomposers, and prey for other organisms.

16-48. First aid treatment for insect bites can vary depending on the type of insect and the individual's reaction. The general first aid steps for treating insect bites are:

- Remove the stinger (if applicable): In the case of stinging insects like bees or wasps, it's important to remove the stinger as quickly as possible to prevent further venom injection. Gently scrape it off the skin using a blunt edge like a credit card or your fingernail. Avoid squeezing the venom sac, as it may release more venom.

- Wash the bite area: Clean the bite site with mild soap and water to reduce the risk of infection. Be gentle and avoid excessive scrubbing.

- Apply a cold compress: To alleviate pain, reduce swelling, and minimize itching, apply a cold compress or an ice pack wrapped in a cloth to the bite area. Do this for 10-15 minutes at a time, several times a day.

- Use over-the-counter remedies: Over-the-counter topical creams, ointments, or sprays containing ingredients such as hydrocortisone, calamine lotion, or antihistamines can help relieve itching and inflammation. Follow the instructions and precautions on the product packaging.

- Take oral antihistamines: If itching or swelling persists, you can take over-the-counter oral antihistamine to help reduce allergic reactions and relieve symptoms. Consult a pharmacist or health care professional for appropriate dosage and recommendations.

- Avoid scratching: Although it may be tempting, refrain from scratching the insect bite to prevent further irritation, infection, or the development of a secondary skin condition.

- Monitor for severe reactions: In rare cases, insect bites can cause severe allergic reactions, known as anaphylaxis. If you experience symptoms such as difficulty breathing, dizziness, swelling of the face or throat, or a widespread rash, seek immediate medical attention.

16-49. If the insect type is unknown or if the Service member is experiencing severe or worsening symptoms, it is best to consult a health care professional for proper evaluation and guidance.

### Arachnids

16-50. An arachnid is a class of joint-legged invertebrate animals that include spiders, scorpions, ticks, and mites. Arachnids are part of the phylum Arthropoda, which also includes insects, crustaceans, and millipedes.

16-51. Arachnids are characterized by having two main body segments: the cephalothorax (a fused head and thorax region) and the abdomen. They typically have four pairs of legs attached to the cephalothorax and possess specialized mouthparts called chelicerae, which are used for feeding and defense. Most arachnids also have specialized structures like spinnerets for producing silk (used by spiders for web-building) and a pair of pedipalps that may serve various functions depending on the species.

16-52. While spiders are perhaps the most well-known arachnids, other arachnids exhibit unique features. Scorpions have large pincers and a long tail ending in a venomous stinger. Ticks and mites are tiny arachnids that often parasitize animals, including humans, and can transmit diseases. Some arachnids, such as daddy longlegs and sun spiders, have distinct body shapes and behaviors.

16-53. Arachnids play diverse roles in ecosystems. Spiders, for example, are predators of insects and help control their populations. Arachnids have adapted to various habitats worldwide, from deserts to forests to aquatic environments. Arachnids are a diverse group of invertebrates that exhibit a wide range of adaptations, behaviors, and ecological roles within the animal kingdom.

### Spiders

16-54. Among the many species of spiders found around the world, a few are known for their potentially dangerous bites. The most well-known dangerous spider species are the:

- Brazilian Wandering Spider.

- Sydney Funnel-web Spider.

- Black Widow Spider.

- Brown Recluse Spider.

16-55. The Brazilian Wandering Spider is found in South and Central America and is known for its highly venomous bite. It is considered one of the most venomous spiders in the world. Their venom can cause severe pain, priapism (prolonged erection), and, in rare cases, can be life-threatening.

16-56. The Sydney Funnel-web Spider is found in Australia, the Sydney Funnel-web Spider is highly venomous and can be aggressive when threatened. Their venom is potent and can cause severe symptoms, including muscle spasm, difficulty breathing, and in rare cases, it can be fatal. Prompt medical attention is crucial if bitten.

16-57. The Black Widow Spider is found in various parts of the world. Females have a distinctive black body with a red hourglass-shaped mark on the abdomen. Their venom contains neurotoxins that can cause intense pain, muscle cramps, nausea, and in severe cases, can affect the nervous system. While bites are rarely fatal, medical attention is still recommended.

16-58. The Brown Recluse Spider is found primarily in the U.S. Their bites can cause tissue necrosis (cell death), leading to an ulcerous wound that may require medical attention. Other symptoms can include pain, itching, and systemic effects.

16-59. While these spiders are considered dangerous, actual bites and severe reactions are relatively rare. Most spiders are not aggressive and will only bite in self-defense. If bitten by a spider and experiencing severe symptoms, seek immediate medical attention.

16-60. Specific risks associated with spider bites can vary depending on a Service member’s health, sensitivity, and potential allergies. Therefore, it is always advisable to exercise caution around spiders and seek professional medical advice if a bite occurs.

- North Africa.

- Middle East.

- Mexico.

- Brazil.

- India.

16-62. Countries in North Africa, such as Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Egypt, are home to highly venomous scorpion species. These species include the Deathstalker scorpion and the Androc tonus australis.

16-64. Mexico is known for its diverse scorpion fauna, and it is home to several highly venomous species. The Bark scorpion is found in various parts of the country, including the notorious Arizona Bark scorpion, which is also found in the southwestern U.S.

16-65. In Brazil, the Brazilian Yellow scorpion is considered one of the most venomous scorpions. It is primarily found in southeastern and southern regions of the country.

16-66. India is home to several dangerous scorpion species. The Indian Red scorpion and the Indian Black scorpion are found to live in this region.

16-67. While these regions have known populations of dangerous scorpions, encounters with venomous scorpions and severe envenomation are relatively rare. ### Centipedes

16-68. A centipede is a type of arthropod characterized by elongated, multi-segmented creatures with numerous pairs of legs, ranging from tens to hundreds depending on the species. Centipedes are found in various habitats worldwide, including forests, deserts, and tropical regions.

16-69. While most centipedes are not considered dangerous to humans, some larger species possess venomous pincers or fangs located on their first pair of legs. When threatened or provoked, they can deliver a painful sting or bite. The severity of the reaction to a centipede sting can vary depending on the species and the individual's sensitivity to the venom. Centipede bites are painful but not life-threatening. If a Service member is stung by a centipede, first aid steps to take are:

- Stay calm and move away from the centipede to avoid further stings.

- Wash the affected area gently with soap and water to clean the wound and reduce the risk of infection.

- Apply a cold compress or ice pack wrapped in a cloth to the sting site. This can help alleviate pain and reduce swelling.

- Take over-the-counter pain relievers, such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen, to manage pain if necessary. Follow the instructions and recommended dosage.

- Keep the sting site clean and dry and avoid scratching or irritating the area to prevent infection.

16-70. If there are signs of an allergic reaction, such as difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or widespread hives, seek immediate medical attention.

### Millipedes

16-71. Millipedes, belonging to the class Diplopoda, are arthropods distinguished by their elongated, segmented bodies with two pairs of legs per body segment, excluding the first few segments. These creatures are commonly found in moist environments such as forests, gardens, and areas with decaying vegetation.

16-72. Unlike centipedes, millipedes lack venomous pincers or fangs and are generally harmless to humans. However, they possess defensive mechanisms that can release toxins or irritating chemicals, which may cause mild reactions in some individuals, particularly those with sensitive skin.

16-73. If a Service member encounters a millipede and experiences irritation or a sting, the recommended first aid steps include:

- Washing the affected area gently with soap and water to cleanse the site and remove any secretions from the millipede.

- Applying a cold compress or an ice pack wrapped in a cloth to reduce swelling and alleviate discomfort at the sting site.

- Using over-the-counter topical antihistamines or hydrocortisone creams to relieve itching or irritation.

- Avoiding scratching or further irritating the site to prevent infection.

16-74. In cases where there is a severe allergic reaction, characterized by symptoms such as difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or widespread hives, it is crucial to seek immediate medical attention. While millipede stings are typically mild and self-limiting, if symptoms persist, worsen, or if there are concerns about an individual’s reaction, consulting a healthcare professional for further advice and treatment is advisable.

### Fire Ants

16-75. Fire ants are a type of stinging ant that are native to South America but have spread to various parts of the world, including the southern U.S, Southeast Asia, and Australia. Fire ants are known for their aggressive behavior and painful stings.

16-76. When a fire ant stings, it injects venom into the skin, resulting in a sharp, burning sensation and the formation of a raised, reddish bump. Multiple ants may attack simultaneously, leading to multiple stings. If stung by a fire ant, the first aid steps are to:

- Move away from the area where the ants are present to avoid further stings.

- Remove any clothing or jewelry that may have ants trapped in them, as they can continue to sting.

- Gently wash the affected area with soap and water to clean the site.

- Apply a cold compress or ice pack wrapped in a cloth to the sting site. This can help reduce pain and swelling.

- Over-the-counter pain relievers, such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen, can be taken to help manage pain if necessary. Follow the instructions and recommended dosage.

- Avoid scratching the sting site to prevent infection. If itching is severe, over-the-counter antihistamines or hydrocortisone creams may be used under the guidance of a health care professional.

- Monitor the individual for signs of an allergic reaction, such as difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or widespread hives. If these symptoms occur, seek immediate medical attention.

16-77. Fire ant stings can be particularly problematic for individuals who are allergic or hypersensitive to ant venom. In such cases, immediate medical attention should be sought. These first aid steps are general guidelines, and the severity of the reaction may vary from person to person. If there are concerns about the Service member’s health or if the symptoms worsen or persist, consult a health care professional for appropriate advice and treatment.

### Ticks

16-78. A tick is a small arachnid parasite that feeds on the blood of animals, including humans. Ticks are found in various environments, such as forests, grasslands, and shrubby areas. They latch onto their hosts and can transmit diseases through their bites.

16-79. When a tick bites, it burrows its mouth parts into the skin to feed on blood. If you discover a tick attached to your skin, it's important to remove it promptly and take the following first aid steps:

- Prepare for tick removal: Use fine-tipped tweezers to grasp the tick as close to the skin's surface as possible. Avoid squeezing or crushing the tick's body.

- Remove the tick: Gently and steadily pull the tick upward with even pressure. Do not twist or jerk it. The goal is to remove the entire tick, including its mouthparts. Dispose of the tick by placing it in a sealed bag or container or by submerging it in alcohol.

- Clean the bite area: Thoroughly clean the bite area with soap and water or an antiseptic solution to reduce the risk of infection.

- Apply an antiseptic: After cleaning the bite area, you can apply an antiseptic ointment or cream to further prevent infection.

- Monitor for symptoms: Keep an eye on the bite site for any signs of an allergic reaction, infection, or the appearance of a rash. If you notice any concerning symptoms, seek medical attention.

16-80. Certain tick-borne diseases, such as Lyme disease or Rocky Mountain spotted fever, may take time to manifest symptoms. If in an area where these diseases are prevalent or if you develop symptoms such as fever, rash, joint pain, or flu-like symptoms after a tick bite, it is crucial to consult a health care professional for proper evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment.

16-81. Prevention is key when it comes to ticks. To minimize the risk of tick bites, wear protective clothing when in tick-prone areas, use insect repellents containing DEET, perform regular tick checks after outdoor activities, and take appropriate measures to protect pets from ticks as well. DEET (chemical name, N, N- diethyl-meta-toluamide) is the active ingredient in many repellent products. It is widely used to repel biting pests such as mosquitoes and ticks.

### Warning

**Lyme disease, transmitted by Blacklegged “Deer” tick, can cause long- term neurological disorders and other complications if not identified and treated early.**

16-82. The signs and symptoms of a tick bite can vary depending on factors such as the type of tick, the individual's sensitivity to the tick's saliva, and the presence of any tick-borne diseases. The common signs and symptoms associated with tick bites are:

- Redness and swelling: After a tick bite, it is common to experience localized redness and swelling at the site of the bite. This is a normal reaction to the tick's saliva.

- Itching or irritation: The bite site may be itchy or irritated, like a mosquito bite. Avoid scratching to reduce the risk of infection.

- Tick remnants: If a tick is not promptly and properly removed, parts of the tick's body, such as its mouthparts, may remain embedded in the skin. This can lead to ongoing irritation and a risk of infection.

- Rash: In some cases, a small red spot or rash may develop around the tick bite. This can expand over time and may resemble a target-like pattern. It is important to note that not all tick bites result in a rash.

- Flu-like symptoms: Tick-borne diseases such as Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, or babesiosis can cause flu-like symptoms. These may include fever, fatigue, headache, muscle aches, joint pain, and swollen lymph nodes. However, these symptoms usually develop days or weeks after the tick bite.

- Allergic reaction: In rare cases, individuals may have an allergic reaction to tick bites, resulting in more severe symptoms such as difficulty breathing, swelling, hives, or anaphylaxis. Immediate medical attention is necessary in such cases.

16-83. Not all tick bites lead to the transmission of diseases, and most tick bites are harmless. If a Service member experiences any concerning symptoms or if you live in an area where tick-borne diseases are prevalent, seek medical attention for proper evaluation, diagnosis, and appropriate treatment.
