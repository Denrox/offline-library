# Chapter 4: Massive Bleeding Control

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Massive Bleeding Control

4-1. The number one medical priority in TCCC is early control of severe bleeding. Extremity hemorrhage is the most frequent cause of preventable battlefield deaths. Understanding the human body and how it works along with how casualties survive in combat results in efficient and proper TCCC skills application and an increased survivability of combat casualties.

4-2. Injury to a major blood vessel can quickly lead to shock and death. Only life-threatening extremity bleeding warrants intervention during CUF (if the tactical situation allows). Casualties with injuries to large blood vessels in the extremities (such as the femoral artery in the thigh, or the brachial artery in the upper arm) can result in death in as little as three minutes if bleeding is allowed to continue. If life-threatening bleeding is encountered at the junctional areas (neck, axillary, or inguinal) treatment with a hemostatic dressing is not performed until the casualty transitions to TFC.

4-3. Treatment priorities should follow MARCH-PAWS, with massive bleeding (M) always being the most important. The treatment priorities are not a linear process; if at any time massive bleeding is not controlled, go back to M.

4-4. The initial casualty evaluation should be a rapid head-to-toe check for any unrecognized life-threatening bleeding (a blood sweep). This blood sweep is a systematic way to ensure rapid identification of any unrecognized life-threatening bleeding, and should include a visual and hands-on (palpation) inspection by checking the—

- Neck.

- Axillary (armpit).

- Inguinal (groin).

- Legs.

- Arms.

- Back.

4-5. The priority in caring for a casualty, should be to identify and stop life-threatening bleeding and prevent them from bleeding to death. Bleeding is considered life-threatening when—

- There is pulsing or steady bleeding from the wound.

- The overlying clothes are soaked with blood.

- Blood is pooling on the ground.

- There is a traumatic amputation of an extremity.

- Bandages or makeshift bandages used to cover the wound are ineffective and are steadily becoming soaked with blood.

- There was prior bleeding, and the patient is now in shock (unconscious, confused, and pale).

4-6. A TQ is immediately needed when any of the above examples are present on an extremity. Injury to a major vein or artery can result in shock or death from blood loss in minutes. Extremity (arm or leg) hemorrhage is a leading cause of preventable combat death. Using TQ(s) to stop the bleeding is essential to the survival of casualties with these types of injuries.

4-7. During CUF a TQ is the only medical intervention available to stop life-threatening bleeding from an extremity injury. Other wounds (such as neck, armpit, groin, or abdomen) are not treated during CUF. If the casualty is able, direct them to apply pressure to the wound as self-aid. Airway and other issues are not treated until the TFC phase.

4-8. It may be difficult to determine whether hemorrhage is life-threatening until the TFC phase when the wound can be exposed and evaluated. A suspected life-threatening hemorrhage is immediately treated.

4-9. During CUF, if a casualty shows to have massive bleeding, from an extremity, a hasty TQ is applied “high and tight” on the injured extremity and then cover should be acquired as quickly as possible. The

equipment in the casualty’s first aid kit should be used and not that of the responder. Do not put a TQ directly over the knee or elbow, over a holster, or cargo pocket that contains bulky items.

4-10. Direct pressure can and should be used as a temporary measure until a TQ or dressing is in place. It is difficult to use direct pressure alone to control significant bleeding or while moving the casualty. Direct pressure can be used if treatment no longer maintains control of the bleeding while a new treatment is started.

4-11. In CUF, consider placing one or more hasty TQs in a “high and tight” manner to an arm or leg to control massive life-threatening bleeding. This should be accomplished in less than one minute. If the casualty is conscious, the responder should direct the casualty to render self-aid and apply a TQ to themselves, if they have one. A TQ applied in the CUF must be re-evaluated in TFC.

4-12. To effectively perform first aid, Service members need a fundamental understanding of the key body systems, including their structures and functions. Knowledge of the respiratory, circulatory, and musculoskeletal systems is crucial as it helps in assessing injuries and providing the appropriate first aid without causing further harm to the casualty. Familiarity with these systems also aids in recognizing when they are damaged or not functioning properly. Injuries to or compromises of these vital systems can lead to severe, permanent injury or even death. Therefore, a solid grasp of these systems not only supports effective first aid intervention but also helps prevent intensifying the casualty's condition.

### Circulatory System

4-13. The heart and the blood vessels (arteries, veins, and capillaries) circulate blood throughout the body. The heart is divided into two separate halves, each acting as a pump. The left side pumps oxygenated blood (bright red) through the arteries into the capillaries. Nutrients and oxygen pass from the blood through the walls of the capillaries and into the cells. At the same time, waste products and carbon dioxide leave the cells and enter the capillaries. From the capillaries, the blood is carried through the veins to the right side of the heart and then into the lungs where it expels the carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen. Blood in the veins is dark red because of its low oxygen content. Blood does not flow through the veins in spurts as it does through the arteries. The entire system of the heart, blood vessels, and lymphatics is called the circulatory system.

##### Blood Vessels

4-14. The body has a complex network of blood vessels that are crucial for blood circulation. These vessels are three main types. First, arteries take oxygen-rich blood from the heart and branch into smaller arterioles, eventually leading to tiny capillaries. Next, capillaries play a key role in exchanging oxygen, nutrients, and wastes between blood and tissues. The blood, now lacking oxygen, moves into small veins called venules, which combine to form larger veins. Lastly, veins are responsible for carrying blood back to the heart, completing the circulation process.

4-15. The blood vessels in the body are all connected and work together. They help deliver oxygen and nutrients to every part of the body and get rid of waste materials. This shows how each type of blood vessel depends on the others, highlighting their important roles in a Service member’s overall health.

##### Blood

4-16. Blood is a mixture of plasma (liquid component), white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets. The body contains about 5 to 6 quarts (about 5 liters) of blood. Once blood is pumped out of the heart, it takes 20 to 30 seconds to circulate through the body and return to the heart.

4-17. Blood performs essential functions as it circulates through the body. It delivers oxygen and essential nutrients (such as fats, sugars, minerals, and vitamins) to the body's tissues. It carries carbon dioxide to the lungs and other waste products to the kidneys for elimination from the body. In addition, it carries components that fight infection and stop bleeding.

### Warning

**If bleeding is not stopped, excessive blood loss can quickly lead to the death of the casualty. It is imperative that first aid providers quickly determine if a casualty is losing blood. Many injuries can result in blood vessels being torn which result in excessive blood loss.**

### Heartbeat

4-18. The heart functions as a pump to circulate blood continuously through the blood vessels to all parts of the body. It contracts, forcing the blood from its chambers. The heart then relaxes, permitting its chambers to refill with blood.

4-19. The rhythmical cycle of contraction and relaxation is called the heartbeat. The normal heart rate (heartbeats per minute) varies based on the age and physical condition of the individual at rest. For example—

- Adults: 60 to 100 heartbeats per minute.

- Children (1 to 6 years): 70 to 120 heartbeats per minute.

- Infants (6 to 12 months): 80 to 140 heartbeats per minute.

- Infants (0 to 5 months): 90 to 140 heartbeats per minute.

**_Note._** The typical pulse for Service members and athletes is 40 to 60 heart beats per minute and is lower than the average adult population due to physical conditioning.

4-20. It is common for a Service member’s pulse to be elevated above the normal range during combat operations as this is both a stressful and high exertional state. Elevated pulse in combination with suspected injuries causing hemorrhage suggests developing shock.

58B **PULSE**

4-21. The heartbeat causes a rhythmical expansion and contraction of the arteries as it forces blood through them. This cycle of expansion and contraction can be felt (monitored) at various points in the body and is called the pulse. The common points for checking the pulse are at the—

- Side of the neck (carotid).

- Groin (femoral).

- Wrist (radial).

- Ankle (posterior tibial).

**_Note._** DO NOT use your thumb to check a casualty’s pulse because you may confuse the beat of your own pulse with that of the casualty.

### Warning

**It is imperative that first aid providers quickly determine if the casualty has a pulse (heartbeat). Absence of a pulse (heartbeat) will lead to the death of the casualty if not quickly restored.**

> 4-22. The literature on combat casualty care raises questions about the reliability of traditional blood pressure measurements as indicators of specific pulse types. However, it underscores a crucial reality: as hemorrhage severity increases, more central pulses are likely to be lost, demanding prompt and decisive intervention. Identifying and responding to these central pulses swiftly is imperative in managing severe hemorrhage and ensuring the best possible outcomes for casualties.

#### Carotid Pulse

4-23. To ascertain the carotid pulse, locate the pulse on the casualty's neck nearest to you. Gently place the tips of your first two fingers alongside their thyroid cartilage, commonly referred to as the Adam's apple (see figure 4-1). This method allows for an effective assessment of the pulse, rhythm, and strength.

#### Femoral Pulse

4-24. To assess the femoral (groin) pulse, apply gentle pressure with the tips of your first two fingers to the center of the groin area (see figure 4-2). This method enables you to accurately detect the presence and strength of the femoral pulse.

## Equipment Used for Massive Bleeding Control

4-27. Specialized equipment plays a crucial role in effectively managing massive bleeding, offering immediate and life-saving interventions. TQs, junctional tourniquets, hemostatic agents, hemostatic gauze, and pressure dressings, are among the tools used for this purpose. These items form integral components of the TCCC approach to massive bleeding control, empowering military personnel to address severe hemorrhage swiftly and efficiently in the field and enhancing the likelihood of survival for wounded individuals.

### Tourniquets

4-28. TQs serve as critical devices for halting severe bleeding from extremities, such as arms or legs, by effectively restricting blood flow when applied higher to the injury site, thus preventing further blood loss.

4-29. Considered the most vital lifesaving item in the first aid kit, TQs should be kept easily accessible and ready for use during combat missions. The "TQ, Nonpneumatic Combat Application One-Handed," commonly known as the combat application TQ, is the most prevalent type in military inventory, featuring key components such as the windlass rod for twisting and tightening, a windlass clip to secure the rod, a windlass safety strap for added security, and a routing buckle for TQ band management after looping it around an arm or leg.

4-30. It's imperative that all personnel deployed on combat missions have a CoTCCC recommended TQ readily available in a standard location on their battle gear and are trained in its proper usage. Additionally, casualties should be capable of easily and swiftly accessing and applying their own TQs, emphasizing the importance of not storing TQs at the bottom of the pack, and prioritizing the use of the casualty’s first aid kit TQ first.

### Hemostatic Dressing and Wound Packing

**4-31.** Hemostatic dressings, engineered with clotting agents like kaolin or chitosan, are purpose-built to staunch bleeding efficiently. These dressings are either packed directly into deep or narrow wounds or applied directly onto a bleeding wound, accelerating blood clotting far more effectively than traditional dressings.

**4-32.** When severe bleeding is noted from a junctional area, such as the neck, axillary or inguinal locations of the body (areas were TQs cannot be applied) hemostatic dressings should be used for massive bleeding control. It's essential to note that they should not be used inside the abdomen or chest, where emergency bandages or other trauma dressings are more appropriate.

**4-33.** Each first aid kit is typically equipped with one hemostatic dressing and one dry sterile gauze. The hemostatic dressings recommended by the CoTCCC are known to be safe and include active ingredients that help blood to clot at the wound site. Hemostatic dressings are available in various types, one type of hemostatic dressing is impregnated with kaolin, another type contains celox granules, or the third type contains chitosan. Chitosan works by binding red blood cells and platelets to the surface of the dressing, enhancing clot formation. Importantly, despite being derived from shellfish, chitosan does not trigger allergic reactions in individuals with shellfish allergies.

### Pressure Dressings

4-34. Pressure dressings play a crucial role in stemming bleeding by applying direct pressure on wounds. Typically comprised of an elastic bandage and a sterile pad, they are placed over the wound and securely fastened to maintain pressure on the injured area. These dressings not only aid in blood clotting but also compress the open blood vessel, assisting in controlling bleeding effectively. After bleeding has been stemmed with a hemostatic dressing or packing material, it's imperative to maintain pressure on the wound. This is achieved by applying a pressure dressing directly on top of the hemostatic dressing or packing material and wrapping it around the limb, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the wound and gauze applied previously.

4-35. The CoTCCC-recommended emergency bandage or trauma dressing serves as a versatile solution for most wounds, particularly those not severe enough to require a TQ. These dressings can be used alone or in conjunction with other hemorrhage control methods, such as hemostatic gauze and normal gauze, to bolster their effectiveness in controlling bleeding by providing consistent pressure to the wound.

## Tier 1 Skills for Massive Bleeding Control

4-36. The urgency of promptly stopping massive external bleeding cannot be overstated, as the longer a Service member bleeds from a major wound, the lower their chances of survival become. Severe injuries have the potential to cause death in as little as three minutes due to blood loss. During TFC, when evaluating the casualty for the location, type, and size of the wound or injury, it is essential to cut or tear their clothing and carefully expose the entire area of the wound. This procedure ensures proper visualization of the injury while minimizing the risk of further contamination. It's crucial not to touch the wound directly and to keep it as clean as possible to prevent complications. It's important to note that in a chemical environment, protective clothing should not be removed, and dressings should be applied over the protective clothing. The skills used by all Service members to control massive bleeding include the application of TQs, hemostatic dressings, and pressure bandages.

### Application of Tourniquets

4-37. In combat scenarios, especially while under enemy fire, a TQ stands as the primary means to control massive extremity bleeding, enabling individuals, battle buddies, or combat medics to swiftly manage lifethreatening hemorrhages until the casualty can be moved away from the firefight. Always prioritize treating life-threatening hemorrhage while seeking cover. The application of a TQ to a massive bleed is timesensitive, with the TQ aiming to halt bleeding within one minute and be fully secured within three minutes, casualties with uncontrolled massive bleeding can succumb within these initial minutes. Expecting pain during application is crucial as it signifies proper TQ application.

4-38. The casualty may need to apply a one-handed TQ (see figure 4-5) to an upper extremity when applying self-aid. One-handed TQs are used to apply self-aid for bleeding from an injury to the upper arm or forearm. (One-handed windlass and one-handed ratchet TQs are the TQs of choice; they are effective and can be applied quickly).

**Figure 4-5. Windlass and Ratchet Tourniquets**

4-39. A hasty TQ is implemented as "high and tight" on the wounded extremity or when the bleeding source is ambiguous during the CUF phase. However, during TFC, all TQs should be deliberate, placed two to three inches above the wound directly on the skin, not over clothing. Once the casualty is in a safer position, the bleeding source should be reassessed, and the effectiveness of the TQs verified. If bleeding remains uncontrolled, a second TQ may be necessary, particularly for severe bleeding wounds of the thigh.

4-40. It is crucial to recognize that TQs should be used cautiously, with the optimal safe duration being less than two hours. Beyond this timeframe, tissues may start to die, potentially leading to limb loss after six hours. Regular reassessment of TQ effectiveness is necessary, including checking for stopped bleeding, absent distal pulse, and ensuring no pulse below the TQ on the limb. Common errors when applying TQs include not using one, when necessary, not tightening it adequately, and using it for minimal bleeding. Additionally, TQs should never be removed except by medical personnel at a higher role of care. If the source of massive bleeding is evident, such as from an amputation or major wound, immediate bleeding control measures like applying a TQ or wound packing should be initiated, especially in areas where a TQ cannot be

used, such as the groin, armpit, or neck. The following steps address tourniquet application in CUF for all Service Members, CLS, and medical personnel.

##### 4-41. One-Handed (Windlass) TQ Application CUF:

- Remove TQ from JFAK and/or carrying pouch.

- Insert the wounded extremity through the loop of the self-adhering band.

- Position the TQ above the bleeding site, high on the extremity over the clothing/uniform.

- Ensure that all the slack in the self-adhering band is pulled through the routing buckle before the band is fastened back on itself and the windlass is twisted.

- Twist the rod until bleeding has stopped (complete steps 1-5 in under 1 min).

- Lock the windlass rod in place with the windlass clip.

- Route the self-adhering band around the rod and between the clips.

- Secure the windlass safety strap.

- If no other major bleeding is present, move to cover.

**_Note._** Do not document the TQ application time until the TFC phase.

##### 4-42. One-Handed (Ratchet) TQ Application CUF:

- Remove TQ from the JFAK and/or carrying pouch.

- Insert the wounded extremity through the loop of the TQ band.

- Position the TQ above the bleeding site, high on the extremity over the clothing/uniform.

- Grasp the TQ loop with your teeth (or if able lean against a hard surface) to prevent slipping when tightening.

- Tighten the TQ strap as much as possible.

- Lift the lever arm of the ratcheting buckle and tighten by ratcheting the TQ until bleeding has stopped (complete steps 1-5 in under 1 min).

- Lock the ratchet on itself (it will click into place).

- If no other major bleeding is present, move to cover.

**_Note._** If bleeding is not controlled, continue to ratchet the maneuver device until bleeding has stopped. Do not document TQ application time until the TFC phase.

##### 4-43. Two-Handed (Windlass) TQ Application CUF:

- Remove TQ from JFAK and/or carrying pouch.

- Insert the wounded extremity through the loop of the self-adhering band or route the band around the limb, passing the tip through the slit of the routing buckle.

- Position the TQ above the bleeding site, high on the extremity over the clothing/uniform.

- Ensure that all the slack in the self-adhering band is pulled through the routing buckle before the band is fastened back on itself and the windlass is twisted.

- Twist the rod until bleeding has stopped (complete steps 1-5 in under 1 min).

- Lock the windlass rod in place with the windlass clip.

- Route the self-adhering band around the rod and between the clips.

- Secure the windlass safety strap.

- If no other major bleeding is present, move to cover.

**_Note._** Do not document TQ application time until the TFC phase.

##### 4-44. Two-Handed (Ratchet) TQ Application CUF:

- Remove the TQ from the casualty’s JFAK and/or carrying pouch.

- Insert the wounded limb through the TQ strap loop or route the strap around the limb; pass the tip through the buckle.

- Position the TQ above the bleeding site, high on the extremity over the clothing/uniform.

- Pull the strap as tightly as possible, removing all excess slack.

- Lift the lever arm of the ratcheting buckle and tighten by ratcheting the TQ until bleeding has stopped (complete steps 1-5 in under 1 min).

- Lock the ratchet on itself (it will click into place).

- If no other major bleeding is present, move the casualty to cover.

**_Note._** If bleeding is not controlled, continue to ratchet the maneuver device until bleeding has stopped. Do not document TQ application time until the TFC phase.

### Application of Hemostatic Dressing and Wound Packing

4-45. Hemostatic dressings serve as effective tools for controlling bleeding in areas where a TQ cannot be applied effectively, such as the groin, armpit, or neck. These dressings should be applied with direct and firm pressure and held for at least three minutes.

### Caution

Do not pack hemostatic dressings into the abdomen or chest.

4-46. After the bleeding has been controlled, it's crucial to check for a distal pulse to ensure the dressing hasn't been applied too tightly. Adjust the dressing if a pulse is not felt to restore circulation.

**WARNING The emergency bandage must be loosened if the skin distal to the injury becomes cool, blue, numb, or pulseless.**

4-47. Before applying the dressing, carefully examine the casualty for multiple wounds, as a projectile may have entered at one point and exited at another, with the exit wound typically being larger than the entrance wound.

4-48. When applying a bandage, prioritize using the casualty’s emergency bandage. Apply it by opening the plastic dressing package, placing the sterile pad directly over the wound, wrapping the elastic tail around the extremity, running it through the plastic pressure bar, and securing the closure bar to prevent slipping without causing excessive pressure.

4-49. While hemostatic dressings should be packed into limb wounds, they should not be used in the abdomen or chest. For compressible hemorrhage in areas where a TQ cannot be applied effectively, such as the neck, armpit, and groin, use a CoTCCC-recommended hemostatic dressing.

4-50. Despite containing active hemostatic agents, hemostatic dressings require direct pressure for at least three minutes to be effective. Non-CoTCCC-recommended supplies may not be as effective in controlling massive hemorrhage.

### Warning

**Continuously monitor the casualty for conditions necessitating basic lifesaving measures. Additionally, all open wounds should be checked for entry and exit points, with first aid administered accordingly.**

### Warning

**If a projectile remains lodged in the body, do not attempt to remove it. Apply a dressing. Similarly, if an object is impaled in the wound, do not remove it; instead, apply a dressing around the object and stabilize it with additional bulky materials to prevent further injury.**

### Application of Pressure Dressings

4-51. Depending on the nature of the injuries, the first responder may need to pack wounds and apply a pressure bandage. Hemostatic dressings, containing special clotting agents, are ideal for wound packing to promote blood clotting. If hemostatic dressings are unavailable, gauze bandage can be used to pack wounds.

### Warning

**Avoid packing wounds in the chest or abdomen.**

4-52. Pressure dressings are crucial for maintaining the effectiveness of hemostatic dressings, ensuring sustained pressure on the bleeding sources. In situations where pressure dressings are not available, maintaining firm, direct pressure is essential until medical assistance arrives.

4-53. When applying a pressure bandage over a packed wound, use short tugs while wrapping and continue with tension. Care should be taken to avoid wrapping the bandage too tightly, ensuring the skin below remains pink and warm, with a present pulse.

**_Note._** Service members assess tightness based on skin coolness and paleness rather than “capillary refill.”

4-54. Hemostatic dressings are only applied during the TFC phase. The steps for wound packing and pressure bandage are as follows:

- Expose the wound, if not previously exposed.

- Remove clothing and equipment as required.

- Locate the source of the most active bleeding and apply direct pressure.

- Remove the hemostatic dressing from its sterile package.

- If a hemostatic dressing is not available, use gauze or clean, dry cloth material.

- Pack the hemostatic dressing tightly into the wound directly over the site that has the most active bleeding.

**_Note._** Fill and pack the whole wound cavity tightly while keeping firm pressure on the wound. More than one hemostatic dressing or gauze may be required to stop the blood flow.

### Caution:

If a penetrating object is lodged in the casualty’s body, bandage it in place. Do not remove the object.

- Ensure the hemostatic dressing or gauze extends 1-2 inches above the skin.

**_Note._** If the hemostatic dressing or gauze does not extend 1 to 2 inches above the skin, place additional hemostatic dressing or gauze.

- After packing, continue to apply firm, manual pressure until the bleeding stops. Hold continuous direct pressure for a minimum of 3 minutes.

- Reassess to ensure bleeding has been controlled while maintaining pressure.

**_Note_** : Ensure blood is not seeping through or around the hemostatic dressing. If bleeding has stopped, leave the hemostatic dressing in place while still maintaining pressure.

- If bleeding has not been controlled and packed initially with a hemostatic dressing, remove the ineffective hemostatic dressing and repack starting at step three of this skill.

- If the wound was previously packed with gauze or other materials, apply additional gauze or materials and pressure (for another 3 minutes) until bleeding has stopped.

- Remove the pressure bandage from its package.

- Place the pad (bandage) directly over the wound on the previously applied hemostatic dressing while continuing to apply direct pressure.

- Wrap the pressure/elastic bandage tightly around the extremity, focusing pressure over the wound and ensuring that the edges of the pad are covered.

**_Note_** : If the bandage has a pressure bar, insert the elastic wrap completely into the pressure bar, pull the bandage tight, and reverse it back over the top of the pressure bar forcing it down onto the pad.

- Secure the hooking ends of the hood and loop straps or closure bar onto the last wrap of the bandage.

- Check for circulation below the pressure bandage by feeling for a distal pulse.

- Document all findings and treatments on a DD Form 1380 and attach it to the casualty.

**_Note._** If the skin below the pressure bandage becomes cool to touch, bluish, or numb, or if the distal pulse is no longer present, the pressure bandage may be too tight and should be loosened and reapplied. If bleeding reoccurs you may need to apply a limb or junctional tourniquet.

4-55. Secure the pressure dressing tail with hooks or a knot, taping them down to prevent loosening. Pressure bandages should not create a TQ effect; if the pulse is absent below the bandage, it's too tight and needs to be loosened and retied.

4-56. Signs of excessive tightness include bluish skin tint, coolness to touch, or numbness in the extremity below the bandage. All bandages should be reassessed after the casualty is relocated.

## Tier 2 Skills for Massive Bleeding Control

4-57. Tier 2 skills expand upon Tier 1 skills, which Service members apply to themselves or others in urgent situations. In the CUF phase, the tactical situation often limits the initiation of Tier 2 skills for hemorrhage control, typically extending only to the application of a hasty TQ. Tier 2 treatments such as the deliberate TQ and hemostatic pressure dressings are not applied during CUF due to the length of time to apply and the immediate threat to life from enemy fire.

4-58. Although Tier 1 and Tier 2 provide the same hemorrhage control treatment of hasty TQ during CUF, during TFC the Tier 2 CLS treats any massive bleeding, from an extremity, with deliberate TQs. Tier 2 personnel are also trained in junctional wound hemostatic pressure dressings.

### Application of Tourniquets

4-59. The CLS must possess proficiency in executing all Tier 1 massive bleeding control skills and be capable of directing or assisting Service members in their application. Emphasizing TQs as the primary method for bleeding control during CUF.

4-60. During TFC, where time allows for thorough assessment, exposing the wound becomes feasible, aiding in and pinpointing the precise site of bleeding. Deliberate TQs applied in this phase, positioned two to three inches above the wound directly on the skin, optimize effectiveness while minimizing unnecessary impact on healthy tissue.

4-61. The CLS conducts reassessment of TQs applied during CUF to ensure their efficacy. TQs hastily applied "high and tight" may need adjustment as medical personnel intervene, addressing the placement for optimal effectiveness.

4-62. If bleeding persists despite a previously applied TQ, the CLS should promptly apply a second deliberate TQ adjacent to the original, positioned higher on the limb. Bleeding cessation is expected within one minute, with full TQ securing achieved within three minutes. Any life-threatening bleeding not identified during CUF warrants immediate intervention with a TQ, hemostatic, or pressure dressing.

4-63. Documenting the time of TQ placement on both the TQ itself and the DD Form 1380 during TFC, rather than CUF, is crucial for subsequent medical personnel. Care should be taken to avoid placing TQs over joints or pockets containing bulky items, ensuring the TQs effectiveness.

### Application of Hemostatic Dressing

4-64. While hemostatic dressings should be packed into wounds of the limbs, when the source of bleeding is too high on the limb for TQ application (such as the neck, armpit, and groin) they should NOT be packed into the abdomen or chest. For compressible (external) hemorrhage, places where a TQ cannot be effectively applied, use a CoTCCC-recommended hemostatic dressing.

4-65. Although the hemostatic dressing contains the active hemostatic agents, direct pressure must be applied for at least three minutes. Non-CoTCCC-recommended supplies may be available; however, they have not been shown to be effective in controlling massive hemorrhaging.

### Application of Direct Pressure

4-66. After packing the wound with a hemostatic dressing or gauze, hold direct pressure for three minutes. Direct pressure can and should be used as a temporary measure until a TQ or dressing is in place. It is difficult to use direct pressure alone to control significant bleeding, or while moving the casualty. While packing a wound, maintain constant, direct pressure, at the source of the bleeding to be effective.

### Application of Wound Packing

4-67. Wounds should not be blindly packed. Effort must be taken to locate the source of bleeding and apply direct pressure immediately while preparing the gauze.

4-68. Wound packing:

- Identify and expose the wound.

- Locate the source of the most active bleeding and apply direct pressure, while preparing the hemostatic dressing.

- Remove the hemostatic dressing from its sterile package and pack it into the wound tightly, directly over the site of the most active bleeding.

- Fill and pack the whole wound cavity tightly while keeping firm pressure on the wound.

- Ensure the hemostatic dressing extends one to two inches above the skin.

- Hold continuous direct pressure for a minimum of three minutes.

- Reassess the wound to ensure that the bleeding has stopped.

- Apply a pressure bandage over the hemostatic dressing to secure it in place.

- Reassess the wound to ensure that the bleeding has stopped. If blood is seeping through or around the hemostatic dressing, the bleeding is not controlled, and the following steps should be applied:

- If extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove the initial, ineffective hemostatic dressing and replace them with new hemostatic dressings.

- If extra hemostatic dressings are unavailable, leave initial, ineffective hemostatic dressing in place and add extra gauze over the wound tract.

- If roller or compressed gauze was used initially, due to unavailability of hemostatic dressings, leave initial, ineffective roller or compressed gauze in place and add extra gauze over the wound tract.

- • Once bleeding has been controlled, apply a pressure bandage over the wound packing material to secure it in place.

**_Note._** More than one hemostatic dressing may be required to stop the blood flow.

### Application of Pressure Dressings

4-69. Once bleeding has been controlled with a hemostatic dressing or packing material, pressure must be maintained on the wound. All hemostatic dressings for significant bleeding should be secured with pressure bandages.

4-70. The pressure dressing is placed directly on top of the hemostatic dressing or packing material. While maintaining direct pressure on the hemostatic dressing or packing material, the CLS will wrap the pressure or elastic bandage tightly focusing pressure directly over the wound. Secure the pressure dressing tail either by hooks, or with a knot, and tape both down to ensure they do not come loose.

4-71. Pressure bandages should not have a TQ effect. If there is no pulse below the pressure bandage, it has been applied too tightly and will need to be loosened and retied. Another sign that the pressure bandage has been applied too tightly is the color of the skin. The skin will have a bluish tint and may be cool to the touch. The casualty will complain of numbness in the extremity below the bandage. All bandages should be reassessed by—

- Checking for circulation BELOW the pressure bandage by feeling for a distal pulse (a pulse below the bandage).

- Checking the skin BELOW the pressure bandage ensures the skin is not cool to the touch, has a bluish tint, or is numb.

- Checking every time a casualty is moved.

4-72. The pressure dressing is too tight if the pulse below the pressure dressing is no longer present, or the skin is cool. If circulation is blocked or stopped, loosen, and re-tie the bandage. Dressings and bandages should be reassessed and checked routinely.

### Application of Junctional Pressure

4-73. Junctional pressure is an alternative method to control bleeding. This method uses pressure to press at the site or point where a main artery supplying the wounded area lies near the skin surface or over bone (see figure 4-6 on page 55). These areas are known as junctional areas and are difficult to stop the bleeding even though the vessels are larger than in the extremities. Junctional areas are located at the junction of the extremities and neck with the torso.

**Figure 4-6. Junctional Anatomy**

4-74. Junctional hemorrhage can also occur on the extremities if the injury is too close to the torso for a TQ to be applied. Blood vessels at junctional areas are larger than in the limbs but can still be compressed with direct pressure. Junctional pressure may help in instances where bleeding is not easily controlled by TQs, where a hemostatic dressing has not yet been applied, or where pressure dressing may assist in clotting. Junctional pressure is a temporary measure that may buy time for more definitive treatment.

#### Neck Junctional Hemorrhage Control

4-75. A neck wound should be packed with a hemostatic dressing using your fingers to ensure that the gauze is packed in all parts of the wound. The wound should be packed sufficiently to ensure that the gauze extends one to two inches above the skin surface. Direct pressure must be maintained continuously for three minutes and maintained throughout the application of the dressing. A pressure dressing is applied on top of the gauze.

4-76. Neck junctional hemorrhage control:

- Consider body substance isolation

- Expose the injury and assess the bleeding source.

- Apply direct pressure to the bleeding source, use dressing from the casualty’s IFAK or JFAK.

- Pack wound tightly with the hemostatic gauze until the wound cavity is filled.

**_Note_** . These steps should be performed within 90 seconds.

- Ensure that the gauze extends 1 to 2 inches above the skin.

   - Hold pressure for a minimum of three minutes.

   - Reassess to ensure bleeding has been controlled while maintaining pressure.

   - If bleeding has not been controlled.

- If packed with hemostatic dressing, and extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove and repack.

- • If packed with gauze or initially packed with hemostatic dressing and no more hemostatic dressing is available, apply additional gauze and pressure (for three minutes) until the bleeding stops.

- Place an elastic bandage over the dressing leaving enough tail to tie it into a knot.

- While maintaining pressure, wrap diagonally across the chest under the opposite arm (armpit) around the back to the neck and back over the wound.

- Wrap the bandage (no less than 1 ½ times) over the packing material covering it completely.

#### Axillary Junctional Hemorrhage Control

4-77. The axillary (upper armpit) area is an area where the arm attaches to the torso. The axillary artery runs from the torso through the armpit and feeds oxygenated blood to both arms. Axillary junctional hemorrhage is very difficult to treat if attention to detail is not observed. The steps for controlling Axillary Junctional Hemorrhage are:

- Consider body substance isolation.

- Cut away any clothing (to expose injury) and lift the arm to assess the bleeding source.

- Apply direct pressure to the most active bleed. Prepare hemostatic dressing.

- Extend the casualty’s arm at a 90-degree angle by placing it on your shoulder.

- Pack the wound tightly with the hemostatic gauze until the wound cavity is filled.

**_Note._** These steps should be performed within 90 seconds.

- Ensure that the gauze extends 1 to 2 inches above the skin.

- Hold pressure for a minimum of three minutes.

- If the bleeding has not been controlled.

- If packed with hemostatic dressing, and extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove and repack starting at step 5.

- If packed with gauze or initially packed with a hemostatic dressing and no more hemostatic dressing is available, apply additional gauze and pressure (for three minutes) until bleeding stops.

**_Note._** Clothing may need to be cut away to properly expose the injury. The best position to treat the casualty is the seated position. If the casualty cannot be treated in the seated position, you will need to sit the casualty up as much as possible to apply the elastic bandage.

- While maintaining pressure on the dressing/gauze, wrap the pressure (or elastic) bandage around the injured shoulder twice to ensure the gauze underneath is completely covered.

- Wrap the elastic bandage across, back and under the opposite armpit, anchoring around the opposite shoulder in a “figure 8” pattern.

- Depending on the bandage used, secure with the closure bar of tie tails of elastic bandage with a nonslip knot.

- Secure the pressure (elastic) bandage tails and knot using tape, wrapping a minimum of 1 ½ times around the knot.

- Swat the upper arm to the side of the chest using a cravat.

- Continue to assess the wound for further bleeding.

## Expedient Techniques for Hemorrhage Control

4-78. Expedient techniques for hemorrhage control are of paramount importance in TCCC due to their ability to rapidly address life-threatening bleeding in high-stress and time-critical combat situations. Expedient techniques enable military personnel to intervene quickly and effectively control hemorrhage and prevent further blood loss. These techniques, such as the use of TQs, pressure dressings, and hemostatic agents, are specifically designed to be simple, easily applied, and accessible to all Service members, regardless of medical training. By swiftly initiating hemorrhage control, expedient techniques significantly increase the chances of survival, allowing injured individuals to receive timely care, and ultimately improving their overall mission success, and preserving the fighting strength of the military forces.

### Improvised Tourniquet

4-79. Using one of the CoTCCC-recommended TQs is the safe and preferred procedure for hemorrhage control. Improvised TQs are much less effective than commercially available TQs discussed in paragraph 4-37 on page 48. Improvised TQs are difficult to make and apply without extensive practice, however they may be needed in situations where commercially produced TQs are unavailable, or supplies have been exhausted.

### Caution

The risks associated with using improvised TQs include:

- Damage may occur to the skin if the band is too narrow.

- Bleeding may worsen.

- Bleeding may not be completely controlled.

- Likely to loosen overtime from not being properly secured.

4-80. If a TQ is not available, pack the wound and hold direct pressure over the source of the heaviest bleed, FIRST. If this fails to control the bleeding in an arm or leg, only then consider an alternate improvised TQ.

4-81. An alternate improvised TQ uses other materials on hand. In constructing one, it is recommended that you fold a cravat (or other pliable material) to a width of approximately 1 ½ to 2 inches and tighten it with some sort of windlass rod (a straight rod that can be used to twist the material tighter). Then secure the rod to ensure it does not loosen.

**_Note._** The cravat is included in the JFAK, however it is not part of the Army IFAK Generation 1 or 2. To resource the cravat for the Army IFAK, the nomenclature is: Sterile Burn Dressing Cravat, NSN: 6510-01-587-6579; Muslin Compressed Bandage NSN: 6510-00-201-1755.

4-82. An improvised TQ should not be used except as an absolute last resort when there is no other option to control life-threatening bleeding. If no TQ is available, pack the wound and hold direct pressure over the main source of bleeding.

4-83. Continue to check the TQ, as improvised TQs are prone to loosening due to (but not limited to) casualty movement, shifting fluid, and quality of improvised material.

### Inguinal Hemorrhage Control with an Improvised Pressure Delivery Device

4-84. A pressure delivery device (PDD) is made using improvised materials, such as a shoe or boot, full water bottle, or canteen which applies additional pressure to the wound after it has been packed. A PDD may be needed to apply additional and targeted pressure to control inguinal or groin hemorrhage.

4-85. For groin injuries packed with hemostatic dressings, use an improvised junctional PDD to secure the dressing. The PDD is placed in the groin while continuously maintaining pressure to the dressing.

4-86. The PDD is then secured with a TQ, which is wrapped over the hips with the windlass or ratchet placed directly over the improvised device and tightened to add additional pressure. Two TQs placed together may be needed when improvising a PDD.

**_Note_** . Junctional hemorrhages involve larger blood vessels requiring more pressure (and targeted pressure) than can be applied with a pressure dressing alone.

4-87. Inguinal (Groin) Hemorrhage Control with Improvised Junctional Pressure Device:

- Consider body substance isolation.

- Expose the injury and assess the bleeding source.

- Apply direct pressure to the wound using a hand, fist, or elbow and open the hemostatic gauze package.

- Tightly pack the wound with hemostatic gauze until the wound cavity is filled (finishing the packing within 90 seconds).

- Hold pressure for a minimum of 3 minutes.

- Reassess to ensure that the bleeding has been controlled while maintaining pressure.

- If packed with hemostatic dressing, and extra hemostatic dressings are available, remove and repack.

- • If packed with gauze or initially packed with hemostatic dressing and no more hemostatic dressing is available, apply additional gauze and pressure (for three minutes) until bleeding stops.

- Select and position a pressure delivery device, such as a canteen, into the inguinal gutter while maintaining pressure.

- Select a TQ that can wrap around the casualty’s waist/hip area or connect the two tourniquets together.

- Place the windlass or ratchet TQ directly over the middle of the pressure delivery device; ensure that the routing buckle is located towards the middle of the body.

- Remove all slack from the band or strap using a pushing motion across the casualty’s body before tightening the TQ.

- Tighten the TQ and ensure that the bleeding is still controlled, and the distal pulse is absent.

**_Note_** . If the bleeding is not controlled and the distal pulse (a pulse located below the TQ) is present, remove any remaining slack in the strapping (if possible) and twist or ratchet the TQ device until bleeding is controlled and/or a distal pulse is absent.

- Visually inspect the placement of the equipment, ensuring that the pressure delivery device is in place and the windlass or ratchet is properly positioned over the device.

- Document all findings and treatments on DD Form 1380.
