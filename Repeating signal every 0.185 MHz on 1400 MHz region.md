## Accepted answer (score 1, by Ryuji AB1WX)

Your **SDR dongle** appears to be generating a strong, harmonic-rich signal at 185 kHz. It may be a switching mode power supply or something else. This issue can be more definitively tested using an independent spectrum analyzer like Tiny SA with a probe optimized for lower frequencies. The goal is to identify direct radiation from your dongle and the presence of the 185 kHz harmonics on the antenna line.

### Potential Solutions

Given the very different noise patterns from the two antennas, my suspicion goes to the antenna line impedance at 185kHz. With a regular dipole, it would appear open. With your Wifi antenna, I can't tell without taking it apart (or making a bunch of measurements) but it probably has a matching section or network.

#### Shunt Inductor

One way to deal with this is to change the feedline impedance at 185 kHz without changing that at 1.4 GHz.

A shunt inductor with sufficient reactance at 1.4 GHz but virtually short at 185 kHz can be an effective solution. This would require a very small inductance, making it easy to fabricate. If this makes the noise worse, you might want to use a very small coupling (series) capacitor to make 185 kHz open but connected at 1.4 GHz.

A third way is to use a shunt inductor with a series 51 ohm resistor, with a small coupling capacitor. This effectively forms a duplexer with its low-frequency port terminated at 50 ohms.

#### Common Mode Choke

Another useful addition could be a **common mode choke** on your feed line, positioned very near your dongle. This could be a 31-ferrite core wound multiple times. This setup would be transparent to the 1.4 GHz signal but would effectively choke the 185 kHz common mode current. Given the low frequency, you may need to use a 31-ferrite mix or something optimized for lower frequencies and higher inductance. You would likely need more turns than what is typical for HF feed lines.

#### USB Connection

It is also advisable to have a common mode choke between your laptop and your dongle. This prevents the 185 kHz noise from spreading through the **USB connection** to your laptop and subsequently to the power line.

#### Comprehensive Approach

Given the strength of the noise, it might be prudent to implement all these fixes:

- **Shunt Inductor, coupling capacitor, or both:** To handle the harmonic interference directly.
- **Common Mode Choke on Feed Line:** To contain the noise near the dongle.
- **USB Common Mode Choke:** To prevent noise from spreading through the USB connection.

Also, if you can identify the exact source of the noise, and if it is indeed a switching mode power supply, you could add a snubber (a series RC component; you could make one yourself as well) to the switching device to suppress the noise from the root cause. But this may be difficult depending on how it is implemented.

Let me know how it works out for you!

### Addendum

So according to ChatGPT, RTL-SDR does not have any switching power supply inside. Just cheap LDOs to make 3.3V for R820T2 and 1.2V for RTL2832U. So, if 185 kHz is from a switching regulator, it is likely from the USB power bus. Try using a powered hub with a clean power supply, or something to isolate the USB noise.

Also, you didn't specify the model, but if your RTL-SDR has a bias-T enabled, try to turn it off.

ChatGPT also said some lots of RTL-SDR had poor internal wirings and had lousy isolation or unwanted couplings in the front end. That would be bad.

Does the noise level change purely based on the distance from RTL-SDR, or does the ***direction*** of the antenna have some effect on the noise level? If *direction* matters, my new hypothesis is that the noise is already injected to the frontend on the board (185 kHz to harmonics up to 8 MHz range) and your antenna may be picking up the leaked LO or some other carrier frequency. RTL-SDR has at least some preselector filter and it is unlikely that low frequency gets passed through easily, but LO or other carriers would. The mixer is Gilbert cell implementation so the "LO" and "RF/IF" (in this case noise) can come in from any port, get multiplied and something will come out, and the result would be consistent with what you observed.

If that is the actual mechanism, I would also expect good shielding of RTL-SDR itself would reduce the problem. I would still try to clean up the source of 185 kHz (probably coming from USB/laptop power supply) and all that, because, even if you don't see those noises in the received spectra, you're probably hurting the receiver performance.
