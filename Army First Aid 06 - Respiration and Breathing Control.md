# Chapter 6: Respiration and Breathing Control

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Respiration and Breathing

6-1. After establishing an open airway, it is important to evaluate the casualty's respirations and maintain that airway in an open position. This is the “R” of MARCH-PAWS. Respiratory distress means difficulty breathing (rapid or abnormally slow breathing), in other words, it is difficult for the casualty to get air in or out. Airway and respiration are not addressed in CUF; however, they must be addressed in TFC.

6-2. The pleural space between the lungs and chest wall naturally has negative pressure, which helps the lungs to collapse (exhale) and expand (inhale). With either a blunt or penetrating injury to the chest wall or lungs, air may counteract the lung’s natural tendency to expand and collapse. This is due to positive pressure replacing negative pressure. It results in air being trapped in the pleural space, putting pressure on the affected lung. This forces the lungs to collapse and reduces the ability to get oxygen to the body.

6-3. While it may be easy to see a gunshot wound or shrapnel wound to the chest, it is important to evaluate casualties for additional injuries from blunt force trauma which may have been caused from an explosive device or from the chest hitting the steering wheel during a high-impact vehicle accident.

6-4. Signs of future life-threatening respiration issues include bruising, contusions (swelling around the chest, back, or rib cage), crepitus (crackling, popping, grating) that are felt or heard, or other deformities of the chest not normally seen without removing the clothing. Signs of respiratory distress include:

- Difficulty in breathing.

- Struggling to get air in and out.

- Breathing is too weak to be effective (less than six times per minute).

- Rapid breathing (greater than 20 times per minute).

**_Note._** The above signs may also indicate a penetrating chest wound injury. Report findings of respiratory distress to medical personnel.

## Equipment Used for Respiration and Breathing Control

6-5. In TCCC, various specialized equipment is used to address respiration and breathing control in combat situations. These tools are designed to promptly manage compromised airways and provide respiratory support. The primary equipment used includes:

- NPA.

- BVM.

- Chest Seal.

- 10–14-gauge catheter over needle device.

6-6. An NPA is a flexible tube inserted through the nose into the oropharynx to maintain an open airway and facilitate adequate breathing. It is particularly useful when there is a risk of tongue obstruction or in cases where the patient is conscious and able to maintain their own airway.

6-7. A BVM device consists of a self-inflating bag and a mask that is manually squeezed to deliver positive pressure ventilation. It is used when there is a need for artificial respiration to support or control the patient's breathing.

6-8. A chest seal is an airtight dressing that is applied over a penetrating chest wound to prevent the entry of air into the pleural cavity, reducing the risk of a tension pneumothorax. It allows for the restoration of normal respiratory function by maintaining appropriate chest pressure. The CoTCCC-recommended chest seal is a vented (preferred) self-adhering chest seal (see figure 6-1 on page 74) is used for treating and sucking chest wounds or open pneumothorax.

**Figure 6-1. Vented chest seal examples**

6-9. The equipment is essential components of TCCC's approach to respiration and breathing control. Utilizing this equipment enables military personnel to manage airway obstructions, provide respiratory support, and prevent potentially life-threatening complications associated with compromised breathing in combat scenarios.

6-10. The CoTCCC-recommended catheter-over-needle device is used to treat tension pneumothorax. The recommended catheter-over-needle device is either a 10-gauge, 14-gauge, or 3¼ inch long device (see figure 6-2).

## Tier 1 Skills for Respiration and Breathing Control

6-11. Often the act of just opening and maintaining the airway will allow the casualty to breathe properly. The first responder opens the casualty’s airway maintaining the head position to keep the airway open. Failure to maintain the open airway will prevent the casualty from receiving an adequate supply of oxygen. While maintaining an open airway the first responder checks for breathing, respiration rate (breaths per minute) and respiration quality (shallow and labored) by placing the casualty on their back and assessing their breathing by observing the casualty’s chest by—

- Looking for the chest to rise and fall.

- Listening for air escaping during exhalation by placing your ear near the casualty’s mouth.

- Feeling for the flow of air on your cheek (see figure 6-3).

**Figure 6-3. Check for breathing**

6-12. If air is not flowing freely in and out, the first responder opens the airway maneuvers as described in paragraphs 5-16 through 5-21.

**_Note._** If the casualty has a suspected neck or spine injury, and the situation allows, use, and maintain the jaw-thrust maneuver.

6-13. If the casualty is unconscious, and the respiratory rate is less than two in 15 seconds, or the casualty is making snoring or gurgling sounds, the first responder inserts an NPA by—

- Keeping the casualty in a face-up position.

- Lubricating the tube of the NPA with water.

- Pushing the tip of the casualty's nose upward gently.

- Positioning the tube of the NPA so that the bevel (pointed end) of the NPA faces toward the septum (the partition inside the nose that separates the nostrils).

- Inserting the NPA into the nostril and advance it until the flange rests against the nostril. Most NPAs are designed to be placed in the right nostril.

### Caution

Never force the NPA into the casualty’s nostril. If resistance is met, pull the tube out and attempt to insert it into the other nostril. If neither nostril will accommodate the NPA, place the casualty in the recovery position.

6-14. The first responder places the casualty in the recovery position by rolling the casualty as a single unit onto their side, placing the casualty's hand of the upper arm under the casualty's chin, and flexing the casualty's upper leg. The first responder then watches the casualty closely for life-threatening conditions and checks for other injuries.

6-15. If the casualty is not breathing, immediately start rescuing breathing and seek medical aid. If the casualty resumes breathing at any time during this procedure, the airway is kept open, and the casualty is continued to be watched. If the casualty continues to breathe, they should be transported to medical aid, or to an MTF IAW the tactical situation.

6-16. Exposure to smoke or toxic inhalants can cause difficulty breathing, but other injuries can also lead to respiratory distress. Life-threatening chest injuries can also result in respiratory distress. There are two types of chest injuries that can potentially be life-threatening, penetrating injuries, like those seen in gunshot or shrapnel wounds, or blunt trauma from blasts or vehicular accidents. It is important to roll the casualty to examine the front and back for signification torso trauma.

6-17. At this point in the MARCH-PAWS protocol, the responder's role is to carefully roll the casualty to examine both the front and back of the torso for any signs of chest trauma. It is crucial for the responder to identify and acknowledge if the casualty has a potentially life-threatening chest injury that is causing respiratory distress. The responder must then effectively communicate these findings to the medical personnel arriving on the scene, ensuring that the casualty receives the appropriate medical attention promptly.

### Important!

**DO NOT pack chest wounds with a hemostatic (or other)** **<mark>dressing.</mark>**

**REPORT a severe chest injury to medical personnel immediately.**

## Tier 2 Skills for Respiration and Breathing Control

6-18. The Tier 2 skills do not replace all Service member skills for respiration and breathing control. The CLS must reassess respiration and breathing control in TFC and start more advanced respiration and breathing control interventions. The CLS must be ready to treat an open pneumothorax, a tension pneumothorax, and identify if the treatment for those injuries was successful.

### Open Pneumothorax

6-19. An open pneumothorax is also known as a sucking chest wound. An open pneumothorax occurs when air enters the pleural space. The pleural space is the thin area between the lungs and chest wall.

6-20. When a casualty experiences penetrating torso trauma the elastic lung deflates and pulls away from the chest wall. If the hole, through the chest wall, is at least the size of a nickel or 2/3 the diameter of the trachea, on inspiration, the air now enters the chest through the hole instead of into the lungs through the normal airways of the mouth and nose. As a result, the affected lung cannot be fully re-inflated by inhalation.

6-21. An open pneumothorax usually occurs with a hole in the chest the size of a nickel or bigger. Not all chest wounds are sucking chest wounds; some do not penetrate as deeply as the lung cavity.

6-22. The first responders check the casualty's respiration and ability to breathe. All open or sucking chest wounds are treated by immediately applying a vented chest seal to cover the defect. If a determination cannot be made if the wound has penetrated the chest wall completely, treat the wound as though it were an open chest wound. A casualty with an open chest wound will exhibit one or more of the following signs and symptoms:

- A “sucking” or “hissing” sound when the casualty inhales.

- Difficulty breathing.

- A puncture wound of the chest.

- Froth or bubbles around the injury.

- Coughing up blood.

- Blood-tinged sputum (spit).

6-23. For respiratory distress not resolved by a chest seal or in a casualty with known or suspected chest or back trauma without an open sucking chest wound, consider a tension pneumothorax, and perform a needle decompression of the chest (NDC). Injuries to the chest are very serious and can be life-threatening. The casualty's condition can change quickly with a chest injury.

6-24. An open chest wound should be treated with a CoTCCC- recommended commercial chest seal found within the first aid kit, which maybe vented or non-vented. Vented chest seals are for treating penetrating wounds to the chest. The vented chest seals allow air to escape out of the chest while non-vented chest seals do not. The injured lung will remain partially collapsed, but the mechanics of respiration will be better.

6-25. Recommended treatment for open or sucking chest wounds is prompt application of a vented chest seal. If a vented chest seal is NOT available, a non-vented chest seal should be used. When the casualty inhales,

the plastic is sucked against the wound, preventing the entry of air. When the casualty exhales, trapped air can escape from the wound and out the valve.

### Warning

**Suspect a tension pneumothorax if the casualty’s condition worsens.**

**Treat the tension pneumothorax by burping the wound or temporarily removing the dressing.**

6-26. The wound should be uncovered unless clothing is stuck to the wound or in a CBRN environment. Once an open wound is discovered, place a gloved hand over the wound to provide a temporary seal.

**_Note._** If unsure that the wound has penetrated the chest wall completely, treat the wound as though it were an open chest wound. If multiple wounds are found, treat as soon as they are discovered. First wound found; first wound treated.

6-27. Fully open the outer wrapper of the casualty’s commercial chest seal located in the casualty’s first aid kit. Wipe any dirt, blood, or sweat from the skin with gauze supplied in the commercial chest seal. Instruct the casualty to fully exhale and then immediately place the vent of the chest seal directly over the first wound encountered. Do not touch the inner adhesive surface of the commercial chest seal. Ensure that the edges of the chest seal extend two inches beyond the wound. Firmly press the chest seal on the skin to ensure a good airtight seal. If the chest seal does not provide a good airtight seal, apply a two-inch tape (from the casualty’s first aid kit) to all four sides of the chest seal, ensuring the tape does not cover any vents, and secure it firmly to the casualty’s chest.

6-28. Roll the casualty on their side or have the conscious casualty sit up and examine their back for any wounds. If any wounds are present on the back, treat as a chest injury with a commercial chest seal. Encourage the casualty to breathe normally. Place the casualty in a position of comfort (usually sitting) if conscious, or in the recovery position (on the casualty’s side), with the injured side down if unconscious. Continue to monitor the casualty for signs of respiratory distress (increased difficulty breathing). If the casualty has increased difficulty breathing, lift the chest seal, release any trapped air by wiping blood or debris from the wound, and reseal or replace the chest seal upon exhalation. Seek medical aid and continue to monitor the casualty until relieved by medical personnel.

### Tension Pneumothorax

6-29. Tension pneumothorax is caused by significant torso (chest) trauma, or a blast injury resulting in severe and progressive respiratory distress. Progressive difficulty breathing is labored, and rapid breathing worsens overtime.

6-30. A tension pneumothorax is the second-leading cause of preventable deaths on the battlefield. As a tension pneumothorax develops, air enters the chest cavity through the wound with every inspiration but does not leave with expiration and is trapped. Every breath adds more air to the air space inside the rib cage and outside the lung, and the pressure inside the chest builds up (figure 6-4).

**Figure 6-4. Tension Pneumothorax**

6-31. When a lung is injured, it can act like a one-way valve, trapping air between the lung and chest wall. This trapped air builds pressure, potentially compressing both lungs and the heart, impairing their function and leading to respiratory distress and shock. In tension pneumothorax, this elevated pressure stops the lungs and heart from expanding normally, which can be fatal. Signs of tension pneumothorax vary from early to late stages:

- Early signs include:

   - Increasing difficulty breathing over time.

   - Shortness of breath.

   - Confusion, lightheadedness, or agitation from low oxygen.

   - Bluish discoloration around the mouth and lips.

   - Rapid pulse.

- Late signs, which may appear as the condition worsens, include:

   - Swollen neck veins.

   - Tracheal deviation (the windpipe shifting to one side).

   - Severe difficulty breathing, often with chest pain or anxiety.

6-32. These signs might be hard to detect in a combat situation. Responders must be vigilant for tension pneumothorax if there is any chest wound accompanied by increasing breathing difficulties.

6-33. It's crucial to monitor these signs during TFC. Like controlling bleeding, treating a tension pneumothorax is crucial; if left untreated, it can lead to severe respiratory issues, shock, and death. The initial treatment for a suspected tension pneumothorax is NDC. After this, any chest injury, whether an open pneumothorax or tension pneumothorax, needs further evaluation by medical personnel and likely evacuation

### Unsuccessful Treatment or Recurrence of Tension Pneumothorax

6-34. Casualties with a tension pneumothorax must be continuously monitored for reoccurring symptoms. The condition may initially be treated unsuccessfully and then spontaneously recur. The immediate remedy for the unsuccessful treatment or recurrence of a tension pneumothorax is to burp the chest seal, if one is in place.

6-35. If initial treatment of tension pneumothorax with NDC does not result in improvement, a second NDC should be attempted at the alternate recommended site. If tension pneumothorax initially responds to NDC, but symptoms later recur, then repeat NDC at the same site right beside the original NDC. If no improvement is noted after the second NDC, proceed with circulation assessment and treatment following the MARCHPAWS protocol.

### Warning

**DO NOT put an NDC through a chest seal! Use an alternate site instead.**

6-36. The treatment is to let the air trapped under pressure escape by inserting an NDC into the chest. The NDC site selection is based on the mechanism of injury and the physical findings during the tactical trauma assessment. The NDC (see figure 6-5 below) is placed either at the—

- Second, intercostal space in the mid-clavicular line.

- If the needle is used at the second intercostal space, ensure the site selection is outside the nipple line.

- Anterior axillary line in the fifth intercostal space.

### Caution

Watch needle placement to avoid the heart and arteries.

**Figure 6-5. Needle Decompression of the Chest Site Selection**

6-37. The NDC should be placed perpendicular to the body. After the NDC is placed, the needle should be held in place for 5-10 seconds before removing the needle and leaving the catheter. As with all other treatment given, it is critical to document all interventions on DD Form 1380.

6-38. After the casualty has been treated with an NDC, the casualty should be placed in the position of comfort. If the casualty is unconscious, place the casualty in the recovery position. If the casualty is conscious, allow the casualty to adopt the sitting position, if breathing is more comfortable.

## Expedient Techniques for Respiration and Breathing Control

6-39. Expedient techniques for respiration and breathing control in TCCC are crucial for immediate and effective management of a compromised casualty in combat situations. These techniques are designed to be quickly implemented by military personnel with minimal equipment, providing life-saving interventions in high-stress and time-critical environments. Expedient techniques may include maneuvers such as the head-tilt/chin-lift or jaw-thrust to open the airway, clearing obstructing objects, or repositioning the casualty to optimize breathing. Additionally, rescue breaths or mouth-to-mouth ventilation can be employed to support respiration. These expedient techniques are crucial time until advanced medical care can be provided, ensuring that the injured Service members receive immediate attention to maintain adequate respiration and oxygenation, thereby increasing their chances of survival in the challenging conditions of the battlefield.

### Penetrating Chest Injuries

6-40. Penetrating chest injuries involve the intrusion of foreign objects such as bullets, knives, or projectiles through the chest wall, penetrating into the thoracic cavity. This breach can lead to severe damage to vital structures including the lungs, heart, major blood vessels, or other organs within the thoracic cavity.

6-41. Penetrating chest injuries can result in significant internal bleeding, pneumothorax (collapsed lung), hemothorax (accumulation of blood in the chest cavity), tension pneumothorax (air buildup under pressure in the chest), or cardiac tamponade (compression of the heart due to blood accumulation). They are considered severe and life-threatening, requiring immediate intervention to stabilize the casualty and prevent further complications. Penetrating chest injuries can also be referred to as an open chest wound.

6-42. A CoTCCC vented commercial chest seal is the preferred type of chest seal. However, if one is unavailable, a non-vented commercial chest seal may be applied in the same manner as a vented commercial chest seal. If there is not a commercial chest seal available, the following examples can be used as occlusive (airtight) materials:

- Plastics such as the outer wrapper of the emergency dressing from the first aid kit.

- The combat ration packing.

- Cellophane.

6-43. An improvised chest seal may be used to treat an open chest wound by—

- Wiping any dirt, blood, or sweat from the skin with gauze or any available clothing.

- Instructing the casualty to fully exhale and then place the inner surface of the outer wrapper or other occlusive material directly over the wound.

**_Note._** Do not touch the inner surface of the occlusive material. Ensure that the edges of the occlusive material extend two inches beyond the wound.

- Applying two-inch tape from the casualty’s first aid kit to three sides of the occlusive material and secure it firmly to the casualty’s chest. Leave the side nearest to the ground un-taped.

- Rolling the casualty on their side or have the conscious casualty sit up and examine the back for any wounds.

- Checking the casualty for any wounds on the back and treating it as a chest injury with a large improvised occlusive chest seal (such as the plastic wrapper from the emergency dressing in the first aid kit).

- Placing the casualty in a position of comfort (usually sitting) if conscious, or in the recovery position (on the casualty’s side) with the injured side down if unconscious.

- Continuing to monitor the casualty for signs of respiratory distress (increased difficulty breathing).

**_Note._** If the casualty has increased difficulty breathing, lift the chest seal, release any trapped air by wiping blood or debris from the wound, and reseal or replace the chest seal upon exhalation.

### External Chest Compressions

6-44. External chest compressions, also known as Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR), in combat casualties is nearly always the result of hemorrhagic or obstructive shock, neither of which will respond to standard CPR. Conducting CPR may delay definitive treatment in nearly all cases. The CoTCCC guidelines recommend performing bilateral needle decompression and expedite blood product administration. Mission variables or the credentialed provider may guide exceptions to this rule in support of CPR on combat casualties. The administration of CPR on a combat casualty should be a rare circumstance and not the standard of care in this trauma population.

6-45. CPR is conducted using a specific technique to provide manual circulation support during cardiac arrest or when the heart has stopped beating effectively. A general outline of the steps involved in conducting external chest compressions is:

- Position: Ensure the person is lying on a firm, flat surface, such as the ground or a CPR board. Kneel beside the person's chest.

- Hand Placement: Place the heel of one hand on the center of the person's chest, between the nipples. For infants or children, use two or three fingers instead of the entire hand.

- Compression Depth: Press down firmly and smoothly. For adults, compress the chest at least two inches (five centimeters) deep. For infants or children, compress the chest about one-third to onehalf of its depth.

- Compression Rate: Perform compressions at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. This equates to about two compressions per second.

- Complete Chest Recoil: Allow the chest to fully recoil between each compression, without lifting your hands off the chest. This allows the heart to refill with blood.

- Compression to Ventilation Ratio: If you are performing CPR alone, do cycles of 30 chest compressions to two rescue breaths (mouth-to-mouth or mouth-to-mask). If there are two rescuers, the ratio can be 30:2 or continuous chest compressions with ventilation being delivered by the other rescuer.

- Continue until Help Arrives: Keep performing chest compressions until professional medical help arrives, or until the person shows signs of movement or starts breathing.

6-46. It is important to note that these steps provide a general overview of external chest compressions. Proper CPR training and certification are needed to gain a thorough understanding of the technique and to ensure accurate and effective application during emergency situations.

6-47. The duration of CPR can vary depending on the circumstances and response of the individual being treated. In general, CPR should be continued until one of the following conditions is met:

- Signs of Life: If the person shows obvious signs of life, such as breathing normally, coughing, or moving, CPR can be stopped.

- Professional Medical Help Arrives: CPR should be continued until professional medical personnel take over the resuscitation efforts.

- Provider Fatigue: If the person performing CPR becomes too exhausted to continue effectively, they can switch with another trained individual to maintain the quality of compression.

6-48. In many cases, CPR is performed continuously without interruptions until one of the above conditions is met. However, it is important to note that high-quality chest compressions are crucial for improving the chances of survival. Therefore, proper training in CPR techniques and regular updates on the latest guidelines are recommended to ensure effective and efficient resuscitation efforts.

### Rescue Breathing

6-49. Rescue breathing, also known as artificial ventilation, is a technique used in CPR to provide oxygen to a person who is not breathing or has inadequate breathing. It involves delivering breaths into the person's lungs to support their respiratory function. Rescue breathing is conducted executing the following:

- Position: Ensure the casualty is lying on a flat surface, preferably with their head tilted back slightly to open the airway.

- Clear the Airway: Check for any visible obstructions in the person's mouth or throat, and if present, gently remove them if possible.

- Seal the Mouth: If the casualty's mouth is open, use your hand to gently lift the chin upward to maintain an open airway. With your other hand, pinch the person's nose closed to prevent air from escaping.

- Breathing Technique: Place your mouth over the casualty's mouth, forming a seal, and blow into their mouth while watching for the chest to rise. Deliver the breath over one second and make sure it is sufficient to visibly inflate the chest.

- Breathing Rate: Provide rescue breaths at a rate of about one breath every five to six seconds (10 to 12 breaths per minute).

- Chest Recoil: Allow the chest to fully recoil between breaths. This enables the chest to return to its resting position, which helps in the exchange of gases during respiration.

- Continue Until Help Arrives: Perform rescue breathing until professional medical help arrives or until the person starts breathing on their own.

6-50. There are several methods for rescue breathing, each suitable for different situations and age groups. The main methods include:

- Mouth-to-Mouth: This common method involves the rescuer forming a seal over the casualty's mouth with their own and delivering breaths into their lungs.

- Mouth-to-Nose: Used when the casualty's mouth is injured, obstructed, or inaccessible, the rescuer closes the casualty's mouth and breathes into their nose to create a seal.

- Mouth-to-Mask: In situations like professional CPR or with specialized equipment, a pocket mask or BVM device is used. The mask covers the casualty's mouth and nose, and the rescuer delivers breaths by squeezing the bag or using the valve.

- Mouth-to-Stoma: For individuals with a tracheostomy or a surgical stoma in the neck for breathing, rescue breaths can be delivered directly into the stoma using the rescuer's mouth or a specialized device.

6-51. It is important to note that the technique and ratio of rescue breaths may vary depending on the age of the person receiving CPR (such as an adult, child, or infant) and the specific guidelines in the area or operation. Proper training in CPR techniques, including rescue breathing, is crucial to ensure accurate and effective administration of artificial ventilation during emergency situations.

6-52. It is important to follow the specific guidelines and protocols for rescue breathing based on the person's age, condition, and the resources available. Proper training and certification in CPR techniques are essential to gain proficiency in rescue breathing and to ensure the best possible outcomes during resuscitation efforts.
