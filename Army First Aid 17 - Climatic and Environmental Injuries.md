# Chapter 17: Climatic and Environmental Injuries

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Climatic Injuries

17-1. Climatic injuries, also known as weather-related injuries, occur when people's health is negatively affected by extreme weather conditions. These injuries can range from heat-related illnesses to cold-related conditions, and they are often a result of prolonged exposure to harsh environmental elements. Climate plays a significant role in determining the risks individuals face, with hot climates posing dangers of heat exhaustion and heatstroke, while cold climates can lead to frostbite and hypothermia. Understanding the signs, symptoms, and prevention strategies for these climatic injuries is essential in ensuring our well-being and staying safe in different weather conditions.

17-2. Specialized clothing and equipment (such as cold weather gear) for a specific environment should be obtained and used properly. For information on the prevention of heat and cold injuries, refer to TC 4-02.3, _Field Hygiene and Sanitation_ .

17-3. Personal factors can significantly influence health and well-being in a climatic environment. Some personal factors that play a role are:

- Physical Health and Fitness: A person's physical health and fitness levels affect how they cope with different climatic conditions. Individuals with good overall physical health, including cardiovascular fitness, strength, and endurance, are generally more resilient to extreme temperatures, humidity, and other climate-related challenges.

- Adaptability: Personal adaptability and the ability to adjust to changing climatic conditions can impact well-being. Some people are naturally more adaptable to different climates, while others may struggle with the challenges posed by extreme temperatures, high humidity, or other climatic factors.

- Sensitivity and Allergies: Some individuals may be more sensitive to specific climate-related factors, such as pollen, air pollution, or mold, which can trigger allergies or respiratory issues. Understanding personal sensitivities and taking necessary precautions can help maintain health and well-being in a climatic environment.

- Mental Resilience: Mental resilience plays a vital role in dealing with challenging climatic conditions. People with strong coping mechanisms, stress management skills, and the ability to maintain a positive mindset are better equipped to handle adverse weather conditions, such as extreme heatwaves or cold snaps.

- Knowledge and Awareness: Being well-informed about the climatic environment and understanding how it can impact health is crucial. Knowing the risks associated with specific climate-related factors, such as heatstroke or frostbite, and taking appropriate preventive measures can help protect health and well-being.

- Lifestyle Choices: Personal lifestyle choices, including clothing preferences, outdoor activities, and exposure to natural elements, can affect health in a climatic environment. Wearing appropriate clothing for protection against extreme temperatures, staying hydrated, and engaging in activities that are suitable for the climate, contribute to well-being.

- Pre-existing Health Conditions: Individuals with pre-existing health conditions may be more vulnerable to the effects of certain climatic conditions. For example, individuals with respiratory conditions like asthma may experience worsening symptoms in highly polluted or allergen-rich climates. Managing pre-existing health conditions effectively becomes crucial for maintaining wellbeing in a climatic environment.

- Access to Resources: Personal access to resources, such as reliable shelter, heating or cooling systems, and clean water, can impact health and well-being in different climates. Having access to appropriate resources to adapt to climatic conditions can significantly improve overall well-being.

17-4. Personal factors interact with other environmental and social factors, such as the availability of infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural norms. Understanding these personal factors and their

interactions can help individuals make informed choices and take the necessary steps to protect their health and well-being in a climatic environment.

### Warning

**Service members should use extreme caution when starting fad diets or taking over-the-counter herbal supplements. Death and severe injury may occur in individuals using dietary or herbal supplements without medical monitoring.**

**A trained health care provider should supervise weight loss and the use of weight loss supplements.**

## Heat Injuries

17-5. Heat injury prevention is of utmost importance in the military, where Service members face intense physical challenges and operate in diverse climatic conditions. When the body gets overheated, it can lead to serious health risks and impair a Service member’s ability to perform their duties effectively. Heat-related injuries, such as heat exhaustion and heat stroke, can be prevented through various measures, including wearing appropriate clothing, taking regular rest breaks, acclimatization and fluid replacement. By prioritizing heat injury prevention, Leaders ensure the well-being and operational readiness of their personnel, allowing them to fulfill their responsibilities and accomplish their missions with strength and resilience.

17-6. Exertional heat illness refers to a spectrum of heat-related conditions that occur when the body's core temperature rises excessively during physical activity or exercise. It is primarily caused by prolonged exertion in hot and humid environments, leading to an imbalance between heat production and heat dissipation. Exertional heat illness encompasses various conditions ranging from mild heat exhaustion to severe heat stroke, which is a medical emergency. Symptoms of heat injuries may include:

- Fatigue.

- Dizziness.

- Headache.

- Nausea.

- Excessive sweating.

- Rapid heartbeat.

- Confusion.

- Loss of consciousness in severe cases.

17-7. Prompt recognition and treatment of exertional heat illness are essential to prevent complications and potentially life-threatening consequences. Effective prevention strategies, such as acclimatization, appropriate clothing, fluid replacement, and regular rest breaks, are crucial in minimizing the risk of exertional heat illness. For more information on heat illness refer to TB MED 507 and TC 4-02.3.

17-8. While there is a range of adverse effects that can result from the body overheating, the two major kinds of heat illnesses that are referred to as heat casualties are heat exhaustion (can be mild or more severe) or heat stroke. Heat stroke is the most severe form of heat illness and possibly fatal.

**_Note._** For more information on heat illness prevention, refer to the Defense Centers for Public Health – Aberdeen website.

### Heat Exhaustion

17-9. Heat exhaustion is a heat-related condition that occurs when the body becomes overheated due to prolonged exposure to high temperatures and inadequate fluid replacement. It is considered a milder form of heat illness compared to heat stroke. Signs and symptoms of heat exhaustion may include:

- Heavy sweating.

- Weaknesses and fatigue.

- Dizziness or lightheadedness.

- Headache.

- Nausea or vomiting.

- Pale, cool, or clammy skin.

- Rapid and shallow breathing.

- Elevated heart rate.

- Muscle cramps.

- Feeling faint or fainting.

17-10. If a Service member is suspected to have heat exhaustion, immediate first aid treatment should be provided. The first aid steps to treat heat exhaustion are:

- Move the Service member to a cooler environment: Get them out of the heat and into a shaded or air-conditioned area.

- Encourage rest and rehydration: Have the Service member lie down and elevate their legs slightly. Provide them with cool water and or an electrolyte-containing sports drink to sip on. Avoid alcohol or caffeine.

- Cool the Service member's body: Loosen or remove any tight or unnecessary clothing. Apply a cool, wet cloth or towels to their skin, especially on the neck, face, and armpits. Use a fan or create air movement to aid in evaporation.

- Monitor their condition: Stay with the Service member and observe their symptoms. If they do not improve within 30 minutes or their condition worsens, seek medical assistance immediately.

17-11. Heat exhaustion can progress to heat stroke, a life-threatening condition. If the person's symptoms worsen, they become disoriented, confused, or lose consciousness, immediately call for medical assistance.

17-12. Prevention is key in avoiding heat exhaustion. Practice proper fluid replacement, especially when engaging in physical activity or spending time in hot environments. Wear lightweight and loose-fitting clothing, use sunscreen, and take regular breaks in cool areas to avoid prolonged exposure to heat.

17-13. Heat exhaustion is often preceded by heat cramps, muscle cramps of the arms, legs, or abdomen. Heat cramps and heat exhaustion often act as indicators. These conditions need to be identified and treated before they get to an extreme case of heat stroke. Catch these conditions early, as the casualty needs rest, water, shade, evaluation, and possible medical care.

### Heat Stroke

17-14. Heat stroke is a severe and potentially life-threatening illness characterized by a rapid increase in body temperature. It occurs when the body's cooling mechanisms fail to regulate internal heat, often because of prolonged exposure to high temperatures or physical exertion in hot environments. Heat stroke is a medical emergency that requires immediate intervention. The signs and symptoms of heat stroke may include:

- Altered mental state or confusion.

- Headache, dizziness, or fainting.

- Nausea or vomiting.

- Difficulty breathing.

- Hot, dry skin (lack of sweating).

- High body temperature (typically above 104°Fahrenheit or 40°Celsius).

- Rapid and strong heartbeat.

- Seizures.

17-15. If someone is suspected of having heat stroke, it is crucial to seek emergency medical help immediately. While waiting for medical professionals to arrive, follow these first aid steps:

- Move the person to a cooler location: Get them out of the heat and into a shaded or air-conditioned area as quickly as possible.

- Call for emergency assistance: Dial 911 or the appropriate local emergency number to request immediate medical help to include MEDEVAC.

- Cool the person rapidly: Reduce their body temperature as quickly as possible. You can immerse the person in a tub of cold water, use ice cooling sheets or apply cold water to their body using wet towels or sponges. Fan the person while misting them with cool water to enhance cooling.

- Monitor their vital signs: Keep a close eye on the person's breathing, pulse, and level of consciousness. If they stop breathing and you can no longer find a pulse, begin CPR if you are trained to do so.

17-16. It's important to note that heat stroke is a severe condition, and professional medical treatment is essential. Rapid cooling of the body is crucial in preventing potential organ damage and reducing the risk of complications.

17-17. To prevent heat stroke, it is important to avoid excessive physical exertion in hot environments, wear lightweight and breathable clothing, and take regular breaks in cool areas. Understanding the signs and symptoms of heat stroke and promptly seeking medical help can save lives.

**_Note._** The same person should observe the Service member during cooling and evacuation to spot symptom changes.

### Hyponatremia (water Intoxication)

17-18. Hyponatremia (water intoxication) is a condition characterized by abnormally low levels of sodium in the blood. Sodium plays a crucial role in maintaining the body's fluid balance and proper nerve and muscle function. When sodium levels become significantly diluted, usually due to excessive water intake or inadequate sodium intake, hyponatremia can occur. The signs and symptoms of hyponatremia can vary depending on the severity of the condition. Common manifestations include:

- Nausea and vomiting.

- Headache.

- Confusion or altered mental state.

- Fatigue and weakness.

- Seizures.

- Muscle cramps or spasms.

- Loss of appetite.

- Swelling in the hands, feet, or legs.

- Low blood pressure.

- Coma (in severe cases).

17-19. If someone is suspected to have hyponatremia, it is important to seek medical attention. In the meantime, you can provide the following first aid measures:

- Assess the person's condition: Check for any signs of severe symptoms such as seizures, loss of consciousness, or difficulty breathing. If any of these are present, call emergency services immediately.

- Move the person to a cool, shaded area: Ensure they are away from direct sunlight or heat sources.

- • Encourage fluid intake: If the person is conscious and able to swallow, give them small sips of a beverage containing electrolytes, such as a sports drink. This can help restore sodium levels.

- Do not give more water. Providing additional water will make the casualty more hyponatremic.

- If awake, allow the Service member to consume salty foods or snacks.

- Monitor the person's vital signs: Keep an eye on their breathing, heart rate, and level of consciousness. If their condition worsens, be prepared to administer CPR if necessary.

17-20. Hyponatremia can be mistaken for heat stroke, and the treatment is very different. Hyponatremia is a medical emergency that requires professional diagnosis and treatment. It is important to address the underlying cause and restore sodium levels under the guidance of a health care professional.

**_Note._** This condition most often occurs during initial entry training; however, it may occur anytime when overhydration is encountered.

## Cold Weather Injuries

17-21. Preventing cold weather injuries is a vital aspect of military training and operations in cold climates. When Service members face extreme cold conditions, they are at risk of various cold weather injuries, such as frostbite and hypothermia, which can significantly impact their ability to fulfill their duties effectively. Cold weather injury prevention measures are essential to ensure the well-being and operational readiness of military personnel. By implementing strategies such as proper clothing layering, maintaining adequate hydration, practicing cold weather survival techniques, and promoting awareness of cold weather risks, the military prioritizes the safety and health of its Service members. By emphasizing cold weather injury prevention, the military ensures that its personnel can endure the challenging cold environments and accomplish their missions with strength, resilience, and readiness.

17-22. Cold weather injuries encompass various conditions that occur due to exposure to extreme cold temperatures. The main cold weather injuries are:

- Frostbite.

- Hypothermia.

- Trench Foot.

- Nonfreezing injuries (such as chilblains and snow blindness).

17-23. These injuries are often caused by a combination of factors. Prolonged exposure to cold temperatures, inadequate protection against the cold, wet clothing, wind chill. Other contributing factors include poor nutrition, dehydration, fatigue, and certain medical conditions can increase the susceptibility to cold weather injuries. Proper preparation, adequate clothing, layering, regular breaks to warm up, maintain hydration, and practicing good cold weather safety measures can help reduce the risk of cold weather injuries. For more information on heat illness prevention, refer to the Defense Centers for Public Health–Aberdeen (Cold Weather Casualties and Injuries) website. For additional information on cold weather injuries refer to TC 4- 02.3.

### Frostbite

17-24. Frostbite is a cold weather injury that occurs when tissues freeze due to prolonged exposure to extreme cold temperatures. It typically affects extremities, such as the fingers, toes, ears, and nose, but can also occur in other exposed areas of the body. The signs and symptoms of frostbite may vary depending on the severity, and they can include:

- Cold, pale, or bluish skin.

- Numbness or a loss of sensation in the affected area.

- Tingling or a “pins and needles” sensation.

- Hard or waxy appearance of the skin.

- Swelling or blistering (in severe cases).

- Pain or a burning sensation (as the affected area rewarms).

- Blackened or gangrenous tissue (in severe, untreated cases).

17-25. If someone is suspected of having frostbite, it is important to provide immediate first aid and seek medical attention. Take the following first aid steps:

- Move to a warm environment: Get the person out of the cold and into a warm area as soon as possible to prevent further damage.

- Remove wet or constrictive clothing: Take off any wet clothing or accessories that could contribute to heat loss or restrict blood flow to the affected area.

- Gradually rewarm the affected area: Immerse the frostbit area in warm (not hot) water or use warm compresses. Avoid using direct heat sources like fires or heating pads, as the person may not be able to sense heat properly and could burn the skin.

- Avoid rubbing or massaging the area: Vigorous rubbing can further damage the tissues. Let the affected area rewarm naturally.

- Protect and elevate the affected area: Once the frostbitten area has thawed, cover it with a sterile dressing or clean cloth. Keep the area elevated to reduce swelling, if possible.

- Offer pain relief if needed: Over-the-counter pain relievers like ibuprofen can help alleviate pain and reduce inflammation. However, consult a health care professional before administering any medication.

- Seek medical attention: Frostbite requires medical evaluation and treatment. Even if the frostbite appears mild, professional medical care is essential to assess the extent of the injury and prevent complications.

17-26. Frostbite can cause long-term damage, including tissue loss and nerve damage. Prompt and appropriate treatment is crucial for minimizing complications and maximizing recovery.

17-27. Frostbite accounts for the largest number of cold weather injuries each year and occurs when tissue is exposed to temperatures that are usually below 32°Fahrenheit depending upon wind chill factor, length of exposure, and adequacy of protection.

17-28. Frostbite can occur suddenly due to contact with cold metal or super-cooled liquids such as alcohol, fuel, or antifreeze or can develop over time due to prolonged cold exposure. Frostbite is most common in exposed skin such as the nose, ears, cheeks, but can also occur in the hands and feet due to reduced blood flow.

**_Note._** The onset is signaled by a sudden blanching of the skin of the nose, ears, cheeks, fingers, or toes followed by a momentary tingling sensation. Frostbite is indicated when the face, hands, or feet stop hurting.

### Hypothermia

17-29. Hypothermia is a concern regarding TCCC and the body’s ability to form clots is impaired. This condition can cause a tendency toward prolonged or excessive bleeding, which may occur spontaneously or following an injury. Hypothermia is a potentially life-threatening condition that occurs when the body's core temperature drops significantly below normal.

17-30. The risks differ with environmental hypothermia. Victims of moderate to severe environmental hypothermia are known to have a risk of an irregular heartbeat associated with “afterdrop.” Afterdrop is the continued fall of deep body temperatures during rewarming after hypothermia. Afterdrop is thought to endanger the heart by further cooling from cold blood presumed to be returning to the body’s center or core.

17-31. Environmental hypothermic casualties should be monitored and gently handled as afterdrop is not always observed. The goals for overall casualty treatment (such as hemorrhage control and resuscitation in TCCC versus warming measures along with gentle handling in environmental hypothermia) should be understood, trained, and rehearsed. For further discussion on hypothermia see Chapter 8.

### Trench Foot

17-32. Trench foot, also known as immersion foot, is a non-freezing cold injury that occurs when the feet are exposed to prolonged damp and cold conditions. It can lead to tissue damage and infection. Contributing factors include wet and cold environments, inadequate foot hygiene, prolonged periods of immobility, and improper footwear.

17-33. Trench foot is often associated with Soldiers during World War I who endured long periods in wet trenches. Trench foot can occur in any situation where the feet are exposed to excessive moisture and cold temperatures. The signs and symptoms of trench foot include:

- Redness and swelling of the feet.

- Numbness or tingling sensation.

- Itching or a burning sensation.

- Pain or discomfort.

- Blisters or open sores.

- Coldness and paleness of the skin.

- Foul odor.

17-34. If someone is suspected to have trench feet, the following first aid steps can be taken:

- Move them to a warm and dry environment: Get the person out of wet and cold conditions as soon as possible to prevent further damage.

- Remove wet footwear and socks: Take off wet footwear and socks to allow the feet to dry. Avoid rubbing or massaging the feet, as it can further damage the skin.

- Elevate the feet: Raise the feet slightly to help reduce swelling and improve circulation.

- Clean and dry the feet: Gently wash the feet with warm water (not hot) and mild soap. Pat the feet dry with a clean towel, avoiding excessive friction.

- Apply clean, dry socks: Once the feet are dry, put on clean, dry socks to help insulate and protect the feet. Avoid using tight or constrictive socks.

- Avoid direct heat sources: Do not use direct heat sources like fires, heaters, or heating pads to warm the feet. The affected feet may be numb, and exposure to high heat can cause burns.

- Seek medical attention: It is important to seek medical evaluation and treatment for trench feet. Health care professionals can provide appropriate care, clean, and dress any blisters or open sores, and monitor any signs of infection.

17-35. Prevention is key in avoiding trench feet. Keep the feet dry, wear appropriate footwear that is waterproof and well-insulated, change wet socks and shoes promptly, and take breaks to allow the feet to dry and warm up. Proper foot hygiene and regular inspection of the feet can help identify any early signs of trench foot and prevent its progression.

### Nonfreezing Injuries

17-36. Exposure to temperatures from 32°Fahrenheit to 60°Fahrenheit may cause nonfreezing cold injuries of skin, fingers, toes, ears, and facial parts. Exposure of skin to cold metal; super cold fuel, petroleum, oil, and lubricants; wind chill; and the wear of tight, circulation-restricting clothing (particularly boots).

17-37. Riding in open vehicles, exposure to propeller or rotor-generated wind, running or skiing, and altitude exposure where there is little tree cover can all contribute to greater wind chill.

17-38. Nonfreezing cold injuries are chilblain and snow blindness. Trench foot occurs when tissues are exposed to temperatures from 32°Fahrenheit to 60°Fahrenheit for prolonged periods of time (>12 hours), whereas chilblains, which is a more superficial injury, can occur after repeated prolonged exposure of bare skin to low temperatures from 60°Fahrenheit down to 32°Fahrenheit. Snow blindness is caused by unprotected exposure to UV rays. Snow blindness can be prevented using appropriate eye protection (sunglasses). Snow blindness can be painful as it gets closer to three to five hours later.

#### Chilblains

17-39. Chilblains, also known as pernio, is a nonfreezing cold weather injury that can occur after one to five hours in cold, wet conditions at temperatures from about 50°Fahrenheit down to 32°Fahrenheit. Chilblains are a localized inflammatory response of the skin that occurs due to prolonged exposure to cold and damp conditions.

17-40. The most affected areas are the fingernail-side of the fingers, but the ears, face, and other exposed skin are also areas of occurrence. There are no lasting effects from chilblain. They are more common in colder climates and during the winter season. The signs and symptoms of chilblains include:

- Redness and inflammation of the affected area.

- Itching or a burning sensation.

- Swelling and tenderness.

- Skin discoloration (bluish or purplish appearance).

- Formation of small, fluid-filled blisters.

- Dry or cracked skin.

- Pain or discomfort, especially when exposed to warmth after being cold.

17-41. If someone is experiencing chilblains, the following first aid procedures can be performed:

- Move to a warm environment: Get the person indoors or to a warmer area to prevent further exposure to cold temperatures.

- Gradually rewarm the affected areas: Avoid sudden temperature changes or direct heat sources like heaters or fires, as this can worsen the condition. Instead, gently warm the affected areas by placing them near warm body parts (such as under the armpits) or by using warm water. The water should be comfortably warm, not hot.

- Protect and cover the affected areas: Once the affected areas have been warmed, keep them protected from further exposure to cold. Cover them with warm clothing, gloves, or socks to help insulate and maintain a stable temperature.

- Avoid scratching or rubbing the affected areas: Itching can be intense, but scratching or rubbing can further damage the skin and increase the risk of infection. Encourage the person to resist the urge to scratch.

- Apply soothing lotions or creams: If recommended by a health care professional, apply mild lotions or creams to alleviate itching and provide relief. Avoid using excessive amounts or substances that may irritate the skin.

- Seek medical advice if needed: If symptoms worsen, if blisters or open sores develop, or if signs of infection (such as increased redness, swelling, or discharge) are present, it is important to seek medical attention for proper evaluation and treatment.

17-42. Preventing chilblains is essential. It can be achieved by keeping the extremities warm and dry, wearing appropriate protective clothing and footwear, avoiding prolonged exposure to cold and damp environments, and gradually acclimating the body to temperature changes. Regularly checking the skin for any signs of chilblains can help identify and address the condition early on.

#### Snow Blindness

17-43. Snow blindness, also known as photokeratitis or UV keratitis, is a condition that occurs when the eyes are exposed to excessive UV radiation, typically from sunlight reflected off snow or ice. It can also happen when exposed to other intense light sources, such as welding arcs or tanning beds. Snow blindness is essentially a temporary sunburn of the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye. The signs and symptoms of snow blindness include:

   - Eye pain or a sensation of grittiness.

   - Redness and inflammation of the eyes.

   - Watery eyes and excessive tearing.

   - Blurred vision or sensitivity to light.

   - Feeling as if there is something in the eye.

   - Headache.

   - Temporary vision loss in severe cases.

- 17-44. If someone is experiencing snow blindness, the following first aid treatment can be administered:

   - Move to a dark and sheltered area: Get the person indoors or to a shaded area to protect the eyes from further exposure to bright light.

   - Rest the eyes: Keep the eyes closed or minimize eye movements to reduce discomfort and prevent further irritation.

   - Avoid rubbing the eyes: Rubbing the eyes can exacerbate the condition and potentially cause additional damage.

   - Apply cold compresses: Place a cold, damp cloth or cool pack over closed eyes to help reduce inflammation and alleviate pain. Avoid using ice directly on the skin.

   - Use artificial tears: Over-the-counter artificial tear drops can be used to provide relief and moisture to the eyes. Follow the instructions on the packaging or consult a health care professional for appropriate usage.

   - Wear protective eyewear: If the person needs to be in a bright environment, ensure they wear sunglasses that offer 100 percent UV protection. Wrap-around sunglasses or goggles with side shields provide the best protection.

   - Seek medical attention: It is advisable to seek medical advice if the symptoms persist, worsen, or if vision loss occurs. A health care professional can examine the eyes and provide appropriate treatment and guidance.

17-45. Prevention is crucial in avoiding snow blindness. To protect the eyes from excessive UV radiation, wear sunglasses or goggles that provide 100 percent UV protection, especially in snowy or high-altitude environments. Additionally, using wide-brimmed hats or visors can help shield the eyes from indirect sunlight.

## Mountainous Terrain Injuries

17-46. Approximately 27 percent of the earth’s land surface is considered mountainous terrain. These areas are characterized by rugged landscapes, elevated peaks, and varying degrees of slope. Mountains play a crucial role in shaping the Earth’s geography, influencing climate patterns, and providing habitat for diverse ecosystems.

17-47. Service members operating in mountainous terrain should be well-prepared, have the necessary skills, and carry a first aid kit to address potential injuries and emergencies. Additionally, understanding the terrain, weather conditions, and having proper equipment can contribute to safer operations in mountainous terrain.

17-48. Mountainous terrain poses various challenges and risks, and injuries can range from minor to severe. Common injuries in mountainous terrain include:

- Sprains and Strains. Uneven and rocky surfaces can lead to twisted ankles or muscle strains.

- Fractures. Falls or accidents may result in broken bones, particularly in extremities.

- Cuts and Abrasions. Sharp rocks and uneven surfaces can cause cuts and scrapes.

- Altitude Sickness. At higher elevations, individuals may experience symptoms like headache, nausea, and dizziness due to reduced oxygen levels.

- Hypothermia. Cold temperatures, especially at higher altitudes, can lead to a drop in body temperature.

- Dehydration. Strenuous activity, combined with lower humidity and potential difficulty accessing water sources, can lead to dehydration.

- Exhaustion. Hiking or climbing in mountainous terrain can be physically demanding, leading to fatigue and exhaustion.

- Frostbite. Exposure to cold conditions, especially at high altitudes, can result in frostbite, where skin and tissues freeze.

- Avalanche and Rockfall Injuries. In certain mountainous regions, avalanches or rockfalls can pose significant dangers, causing trauma and injuries.

- Wildlife Encounters. Depending on the location, encounters with wildlife can lead to injuries.

17-49. Many of the above injury types and treatments have been discussed throughout this publication, however operating in mountainous terrain can be challenging, but it's crucial to be aware of the potential risks, including altered mental status, high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE), and high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE).

### Altitude Emergencies

17-50. Changes in consciousness, confusion, or disorientation can occur due to factors like altitude sickness, dehydration, or exhaustion. Recognizing and responding to an altered mental status promptly is essential for ensuring the well-being of Service members. Some general first aid measures for Service members experiencing altered mental status in mountainous terrain include:

- Move to Lower Altitude: If the altered mental status is due to altitude sickness, move the Service member to a lower altitude immediately. Descending to lower elevations allows the body to acclimatize and receive more oxygen, potentially alleviating symptoms.

- Rest and Hydration: Ensure the Service member rests and stays hydrated. Dehydration and fatigue can contribute to an altered mental status, so providing water and allowing for adequate rest are important.

- Monitor Breathing: Keep a close eye on the Service member's breathing. Shallow or irregular breathing may indicate respiratory distress. If breathing difficulties persist, promptly seek medical assistance.

- Check for Signs of Hypoxia: Assess for signs of hypoxia, which occurs when the body doesn't receive enough oxygen. Look for bluish discoloration of the lips or fingertips. If observed, it may indicate a more severe oxygen deficiency, requiring immediate medical attention.

- Keep Warm: If the altered mental status is due to cold temperatures, provide warmth by using blankets, jackets, or other insulating materials. Preventing hypothermia is crucial.

- Monitor for Improvement or Worsening: Keep a close watch on the Service members’ condition. If there is no improvement or if the symptoms worsen, seek medical assistance as soon as possible.

- Administer Oxygen (if available): If you have access to supplemental oxygen and the person is experiencing severe symptoms, administering oxygen can be beneficial. However, this may require specific training and equipment.

- Evacuation if Necessary: In severe cases or if the person's condition doesn't improve, consider evacuating them to a medical facility at a lower altitude. This may involve calling for professional rescue or using available means of transportation.

17-51. First aid of an altered mental status is a temporary measure, and medical assistance should be sought for any significant or worsening symptoms. If in doubt or if the altered mental status persists, it is essential to prioritize the Service member's safety and seek timely medical help.

### High-altitude Pulmonary Edema

17-52. High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema is a serious condition that can occur at higher elevations, typically above 2,500 meters (8,200 feet). It involves the accumulation of fluid fluids in the lungs and can be lifethreatening. Some general first aid measures for Service members experiencing HAPE in mountainous terrain include:

- Immediate Descent. The most critical first aid measure for HAPE is an immediate descent to lower altitudes. Every effort should be made to get the affected individual to a lower elevation as quickly as possible, as this is the most effective way to alleviate symptoms.

- Rest and Oxygen. Ensure the person rests and provide supplemental oxygen if available. Oxygen therapy can help improve oxygen levels in the blood and alleviate breathing difficulties.

- Keep Warm. Maintain the person's warmth by using blankets and insulating layers. Cold temperatures can exacerbate symptoms, so preventing hypothermia is important.

- Stay Hydrated. Encourage the individual to drink fluids to stay hydrated. Dehydration can worsen the condition, so maintaining adequate fluid intake is essential.

- Monitor Breathing. Keep a close watch on the person's breathing. If there are signs of severe respiratory distress, such as blue lips or fingertips, it may indicate a critical situation, and professional medical help should be sought immediately.

- Administer Medications (if available). In some cases, medications such as nifedipine or sildenafil may be used to help manage HAPE symptoms. The medical professional should determine the availability and use of these medications.

- Evacuation. Seek evacuation to the MTF at a lower altitude as soon as possible. Evacuation may involve contacting medical personnel and requesting evacuation to a lower elevation where more definitive medical care can be provided.

17-53. HAPE is a medical emergency, and prompt action is crucial. While these first aid measures can provide initial assistance, professional medical help should be sought without delay. If you suspect someone has HAPE, it's essential to prioritize their safety and act swiftly to get them to a lower altitude and seek immediate medical attention.

### High-altitude Cerebral Edema

17-54. High-Altitude Cerebral Edema is a severe and life-threatening condition that can occur at high elevations, typically above 3,000 meters (9,800 feet). It involves swelling of the brain and can lead to neurological symptoms. Some general first aid measures for Service members experiencing HACE in mountainous terrain include:

- Immediate Descent. The most critical first aid measure for HACE is an immediate and rapid descent to lower altitudes. Descending to a lower elevation is essential to reduce the impact of high altitude on the brain.

- Rest and Oxygen. Ensure the affected person rests and provide supplemental oxygen if available. Oxygen therapy can help improve oxygen levels in the blood and alleviate neurological symptoms.

- Keep Warm. Maintain the person's warmth using blankets and insulating layers. Cold temperatures can worsen symptoms, so preventing hypothermia is important.

- Hydration. Encourage the individual to drink fluids to stay hydrated. Dehydration can exacerbate the condition, so maintaining adequate fluid intake is crucial.

- Administer Medications (if available). In some cases, medications such as dexamethasone may be used to help manage HACE symptoms. Medical professionals should determine the use of medications, and access to such medications may be limited in remote mountainous areas.

- Monitor Neurological Symptoms. Keep a close watch on the person's neurological symptoms. Signs of severe neurological impairment, such as confusion, loss of coordination, or changes in consciousness, require urgent medical attention.

- Evacuation. Seek evacuation to an MTF at a lower altitude as quickly as possible. HACE is a medical emergency, and professional medical help should be sought without delay.

- Reassure and calm. Keep the affected individual calm and reassure them. Stress and anxiety can exacerbate symptoms, so a supportive and calming environment is beneficial.

17-55. HACE is a critical condition, and time is of the essence. While these first aid measures can provide initial assistance, medical assistance should be sought urgently. If HACE is suspected in a Service member, prioritize their safety by facilitating a rapid descent and seeking immediate medical attention.
