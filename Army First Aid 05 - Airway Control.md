# Chapter 5: Airway Control

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Airway Control

5-1. After massive bleeding is controlled, the responder can move on to airway control, the first “A” in the MARCH-PAWS sequence. The casualty must have an open (unobstructed) airway. If the casualty is awake and talking, you can assume the casualty’s airway is open. However, the casualty may still have difficulty breathing. If the casualty is unconscious, their airway must be inspected for any obvious blockages.

5-2. Humans must have oxygen to live. Through the breathing process, the lungs draw oxygen from the air and put it into the blood. The heart pumps the blood through the body to be used by the cells that require a constant supply of oxygen. Some cells are more dependent on a constant supply of oxygen than others. For example, cells of the brain may die within five minutes without oxygen. The brain, heart and lungs are very intolerant to lack of oxygen. The brain is considered one of the organs that is least tolerant to oxygen deprivation. Once these cells die, they are lost forever and do not regenerate. This could result in permanent brain damage, paralysis, or death.

5-3. Airway obstruction on the battlefield is often due to facial trauma. If the casualty is breathing on their own but unconscious or semi-conscious, and there is no airway obstruction, further airway management is achieved through a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA). First responders must assess casualties with facial trauma when contraindications exist to NPA use (such as basilar skull fractures, disruption of the midface, nasopharynx, or roof of the mouth).

5-4. Unconscious casualties can also lose their airway as the muscles of their tongue may have relaxed. This causes the tongue to block the airway by sliding to the back of the mouth and covering the opening to the windpipe. The signs and symptoms of airway obstruction include:

- Casualty is in distress and indicating that they cannot breathe properly.

- Casualty making snoring or gurgling sounds.

- Visible blood or foreign objects are present in the airway.

- Severe trauma to the face is observed.

### Respiration

5-5. Respiration is the process where we breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide. The efficiency of this process can vary depending on someone's age and physical health. Generally, adults breathe 12 to 20 times a minute. But children and infants have different rates. For instance, children aged one to 10 years take 15 to 30 breaths per minute. Infants who are six to 12 months old breath faster, at a rate of 25 to 50 times per minute. Meanwhile, the youngest infants, those aged zero to five months, have a rate of 25 to 40 breaths per minute.

### Anatomy and Physiology of the Respiratory System

5-6. The respiratory system consists of the airway, lungs, and rib cage. The airway consists of the nose, mouth, throat, voice box, and windpipe. Air passes to and from the lungs through the canal. The lungs are two elastic organs made up of thousands of tiny air spaces or sacs and covered by an airtight membrane. The bronchial tree is a part of the lungs. The rib cage is the structure that joins the spine in the back and the breastbone in front, which protects the heart and lungs. The top part of the rib cage is closed by the structure of the neck, and the bottom part is separated from the abdominal cavity by a large dome-shaped muscle called the diaphragm (figure 5-1 on page 60).

**Figure 5-1. Airway, lungs, and the rib cage**

5-7. The diaphragm and rib muscles, which are under the control of the respiratory center in the brain, automatically contract and relax. Inspiration increases and expiration decreases the size of the rib cage. When the rib cage increases and then decreases, the air pressure in the lungs is first less and then more than the atmospheric pressure, thus causing the air to rush into and out of the lungs to equalize the pressure.

## Equipment Used for Airway Control

5-8. The CoTCCC-recommended NPA with lubricant is a nonsterile, rubber tube-shaped device that is inserted into the nostril of a casualty. It acts as a wedge to keep the airway open by keeping the tongue from falling back into the space behind the mouth leading to the windpipe. Only an NPA, size 32 French, is found within the standard issued JFAKs. Sizing the NPA before insertion is not necessary but is important to minimize nasopharyngeal trauma or elicit the casualty’s gag reflex. The lubricant is a water-based substance that assists in inserting the device into the nose. Use of an NPA is a Tier 2 skill.

### Caution

Do NOT use blood or other body fluids to lubricate the NPA.

## Tier 1 Skills for Airway Control

5-9. Tier 1 TCCC skills for airway control encompass techniques and procedures designed to address various challenges, including airway obstructions and respiratory distress. Proficiency in these skills is imperative for Service members operating in high-stakes, dynamic situations, and where swift and precise intervention can mean the difference between life, and death on the battlefield.

### Safely Position the Casualty to Open the Airway

5-10. Check for responsiveness (figure 5-2 step A on page 61) and establish whether the casualty is conscious by gently shaking them and asking, “Are you OK?” Call for help (figure 5-2 step B on page 61) and then position the unconscious casualty so that they are lying in the recovery position, head tilted back; chin away from chest (figure 5-2 step C on page 61) by —

- Straightening the casualty’s legs.

- Moving the casualty’s arm that is nearest to you. Move it so that it is straight and above their head. Repeat the procedure for the other arm placing it by their side.

- Kneeling beside the casualty with your knees near their shoulders (leave space to roll their body).

- • Placing one hand behind their head and neck for support. On your other hand, grasp the casualty under their arm (figure 5-2 step C on page 61).

- Rolling the casualty towards you using a steady, even pull. The casualty’s head and neck should stay in line with their back.

5-11. The recovery position allows blood and mucus to drain out of the casualty’s nose and mouth and not drain back into the airway. However, if a neck injury is suspected and the jaw-thrust technique will be used, kneel at the casualty’s head, looking towards their feet.

### Warning

**If the casualty is lying on their chest (prone position), cautiously roll the casualty as a unit so that their body does not twist. The twisting may further complicate a back, neck, or spinal injury.**

76B **AIRWAY OBSTRUCTIONS**

5-12. Oxygen flows to and from the lungs requires an unobstructed airway, yet obstructions can arise due to various factors:

- Unconsciousness may lead to the tongue falling back into the throat, obstructing the airway if not properly managed.

- Foreign objects, particularly food, may become lodged in the throat during eating, especially when large pieces are swallowed without proper chewing, exacerbated by factors like alcohol consumption or slipping dentures.

- Regurgitation of stomach contents or the formation of blood clots due to head and facial injuries can further contribute to airway blockage.

- Ensure correct positioning and maintenance of the airway in injured or unconscious casualties to mitigate these risks.

5-13. Upper airway obstruction, a potentially life-threatening emergency, can occur as either a partial or complete blockage. The severity and nature of the obstruction underscore the importance of rapid recognition and immediate intervention to prevent respiratory compromise. Identifying the signs early and responding effectively are crucial in maintaining the airway and supporting breathing, which are vital for the casualty's survival. This situation demands prompt and decisive action to alleviate the obstruction and ensure an open airway.

#### Partial Airway Obstruction

5-14. The casualty may still have an air exchange. A good air exchange means that the casualty can cough forcefully, though they may be wheezing between coughs. The rescuer should not interfere and should encourage the casualty to cough up the object obstructing their airway on their own. Weak coughing with a high-pitched noise between coughs may indicate a poor air exchange. You should assist the casualty and treat them as though they had a complete obstruction.

#### Complete Airway Obstruction

5-15. A complete obstruction (no air exchange) is indicated if the casualty cannot speak, breathe, or cough at all. They may be clutching their neck and moving erratically. In an unconscious casualty, a complete obstruction is also indicated if after opening their airway you cannot ventilate them. A casualty, experiencing a partial foreign body airway obstruction, with poor air exchange, or a complete airway obstruction will not survive without medical intervention. Both conditions are treated the same.

### Opening the Airway of an Unconscious or Not Breathing Casualty

5-16. The tongue is the single most common cause of an airway obstruction (see figure 5-3). In most cases, simply using the head-tilt/chin-lift or jaw thrust technique can open the airway. This action pulls the tongue away from the air passage in the throat (see figure 5-4).

**Figure 5-3. Airway, blocked**

**Figure 5-4. Airway, opened**

5-17. Call for help and then position the casualty. Move (roll) the casualty onto their back as described in paragraph 5-10 on page 60.

5-18. If a foreign body is seen in the airway, an attempt to remove it should be made. Do not put your fingers in the mouth to try to find or remove any unseen objects that might be causing an obstruction as this may push the object further into the airway. Open the airway using the head-tilt/chin-lift method.

**_Note._** If foreign material or vomitus is visible in the mouth, it should be removed as quickly as possible.

### Important!

**Remove any visible objects, but do not perform a blind finger sweep.**

### Caution

The head-tilt/chin-lift technique is an important procedure in opening the airway; however, use extreme care because excess force in performing this maneuver may cause further spinal injury.

In a casualty with a suspected neck injury or severe head trauma, the safest approach to opening the airway is the jaw-thrust technique because in most cases, it can be accomplished without extending the neck.

#### Head-Tilt/Chin-Lift Method

5-19. Place one hand on the casualty’s forehead and apply firm, backward pressure with the palm to tilt the head back. Place the fingertips of the other hand under the bony part of the lower jaw and lift, bringing the chin forward. The thumb should not be used to lift the chin (see figure 5-5).

**_Note._** The fingers should not press deeply into the soft tissue under the chin because the airway may be obstructed.

**Figure 5-5. Head-tilt/chin-lift technique of opening the airway**

#### Jaw-Thrust Method

5-20. The jaw-thrust method may be accomplished by the rescuer grasping the angles of the casualty’s lower jaw and lifting with both hands, one on each side, displacing the jaw forward and up (see figure 5-6). The rescuer’s elbows should rest on the surface on which the casualty is lying. If the lips close, the lower lip can be retracted with the thumb. If mouth-to-mouth breathing is necessary, close the nostrils by placing your cheek tightly against them. The head should be carefully supported without tilting it backwards or turning it from side-to-side. If this is unsuccessful, the head should be tilted back slightly. The jaw-thrust is the safest first approach to opening the airway of a casualty who has a suspected neck injury because in most cases it can be accomplished without extending the neck.

**Figure 5-6. Jaw-thrust technique of opening the airway**

**_Note._** If the casualty’s lips are still closed after the jaw has been moved forward, use your thumbs to retract the lower lip, and allow air to enter the casualty’s mouth.

5-21. The jaw-thrust method, commonly emphasized in training, can sometimes serve as a distraction in the field. Neck injuries in trauma cases are typically associated with dramatic incidents such as falls and motor vehicle accidents. Most neck injuries occur upon impact, and the extent of injury is usually immediate and unchangeable at the scene. If neck trauma is present, it is often necessary to perform a jaw-thrust maneuver to secure an airway and maintain that position until additional personnel arrive. This intervention allows the responder to proceed with the MARCH sequence.

### Managing the Airway

5-22. If the casualty is breathing independently yet is unconscious or semiconscious, and there is no airway obstruction, managing the airway is best accomplished using an NPA. An NPA is suitable for both conscious and unconscious individuals and is effective in helping to open or maintain an open airway. This method ensures that the airway remains clear, facilitating easier breathing and potentially preventing further complications.

5-23. Casualties with severe facial trauma can often protect their own airways by sitting up and leaning forward. Assisting a conscious casualty by helping them assume any comfortable sitting-up position that allows them to breathe easily. If the casualty is unconscious but breathing, the casualty should be placed in the recovery position (figure 5-7 on page 65).

**Figure 5-7. Unconscious Casualty Recovery Position**

5-24. The recovery position allows blood and mucus to drain out of the casualty’s nose and mouth and not drain back into the airway. The recovery position also helps to protect against inhaling vomit if the casualty throws up. If a casualty can breathe on their own, let them assume the best position (position of comfort) that allows them to breathe, including sitting up.

## Tier 2 Skills for Airway Control

5-25. The primary objective of Tier 2 airway control skills is to confirm the initiation of Tier 1 skills, with the addition of Tier 2 insertion of a nasopharyngeal airway and monitor the casualty for any signs of airway compromise, as outlined in paragraph 5-3. If the casualty shows signs of not breathing spontaneously, it's imperative to promptly notify medical personnel. Medical personnel may then require assistance from the CLS in administering breathing assistance using a bag valve mask (BVM) device, as depicted in figure 5-8. In instances where respirations are observed to be diminished, providing ventilation support with a BVM becomes necessary, and the CLS may be requested to assist in its usage by medical personnel.

**Figure 5-8. Bag valve mask**

5-26. Casualties requiring ongoing BVM support due to lack of spontaneous breathing effort, if not just administered a sedating pain medication, might indicate an injury or condition warranting earlier or immediate evacuation (such as severe traumatic brain injury or TBI). If definitive treatment or evacuation is not available, consider triaging the casualty to expectant if resources are limited or better applied to more likely survivable casualties.

5-27. Ventilations can be performed alone or with two people working together. The mask is held using the “E-C” holding technique (figure 5-9 on page 66). The mask is sealed over the casualty’s mouth so that air does not escape (figure 5-10 on page 66). The mask is then squeezed firmly for one to two seconds and five to six seconds apart. The Deployed Medicine website has an instruction video on the proper application of the one and two-person BVM.

## Expedient Techniques for Airway Control

5-28. Expedient techniques for airway control are critically important in TCCC due to the immediate threat posed by compromised airways in combat situations. Injured Service members may experience airway obstructions, respiratory distress, or loss of consciousness, which can rapidly progress to life-threatening situations. Expedient airway control techniques, such as the head tilt/chin lift, jaw thrust maneuver and use of NPAs may enable prompt restoration of adequate breathing and oxygenation. These techniques can be quickly implemented by trained personnel, ensuring timely intervention even in chaotic and high-stress environments. By swiftly addressing airway compromises, through the use of expedient techniques in TCCC play a vital role in preserving life, preventing further complications, and maximizing the chances of survival for injured military personnel on the battlefield.

### Opening the Obstructed Airway – Conscious Casualty

5-29. Clearing an airway obstruction in a conscious casualty can be managed while they are either standing or sitting. However, it's critical to recognize that an obstructed airway can lead to oxygen deficiency in the brain, resulting in respiratory arrest, unconsciousness and, if not promptly corrected, death.

5-30. To assess and manage airway obstruction, start by asking the casualty if they can speak or if they are choking. Additionally, check for the universal choking sign (see figure 5-11 on page 67). If the casualty can speak or cough effectively, encourage them to continue coughing. This indicates that they still have good air exchange, and you should not interfere with their efforts to expel the obstruction.

**Figure 5-11. The universal sign for choking**

5-31. However, if you notice high-pitched sounds when the casualty breathes or coughs, indicating poor air exchange, or if the casualty is unable to breathe, immediate action is required. Call for help and prepare to perform manual thrusts to clear the obstruction. Use abdominal thrusts, also known as the Heimlich maneuver, by positioning your hands between the waist and the rib cage and delivering quick upward thrusts. For individuals in advanced stages of pregnancy, those who are markedly obese, or if there is a significant abdominal wound, use chest thrust instead. This involves placing your hands in the center of the breastbone and pushing sharply inward and upward.

5-32. Quick action is essential in these situations to restore breathing and prevent further complications, including death. Always ensure to assess the situation carefully and apply the appropriate technique based on the casualty's condition

#### Apply Abdominal Thrusts

5-33. Prior to applying abdominal thrust, first responders should administer five back blows. Abdominal thrust can be accomplished by using the following procedures:

- Stand behind the casualty and wrap your arms around their waist.

- Make a fist with one hand and grasp it with the other. The thumb side of your fist should be against the casualty’s abdomen, in the midline and slightly above the casualty’s navel, but well below the tip of the breastbone (figure 5-12 on page 68).

- Press the fists into the abdomen with a quick backward and upward thrust (figure 5-13 on page 68).

**Figure 5-12. Anatomical view of the abdominal thrust procedure**

**Figure 5-13. Profile view of the abdominal thrust**

- Each thrust should be a separate and distinct movement.

- • Continue performing abdominal thrusts until the obstruction is expelled or the casualty becomes unresponsive.

- If the casualty becomes unresponsive, call for help as you proceed with the steps to open the airway.

#### Apply Chest Thrusts

5-34. An alternate technique to the abdominal thrust is the chest thrust. This technique is useful when the casualty has an abdominal wound, is pregnant, or when the casualty is so large that you cannot wrap your arms around the abdomen. To apply chest thrusts with casualty sitting or standing:

- Stand behind the casualty and wrap your arms around their chest with your arms under their armpits.

- • Make a fist with one hand and place the thumb side of the fist in the middle of the breastbone (take care to avoid the tip of the breastbone and the margins of the ribs).

- Grasp the fist with the other hand and exert thrusts (figure 5-14 on page 69).

**Figure 5-14. Profile view of the chest thrust**

- Each thrust should be delivered slowly, distinctly, and with the intent of relieving the obstruction.

- Perform chest thrusts until the obstruction is expelled or the casualty becomes unresponsive.

- • If the casualty becomes unresponsive, call for help as you proceed with steps to open the airway, and perform rescue breathing.

80B **OPENING THE OBSTRUCTED AIRWAY – CASUALTY LYING DOWN OR UNRESPONSIVE**

5-35. When an individual who is choking becomes unresponsive while lying down, or if an unconscious person is discovered and the cause of their condition is unknown, specific emergency protocols must be followed to address potential airway obstruction.

5-36. If a conscious individual begins choking and subsequently loses consciousness, immediate action is required:

- Summon help right away to ensure assistance is on the way.

- Open the casualty's airway to assess and manage the obstruction.

- Perform a finger sweep only if there is visible obstruction in the mouth to avoid pushing the obstruction deeper.

- Attempt to rescue breathing, which involves assessing whether the air can flow into the lungs. Refer to detailed instructions on mouth-to-mouth resuscitation provided in paragraph 6-49 on page 81. If breathing efforts fail due to a blockage, swiftly move to clear the airway using procedures outlined in paragraph 5-39.

5-37. In scenarios where a casualty is found already unresponsive, the procedure slightly differs:

- Quickly assess the situation to understand the context and severity.

- Call for immediate assistance.

- Position the casualty on their back to manage and secure the airway effectively.

- Check for a pulse to determine the urgency of cardiovascular support.

- Open the airway using recommended techniques.

- Confirm if the casualty is not breathing and, if so, proceed to attempt rescue breathing as per the guidelines detailed in paragraph 6-49 on page 81.

5-38. These critical steps are designed to maximize the chances of recovery by ensuring that the airway is cleared, and that breathing is restored at the earliest possible opportunity. Quick and effective intervention is crucial in such emergencies, underscoring the importance of thorough training and readiness.

5-39. If you are still unable to ventilate the casualty, perform 6 to 10 manual (abdominal or chest) thrusts. To perform the abdominal thrusts:

- Kneel astride the casualty’s thighs (see below figure 5-15).

**Figure 5-15. Abdominal thrust on an unresponsive casualty**

- Place the heel of one hand against the casualty’s abdomen (in the midline slightly above the navel but well below the tip of the breastbone). Place your other hand on top of the first one. Point your fingers toward the casualty’s head.

- Press into the casualty’s abdomen with a quick, forward, and upward thrust. You can use your body weight to perform the maneuver. Deliver each thrust quickly and distinctly.

- Repeat the sequence of abdominal thrusts, finger sweeping, and rescue breathing (attempt to ventilate) as long as necessary to remove the object from the obstructed airway.

- If the casualty’s chest rises, proceed to feel for their pulse.

5-40. To perform chest thrusts the unresponsive casualty is placed on their back, face up, and open their mouth. The first responder kneels close to the side of the casualty’s body and—

- Locates the lower edge of the casualty’s ribs with their fingers by running theirs fingers up along the rib cage to the notch (figure 5-16 step A on page 71).

- Places the middle finger on the notch in the casualty’s chest and the index finger next to the middle finger on the lower edge of the casualty’s breastbone.

- Place the heel of the other hand on the lower half of the breastbone next to the two fingers (figure 5-16 step B on page 71).

- Remove the fingers from the notch and place that hand on top of the positioned hand on the breastbone, extending or interlocking the fingers (figure 5-16 step C on page 71).

- Straighten and lock elbows with their shoulders directly above the hands without bending the elbows, rocking, or allowing the shoulders to sag.

- Apply enough pressure to depress the breastbone 1½ to 2 inches and then release the pressure completely (figure 5-16 step D on page 71). This press and release process is delivered quickly and distinctly, six to 10 times. Figure 5-17 on page 71 shows another view of the breastbone being depressed.

### Caution

Rescuers may feel ribs fracture, and this is OK since it's a lifethreatening situation.

5-41. The first responder repeats the sequence of the chest thrust, finger sweep, and rescue breathing as long as necessary to clear the object from the obstructed airway. If the casualty’s chest rises, proceed to feeling for their pulse.

5-42. If rescue breathing cannot be administered due to airway obstruction, remove the airway obstruction by—

- Placing the casualty on their back, face up, turn the unresponsive casualty as a unit, and call out for help.

- Performing a finger sweep.

- Using the tongue-jaw lift by opening the casualty’s mouth by grasping both their tongue and lower jaw between your thumb and fingers and lifting (tongue-jaw lift) (see figure 5-18). If you are unable to open their mouth, cross your fingers and thumb (crossed-finger method) and push their teeth apart (see figure 5-19) by pressing your thumb against their upper teeth and pressing your finger against their lower teeth.

**Figure 5-18. Opening the casualty’s mouth (tongue-jaw lift)**

**Figure 5-19. Opening the casualty’s mouth (crossed-finger method)**

- Insert the index finger of the other hand down along the inside of their cheek to the base of the tongue. Use a hooking motion from the side of the mouth toward the center to dislodge the foreign body (see figure 5-20).

**Figure 5-20. Using a finger to dislodge a foreign body**

**Take care not to force the object deeper into the airway by pushing it with the finger.**
