# Chapter 19: First Aid in a Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear Environment

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## General Information for First Aid in a Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear Environment

19-1. Planning for large-scale combat operations must account for possible operations in a CBRN environment and allied response options. The threat of CBRN hazards can rapidly degrade unit effectiveness by forcing troops to wear protective clothing and by creating confusion and fear. Through training in CBRN protective and preventive procedures and first aid, units can maintain the desired level of combat effectiveness to meet mission requirements.

19-2. Because of the differences in presentation for CBRN casualties, specific triage and treatment recommendations along with evacuation priority considerations are addressed in the CPGs located on the deployed medicine website.

19-3. CBRN exposure is an enemy attack, and security will be the first priority to be coordinated. Triage for a CBRN event follows established triage principles, including doing the best for most people. Because of the complexity of combining CBRN and trauma casualties, it may be beneficial to assign experienced medical personnel to triage. Triage occurs for decontamination, for medical treatment, and for evacuation. Although assigning casualties to ambulatory vs. non-ambulatory decontamination lanes is a method of sorting, the optimal triage for decontamination determines who can tolerate delays in decontamination.

19-4. Decontamination triage may be based on extent of exposure and not on initial symptoms, as symptoms may be delayed. Those with greater exposure may benefit from rapid decontamination to increase their survivability and thus reduce the future burden on medical resources. Once decontaminated, patients are triaged according to medical priority. Decontamination is a medical intervention (it minimizes or eliminates the conversion of an external dose to an internal dose). Any casualty within an area suspected to contain a liquid chemical agent is automatically medically triaged as immediate until immediate decontamination has been accomplished. The casualty is then re-triaged. Except for blister agent casualties, those exposed only to vapor can tolerate delays in decontamination or may not even require thorough decontamination.

19-5. It is important to frequently reassess patients. Because many CBRN hazards have delayed symptoms, a casualty initially assessed to be minimal may become immediate as the hazard presents itself and process of decontamination can significantly change the casualty status. Additionally, actions during decontamination can significantly change the casualty status. It is critically important to monitor hypothermia, disruption of previously controlled bleeding, and other changes in casualty status that would change triage priority.

### Classification of Chemical and Biological Agents

19-6. Chemical agents are classified based on their properties, effects, and intended use. There are different classification systems depending on the context, such as their use in warfare, industry, or transportation. One widely used classification system for chemical agents is based on their potential harm to human health and the environment. Common chemical agent categories are:

- Adversary use: Persistent agents intended to cause casualties for more than 24 hours or restrict terrain. Nonpersistent agents that can lose their ability to cause casualties after 10-15 minutes.

- Hazard classification: Explosiveness (Class 1), flammability (Classes 3,4), toxicity (Class 6).

- Physiological action: Nerve, blood, blister, choking, and incapacitating.

- Physical state: solid, liquid, or vapor.

19-7. Different regulatory bodies and organizations may have their own specific classification systems and criteria for chemical agents, depending on the context and intended use. These classifications help in

identifying and managing potential hazards, ensuring appropriate handling, storage, transportation, and emergency response measures are in place.

19-8. Biological agents, also known as bioagents or biological hazards, are classified based on their potential to cause harm to living organisms, including humans. They can be categorized in various ways, considering factors such as their origin, mode of transmission, pathogenicity, and potential for use as weapons. Common classification systems for biological agents are:

- Risk Group Classification: Biological agents can be classified into different risk groups based on their level of pathogenicity and the risk they pose to human health. This classification system is commonly used in research laboratories and health care settings. The risk groups range from Risk Group 1 (low individual and community risk) to Risk Group 4 (high individual and community risk). The classification is determined by considering factors such as infectivity, severity of disease, available preventive measures, and efficacy of treatment.

- Biosafety Level Classification: This classification system is primarily used in laboratory settings to ensure appropriate containment measures and safety protocols are followed when handling biological agents. It categorizes agents into different biosafety levels based on their risk to laboratory personnel and the environment. The biosafety levels range from biosafety levels 1 (lowest level of risk) to biosafety levels 4 (highest level of risk). The classification is determined by factors such as pathogenicity, ease of transmission, and availability of effective preventive measures and treatments.

- The U.S. Centers for Disease Control (also known as CDC) and Prevention Category A, B, and C Classification: Developed by the Centers for Disease Control, is focused on potential bioterrorism agents. Biological agents are classified into three categories:

   - Category A: Agents that pose the highest risk to national security and public health. They have the potential for widespread dissemination, high mortality rates, and significant public panic. Examples include anthrax, smallpox, and Ebola virus.

   - Category B: Agents that are moderately easy to disseminate and cause moderate morbidity and low mortality rates. Examples include Q fever, Brucellosis, and ricin toxin.

   - Category C: Agents that have the potential for emerging threats and can be engineered for mass dissemination. Examples include Nipah virus, hantavirus, and emerging strains of influenza.

19-9. These classification systems help in identifying and managing biological hazards, implementing appropriate biosafety and biosecurity measures, developing effective response plans, and conducting research and surveillance activities. They are essential tools in ensuring the safe handling, containment, and prevention of the spread of biological agents. The classification of biological warfare (also known as BW) agents can vary slightly depending on the organization or country using the classification system. These classifications help guide efforts in preparedness, response planning, research, surveillance, and the development of countermeasures against potential biological warfare threats.

### Warning

**Swallowing water or food contaminated with some chemical agents (i.e. nerve, blisters), and with some biological agents can be fatal.**

163B **CONDITIONS FOR MASKING WITHOUT ORDER OR ALARM**

19-10. Once an attack with a chemical or biological agent is detected or suspected, you must STOP BREATHING and mask immediately. DO NOT WAIT to receive an order or alarm if you observe any of these indicators:

- Artillery missiles, rockets, unmanned ariel vehicles or other delivery systems that produce vapors, smoke, mists, and aerial sprays, near your position.

- Smoking or vaporing from an unknown source is present or approaching.

- A suspicious odor, liquid, or solid is present.

- You are entering an area known or suspected of being contaminated.

- Casualties from an area where chemical or biological agents have reportedly been used enter your area.

- You have one or more of the following symptoms:

   - An unexplained runny nose.

   - A sudden unexplained headache.

   - A feeling of choking or tightness in the chest or throat.

   - Dimness of vision.

   - Irritation of the eyes.

   - Difficulty in or increased rate of breathing without obvious reasons.

   - Sudden feeling of depression.

   - Dread, anxiety, or restlessness.

   - Dizziness or light-headedness.

   - Slurred speech.

- Unexplained laughter or unusual behavior is noted in others.

- Numerous unexplained ill personnel.

- Service members collapsing without evident cause.

- Animals or birds exhibiting unusual behavior or suddenly dying.

19-11. For further information on individual protective measures, masking procedures, and other tasks related to surviving and operating in a CBRN environment, refer to ATP 3-11.32, ATP 4-02.7, ATP 4-02.84, and ATP 4-02.85.

## First Aid in a Chemical Environment

19-12. Assessment and management of CBRN casualties is complex because it involves not only lifethreatening symptoms (some of them have rapid onset) but also the threat of contamination of first responders. An added source of complexity is the combination of CBRN and penetrating trauma injuries. This complexity requires a stepwise approach that first rules in or rules out CBRN injuries so first responders can protect themselves before rendering aid. The next step integrates assessment and management of both CBRN and traumatic injuries, prioritizing the management of life-threats first. Chemical agents may be inhaled or ingested. Chemical agents are ingested when food or water contaminated by the agent is consumed or absorbed when the agent is exposed to the skin or eyes.

19-13. Service member’s field protective mask and chemical protective clothing provide good protection from most chemical agents. The Skin Decontamination Kits are used to remove liquid chemical agents from exposed skin. The Individual Equipment Decontamination Kit is used to decontaminate personal equipment. Medications contained in the antidote treatment – nerve agent, auto-injector (ATNAA) kit help to counteract nerve agent poisoning. The effects of the antidote can be enhanced by taking pyridostigmine bromide tablets prior to being exposed to the nerve agent.

19-14. The field protective mask gives protection against biological and chemical warfare agents as well as radiological fallout. Service members should be able to do their field protective masks in nine seconds or less without the hood, and within 15 seconds with the hood.

19-15. The steps to donning a field protective mask in response to a chemical attack are to: stop breathing, put on (don) the mask, seal it properly, clear, and check it; resume breathing, give the alarm, and continue the mission. Keep your mask on until the “all clear” signal has been given.

**_Note._** Keep your protective mask on until the area is no longer hazardous, and you are told to unmask.

19-16. If you begin to experience symptoms of nerve agent poisoning, immediately give yourself one ATNAA.

### Caution

Do not inject a nerve agent antidote until you are sure you need it.

19-17. If your eyes and face become contaminated, you must immediately begin decontamination. You should try to get under cover before beginning immediate decontamination, but do not delay immediate decontamination to search for overhead cover. You need shelter to prevent further contamination while performing decontamination procedures on your face. If no overhead cover is available, put your poncho over your head before beginning the decontamination process. Then, you put on the remaining protective clothing. If vomiting occurs, the mask should be lifted momentarily and drained—with your eyes closed and while holding your breath—then replace, clear, and seal.

19-18. Decontaminate your skin, immediately, and clothing and equipment as soon as the mission permits.

19-19. You may come upon an unconscious chemical agent casualty who requires first aid. You should be able to successfully—

- Mask the unconscious casualty if they are unmasked.

- Inject them, if necessary, with all their auto injectors.

- Decontaminate their skin.

- Seek medical assistance.

### Chemical Biological Radiological Nuclear Casualty Assessment

19-20. Just as trauma casualties present differently (blunt, trauma, GSW, blasts, etc.), CBRN casualties also have varied presentations. CBRN casualties can be categorized by circumstances of exposure and presence or absence of trauma and CBRN effects. The acronym CRESS (Consciousness, Respirations, Eyes, Secretions, Skin) is employed to improve rapid identification of the type of chemical agent exposure.

- C - Consciousness (unconscious, seizures, depressed consciousness, agitation).

- R - Respirations (present or absent, labored, increased or decreased).

- E - Eyes (constricted, dilated, normal).

- S - Secretions (dry, normal, increased).

- S - Skin (diaphoretic, dry, hot, cyanosis).

**_Note._** After using the CRESS algorithm to rule out or rule in a CBRN injury, and first responders doing PPE if present, the integrated assessment and management of TCCC and CBRN injuries can proceed. Additionally, NATO STANAG 2358 and _Joint Trauma System Clinical Practice Guide_ contain additional details, figures, and tables.

19-21. The acronym (MARCHE)2 which stands for (mask, antidotes, rapid spot decontamination, countermeasures, hypothermia prevention, extraction or evacuation) integrates the established TCCC MARCH algorithm with CBRN considerations. Combining these two approaches gives the acronym (MARCHE)2 or “MARCHE-squared.” Like TCCC, (MARCHE)2 can be broken into phases of care from highest threat (care under fire or active CBRN threat), to intermediate threat (tactical field care or warm zone casualty management), to lowest threat (PFC and or evacuation or care of decontaminated casualties in cold zone).

- M - Massive Hemorrhage.

- A - Airway.

- R - Respirations.

- C - Circulation.

- H - Hypothermia.

- M - Mask.

- A - Antidotes.

- R - Rapid spot decontamination.

- C - Countermeasures.

- H - Hypothermia Prevention.

- E - Extraction and Evacuation.

### Nerve Agents

19-23. Nerve agents are among the deadliest chemical agents. They can be delivered by artillery shell, mortar shell, rocket, aircraft bomb, spray, or land mine. In general, nerve agents are colorless, odorless, and tasteless. There are multiple signs and symptoms of nerve agent poisoning, which can vary from mild to severe.

19-24. Nerve agents enter the body by inhalation, ingestion, and through the skin. Depending on the route of entry and the amount, nerve agents can produce injury or death within minutes. Nerve agents can achieve their effects with small amounts. Nerve Agent Pyridostigmine Pretreatment (NAPP) provides an effective pre-treatment for all Soldiers before exposure to nerve agents.

19-25. Service members will be issued the NAPP. NAPP tablet set (figure 19-2 on page 194) when the corps, division, or wing command determines there is a need. The instructions on the proper use of pyridostigmine will be provided at the time of issue. Additionally, instructions are on the tablet set. Although the pyridostigmine itself does not provide protection against nerve agents, it does enhance the efficiency of the medications contained in the ATNAA.

**Figure 19-1. Nerve Agent Pyridostigmine Pretreatment Tablet Set**

19-26. To be effective the NAPP Tablet Set must be taken within eight hours prior to exposure to nerve agents. Since the specific time of exposure is difficult to predict, units should assess the threat and direct Soldiers to initiate NAPP before they could be exposed. The NAPP consists of a blister pack containing 21 tablets to be taken over seven days. Each tablet contains 30mg pyridostigmine bromide. If a dose is missed, do not make it up. Do not take two tablets at one time because of a missed dose; merely start again with one tablet every eight hours. Taking more than one tablet at a time DOES NOT provide additional protection-in fact, it may be more hazardous if there is exposure to a nerve agent.

19-27. Service members are initially issued one NAPP when the chemical biological protective ensemble is expected to be donned. You are responsible for carrying out the NAPP and safeguarding it against loss. Service members will secure the blister pack in the sleeve or breast pocket of the chemical biological protective ensemble (or in another part of the ensemble, as directed by local SOP).

19-28. When the order to take pyridostigmine pretreatment is given, it should be taken as directed even if the protective mask is worn.

**_Note_** . Commanders must follow protocols for pyridostigmine bromide issuance and use.

### Warning

**Pyridostigmine bromide is for pre-exposure prophylaxis only. Do not give to any patient who has been exposed to, presents, or has symptoms of organophosphate nerve agent poisoning.**

19-29. Although no detrimental effects are expected at the recommended dosage, depending on the length of time and the amount of medication taken as well as individual physiological variations, some individuals may have contraindications for taking pyridostigmine bromide, while others may experience adverse reactions.

19-30. Signs and symptoms of overdose, adverse reactions, or side effects are:

- Abdominal cramps.

- Nausea and vomiting.

- Diarrhea.

- Blurred vision.

- Increased bronchial secretions.

- Cardiac arrhythmias and hypertension.

- Weaknesses, muscle cramps, and muscular twitching.

- Skin rash.

19-31. Since pyridostigmine bromide may increase bronchial secretions and aggravate bronchiolar constriction, caution should be used in its administration to personnel with bronchial asthma.

19-32. If any of the above signs or symptoms are present, medical personnel must be consulted as soon as possible.

#### First Aid for Nerve Agent Poisoning

19-35. Nerve agents are absorbed rapidly, and the effects are felt immediately upon entry into the body. You will be issued three ATNAAs and one convulsant antidote nerve agent (CANA) or advanced anticonvulsant system (AAS). Each ATNAA consists of a multi-chambered auto injector with the atropine and pralidoxime chloride in separate chambers. You will use the ATNAA in self-aid and buddy aid as issued.

19-36. If nerve agents are used, mission permitting, watch for persons needing nerve agent antidotes and immediately follow procedures. When you have the signs and symptoms of nerve agent poisoning, you should immediately put on the protective mask and then inject yourself with one set of the ATNAA. First aid for nerve agent poisoning consists of administering the ATNAA, CANA, or AAS.

19-38. Diazepam or midazolam should be administered with the three ATNAA kits when the casualty's condition warrants the use of three ATNAA kits at the same time. Use either the CANA or the AAS to control an actively seizing patient. Do not administer both for the same series of three ATNAA kits.

19-39. The ATNAA (figure 19-4) is a multi-chambered device with the atropine and pralidoxime chloride in separate chambers. Both antidotes will be administered through a single needle. 19-40. The ATNAA is a specially designed unit for automatic self-aid or buddy aid administration by military personnel. When activated, the ATNAA sequentially administers atropine and pralidoxime chloride through a single needle. The recommended procedure is to inject the contents of the auto-injector into the muscles of an outer thigh or into the buttocks. When activated, each ATNAA dispenses:

- 2.1mg atropine in 0.7 milliliter of a sterile, pyrogen-free solution containing 12.47mg glycerin and not more than 2.8mg of phenol, citrate buffer, and water for injection. The potential hydrogen (pH) range is 4.0 – 5.0.

- 600mg of pralidoxime chloride in 2 milliliters of a sterile, pyrogen-free solution containing 40mg benzyl alcohol, 22.5mg glycine and water for injection. The pH is adjusted with hydrochloric acid. The pH range is 2.0 – 3.0.

19-41. The ATNAA should be self-aid or buddy aid administered by military personnel after donning a protective mask and hood but only if some or all the following mild symptoms of nerve agent exposure are present.

##### Reactive Skin Decontamination Lotion Kit

19-42. The RSDL kit (figure 19-5) is intended to remove or neutralize chemical warfare agents from the skin. The RSDL contains Dekon 139 and a small amount of 2,3-butanedione monoxime. These compounds are dissolved in a solvent composed of polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether and water. This solvent system is particularly important as it promotes the decontamination reaction by actively desorbing, retaining, and sequestering the chemical agent, while the active ingredient (Dekon 139) chemically reacts with, and rapidly neutralizes the vesicant chemical or the organophosphorus nerve agent. This reaction starts immediately, and neutralization is usually complete within two minutes.

**Figure 19-5. Reactive Skin Decontamination Lotion Kit**

19-43. The RSDL kit is the preferred method of decontamination. If a RSDL kit is not available soap and large amounts of water can be used for decontamination. Although soap and a large amount of water may be effective in physically removing chemical warfare agents, they do not neutralize chemical warfare agents. Soap and water can increase dermal absorption of mustard agents and can result in contaminated runoff that can affect others.

### Warning

**For external use only. May be slightly irritating to the eyes. Keep decontaminating powder out of the eyes. Use water to wash the toxic agent out of the eyes.**

#### Injection Site

19-44. The injection site for administering the antidotes is normally in the outer thigh muscle. The thigh injection site is the area about a hand’s width below the hip joint (figure 19-6 step A on page 199). It is important that the injection be given into a large muscle area. If the individual is thinly built, then the injections should be administered into the upper outer quarter (quadrant) of the buttock (figure 19-6 step B on page 199). Injecting in the buttocks of a thinly built individual avoids injury to the thighbone.

### Warning

**There is a nerve that crosses the buttocks; hitting this nerve can cause paralysis.**

**Service members that are thinly built, should inject themselves in the upper outer quadrant of the buttock to avoid this nerve.**

**Figure 19-6. Injection site locations**

19-45. If experiencing any or all of the MILD nerve agent symptoms, IMMEDIATELY self-administer one ATNAA kit and put on your protective mask. Follow the procedure given in table 19-3 on page 200. The ATNAA is carried in your protective mask carrier, pocket of the Mission-Oriented Protective Posture over garment, or other location as specified in your unit tactical SOP. In cold weather, the ATNAA should be stored in an inside pocket of the Service member’s clothing to protect the antidote from freezing. A frozen ATNAA cannot be immediately used to provide the antidote, when needed; however, the ATNAA can still be used after complete thawing.

### Caution

DO NOT cover or hold the needle end with your hand, thumb, or fingers—you might accidentally inject yourself. An accidental injection into the hand WILL NOT deliver an effective dose of the antidote, especially if the needle goes through the hand.

19-46. DO NOT self-administer another set of injections. If the Service member can walk without assistance, know who they are, and where they are, they may not need a second set of injections. Administering not needed additional injections may create a nerve-agent-antidote overdose, which could cause incapacitation or the inability to perform the mission or defend themselves.

#### Buddy Evaluation and Buddy Aid

19-48. Service members may seek assistance after self-aid (self-administering) one ATNAA or may become incapacitated after self-aid. A teammate must evaluate the individual to determine if additional antidotes are required to counter the effects of the nerve agent. In addition, Service members may experience SEVERE symptoms of nerve agent poisoning; they will not be able to treat themselves. In either case, other Service members must perform buddy aid as quickly as possible. Before initiating buddy aid, determine if one set of MARK I auto injectors has already been used so that no more than three sets of the antidote are administered. Buddy aid also includes administering the CANA or AAS with the third ATNAA to prevent convulsions. Follow the procedures indicated in table 19-4 on page 201.

Buddy aid also includes administering the CANA or AAS with the third ATNAA to prevent convulsions. Follow the procedures indicated in table 19-4.

### Warning

**Squat, DO NOT kneel, when masking the casualty or administering the nerve agent antidotes to the casualty. Kneeling may force any chemical agent on your over garment into or through your protective clothing.**

**_Note._** If the casualty is thinly built, inject the antidote into the buttocks (figure 19-7 on page 202). Only inject the antidote into the upper outer portion of the casualty’s buttocks. This avoids hitting the nerve that crosses the buttocks. Hitting this nerve can cause paralysis.

### Warning

**DO NOT inject into areas close to the hip, knee, or thighbone.**

#### Combat Lifesaver Assistance for Nerve Agent Poisoning

19-50. The CLS must check to verify if the individual has received three sets of ATNAAs. If not, the CLS performs first aid as described for buddy aid above. If the individual has received the three sets of, ATNAA, the CLS may administer additional injections at approximately 15-minute intervals until atropinization is achieved (that is a heart rate above 90 beats per minute, reduced bronchial secretions, and reduced salivations). Administer additional atropines at intervals of 30 minutes to four hours to maintain atropinization or until the casualty is placed under the care of medical personnel. Check the heart rate by lifting the casualty’s mask hood and feeling for a pulse at the carotid artery. Request medical assistance as soon as the tactical situation permits.

19-51. The CLS should administer additional CANA or AAS to casualties suffering from convulsions. Administer a second, and if needed, a third CANA or AAS at 5-to-10-minute intervals for a maximum of three injections (30mg diazepam or 30mg midazolam). Follow the steps and procedures described in paragraph 19-49 on page 201 for administering the CANA or AAS. DO NOT give more than two additional injections for a total of three (one self-aid plus two by the CLS).

##### Blister Agents

19-52. Blister agents (vesicants) include mustard, nitrogen mustards, lewisite, and other arsenicals, mixtures of mustards and arsenicals, and phosgene oxime. Blister agents may act on the eyes, mucous membranes, lungs, and skin. They burn and blister the skin or any other body parts they contact. Even relatively low doses may cause serious injury. Blister agents damage the respiratory tract (nose, sinuses, and windpipe) when inhaled and cause vomiting and diarrhea when absorbed. Lewisite and phosgene oxime cause immediate pain on contact. However, mustard agents are deceptive as there is little or no pain at the time of exposure. In some cases, signs of injury may not appear for several hours after exposure.

#### Protective Measures for Blister Agent Poisoning

19-53. Your protective mask with hood and protective over garment provides protection against blister agents. If it is known or suspected that blister agents are being used, STOP BREATHING, put on your mask and your protective over garment.

### Caution

Large drops of liquid vesicants on the protective over garment ensemble may penetrate it if allowed to stand for an extended period. Remove large drops as soon as possible.

#### Signs and Symptoms of Blister Agent Poisoning

19-54. Immediate and intense pain upon contact with L, LH (lewisite and mustard) mixture, and phosgene oxime. No initial pain upon contact with mustard.

19-55. Inflammation and blisters (burns) resulting in tissue destruction. The severity of a chemical burn is directly related to the concentration of the agent and the duration of contact with the skin. The longer the agent is in contact with the tissue, the more serious the injury will be.

19-56. Vomiting and diarrhea. Exposure to high concentrations of vesicants may cause vomiting or diarrhea.

19-57. The blister agent vapors absorbed during ordinary field exposure will probably not cause enough internal body (systemic) damage to result in death. However, death may occur from prolonged exposure to high concentrations of vapor or from extensive liquid contamination over wide areas of the skin, particularly when decontamination is neglected or delayed.

#### First Aid Measures for Blister Agent Poisoning

19-58. Use your RSDL to decontaminate your skin and use water to flush contaminated eyes. Decontamination of vesicants must be done immediately (within one minute is best).

19-59. If blisters form, cover them loosely with a field dressing and secure the dressing.

### Caution

Blisters are burns. DO NOT attempt to decontaminate the skin where blisters have formed, as the agent has already been absorbed.

19-60. If you receive blisters over a wide area of the body, you are considered seriously burned. Seek medical assistance immediately.

19-61. If vomiting occurs, the mask should be lifted momentarily and drained. Make sure, while doing this, the eyes are closed, and the breath is held. As soon as it is complete, replace, clear, and seal the mask.

19-62. Remember, if vomiting or diarrhea occurs after having been exposed to blister agents, seek medical assistance immediately.

### Choking Agents (lung-damaging Agents)

19-63. Chemical agents that attack lung tissue, primarily causing fluid buildup (pulmonary edema), are classified as choking agents (lung-damaging agents). This group includes phosgene, diphosgene (known as DP), chlorine (known as Cl), and chloropicrin (known as PS). Of these four agents, phosgene is the most dangerous and is more likely to be employed by the enemy in future conflict. Your protective mask gives adequate protection against choking agents.

#### Signs and Symptoms for Choking Agents

19-64. During and immediately after exposure to choking agents (depending on agent concentration and length of exposure), you may experience some or all the following signs and symptoms:

- Tears (lacrimation).

- Coughing.

- Choking.

- Tightness of chest.

- Nausea and vomiting.

- Headaches.

#### Self-Aid for Choking Agents

19-65. The protective mask should be put on immediately when any of the conditions described above exist. Another indication of a phosgene attack is an odor like newly mown hay; however, DO NOT rely upon odor as indication of a chemical attack.

19-66. If some phosgene is inhaled, normal combat duties should be continued unless there is difficulty in breathing, nausea, vomiting, or more than the usual shortness of breath during exertion. If any of the above symptoms occur and the mission permits, remain at quiet rest until MEDEVAC is accomplished.

#### Death from Choking Agents

19-67. With ordinary field exposure to choking agents, death will probably not occur. However, prolonged exposure to high concentrations of the vapor and neglect, or delay in masking can be fatal.

#### Cyanogen (Blood) Agents

19-68. Cyanogen agents interfere with proper oxygen utilization in the body. Hydrogen cyanide (AC) and cyanogen chloride are the primary agents in this group.

#### Protective Measures for Cyanogen Agents

19-69. The protective mask with a fresh filter gives adequate protection against field concentrations of cyanogen agent vapor. Protective over garments, as well as the mask, are needed when exposed to liquid AC hydrogen cyanide.

#### Signs and Symptoms for Cyanogen Agents

19-70. During and immediately after exposure to cyanogen agents (depending on agent concentration and length of exposure), you may experience some or all the following signs and symptoms:

- Tearing (lacrimation).

- Eye, nose, and throat irritation.

- Sudden stimulation of breathing (unable to hold breath).

- Nausea.

- Coughing.

- Tightness of chest.

- Headache.

- Light-headedness (dizziness).

- Unconsciousness.

#### First Aid for Cyanogen Agents

19-71. Hydrogen cyanide. During any chemical attack, if you get a sudden stimulation to breathe or detect an odor like bitter almonds, PUT ON YOUR MASK IMMEDIATELY. Speed is essential since this agent acts so rapidly that within a few seconds its effects will make it impossible for Service members to put on their mask by themselves. Stop breathing until the mask is on. This may be very difficult since the agent strongly stimulates respiration.

19-72. Cyanogen chloride. Put your mask on immediately if you experience any irritation of the eyes, nose, or throat. The nearest Service member should mask Service members who are unable to mask themselves.

|**CAUTION**|
|---|

Before attempting to help anyone else, make sure you appropriately mask yourself first.

#### Medical Assistance for Cyanogen Agents

19-73. If exposure to blood agents is suspected, immediate medical assistance is needed. Medical assistance for cyanogen agents is crucial because cyanogen is a highly toxic compound that poses significant health risks to humans. Cyanogen is a colorless gas that can be lethal when inhaled or when it encounters the skin or eyes.

### Incapacitating Agents

19-74. An incapacitating agent is a chemical agent that produces temporary, disabling conditions, which persist for hours to days after exposure. Unlike riot control agents, which usually are momentary or fleeting in action, incapacitating agents have a persistent effect. It is likely that smoke-producing munitions or aerosols will disseminate such agents, thus making breathing their means of entry into the body. The protective mask is, therefore, essential.

19-75. There are no specific first aid measures to relieve the symptoms of incapacitating agents. Supportive first aid and physical restraint may be indicated. If the casualty is stuporous or comatose, be sure that respiration is unobstructed; then turn them on their side in case vomiting should occur. Complete cleansing of the skin with soap and water, this should be done as soon as possible; or RSDL can be used if washing is impossible. Remove weapons and other potentially harmful items from Service members who are suspected of having these symptoms. Harmful items include cigarettes, matches, medications, and small items that might be swallowed accidentally. Delirious (confused) persons have been known to attempt to eat items bearing only a superficial resemblance to food.

19-76. Incapacitating agents (anticholinergic drugs BZ type) may produce alarming dryness and coating of the lips and tongue; however, there is usually no danger of immediate dehydration. Fluids should be given sparingly, if at all, because of the danger of vomiting and because of the likelihood of temporary urinary retention due to paralysis of bladder muscles.

19-77. Immediate and vigorous cooling of the casualties is indicated if the body temperature is elevated and mucous membranes are dry, (as for heatstroke). The casualty can be cooled by spraying them with cool water and fanning; applying alcohol-soaked cloths and air circulation; and providing maximum exposure to air in a shaded area, along with maximum air circulation. Such cases are usually a result of anticholinergic poisoning.

### Caution

DO NOT use ice for cooling the skin.

19-78. Rapid evacuation should be accomplished since medical treatment with the appropriate medication may be lifesaving. Reassurance and a firm, but friendly, attitude by individuals providing first aid will be beneficial if the casualty appears to comprehend what is being said. Conversation is a waste of time if the Service member is incoherent or cannot understand what is being said. In such cases, the less said, the better it is. These casualties will benefit more from prompt and vigorous restraint and evacuation to an MTF.

### Incendiaries

19-79. Incendiaries can be grouped as white phosphorus (WP), thickened gasoline, metal, and oil and metal. You must learn to protect yourself against these incendiaries.

#### White Phosphorus

19-80. WP is primarily used as a smoke-producing agent but also serves an incendiary role for igniting field expedients and combustible materials. The burns caused by WP are typically multiple, deep, and vary in size. Once WP particles contact the skin or clothing, they will continue to burn until deprived of air, and they tend to adhere to any surface they contact, requiring physical removal.

19-81. In the event that WP particles ignite clothing, it's crucial to remove the contaminated clothing quickly to prevent the WP from burning through to the skin. If WP particles directly strike the skin, the immediate response should be to smother the flames using water, a wet cloth, or mud. It is important to note that since WP is soluble in oil, one should not use grease or oily substances to extinguish the flames as these can exacerbate the burning.

19-82. To prevent further combustion, keep the WP particles covered with wet material to exclude air. Removal of WP particles from the skin should be done cautiously using a wet cloth to brush them away and a knife, bayonet, stick, or other available tools to pick them out.

19-83. Once immediate first aid measures are taken, it is vital to seek medical assistance as soon as the mission allows us to address any burns or injuries caused by WP exposure properly.

#### Thickened Fuel Mixtures (Napalm)

19-84. Thickened fuel mixtures (napalm) tend to cling to clothing and body surfaces, thereby producing prolonged exposure and severe burns. The first aid for these burns is the same as for other heat burns. The heat and irritating gases given off by these combustible mixtures may cause lung damage, which must be treated by medical personnel.

#### Metal Incendiaries

19-85. Metal incendiaries pose special problems. Thermite particles on the skin should be immediately cooled with water and then removed. The first aid for these burns is the same as for other heat burns. Particles of magnesium on the skin burn quickly and deeply. Like other metal incendiaries, they must be removed. Ordinarily, medical personnel should do the complete removal of these particles as soon as possible. Immediate medical treatment is required.

#### Oil and Metal Incendiaries

19-86. Oil and metal incendiaries, upon contact with skin and clothing, produce effects similar to those caused by white phosphorus. These substances can cause severe burns that require immediate and proper first aid attention to prevent further damage and promote healing. For detailed guidance on administering first aid for burns resulting from exposure to incendiaries like oil and metal, see Chapter 12. This chapter provides comprehensive instructions on the steps to take in treating burns, including how to manage the injury before professional medical help is available.

## First Aid in a Biological Environment

19-87. Biological hazards can provide important advantages to adversaries who use them because of factors such as—

- Clandestine employment.

- Delayed onset of symptoms.

- Detection, identification, and verification difficulties.

- Agent persistence.

- Communicability.

19-88. Biological attacks can result in combat ineffectiveness by introducing disease-causing organisms such as bacteria or viruses into the force through aerosol release; food and water contamination; vectors such as ticks, fleas, mosquitoes, or body fluids from infected animals or humans. The occurrence of the signs and symptoms from a biological hazard may appear in a few minutes to several hours or days to weeks depending on the biological hazard, the Service member’s susceptibility, and the amount of biological hazard that is inhaled, ingested, or deposited on the skin. Many biological warfare agents present the same signs and symptoms as the flu or common cold such as fever, cough, runny nose, and tiredness.

19-89. First aid or medical treatment is initiated after a disease is identified, depending on the seriousness of the disease. First aid measures are concerned with observable symptoms of the disease such as diarrhea or vomiting. For certain biological warfare hazards, Service members may be directed to take doxycycline or ciprofloxacin from the initial issue medical CBRN defense materiel.

### Toxins

19-90. Toxins have reportedly been used in past conflicts. Witnesses and survivors have described the employment of the T-2 mycotoxin as toxic rain (or yellow rain) because it was reported to have been released from aircraft as a yellow powder or liquid that covered ground, structures, vegetation, and people.

#### Signs and Symptoms of Toxin Poisoning

19-91. The occurrence of the symptoms from toxins may appear in a period of a few minutes to several hours depending on the toxin, the Service member’s susceptibility, and the amount of toxin that is inhaled, ingested, or deposited on the skin. Symptoms from toxins usually involve the central nervous system but are often preceded by less prominent symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, cramps, stomach irritation, and burning sensation. Typical neurological symptoms often develop rapidly in severe cases; for example, visual disturbances, inability to swallow, speech difficulty, lack of muscle coordination, and sensory abnormalities (numbness of the mouth, throat, or extremities). Yellow rain (mycotoxins) also may have hemorrhagic symptoms, which could include any or all of the following:

- Dizziness.

- Severe itching or tingling of the skin.

- Formation of multiple, small, or hard blisters.

- Coughing up blood.

- Shock (which could result in death).

#### Self-Aid of Toxin Poisoning

19-92. Upon recognition of an attack employing toxins, you must immediately take the following actions:

- Stop breathing, put on your protective mask with hood, and then resume breathing. Next, put on your protective ensemble.

- Should itching of the face become severe or unbearable, quickly—

   - Loosen the cap in your canteen or valve on your water source.

   - Take and hold a deep breath and lift your mask.

   - While holding your breath, close your eyes and flush your face with generous amounts of water.

### Caution

DO NOT rub or scratch your eyes. Try not to let the water run onto your clothing or protective over garment.

- Put your protective mask back on, seat it properly, clear it, and check it for a seal; then resume breathing.

- Decontaminate your skin by bathing with soap and water as soon as the mission permits.

- Change clothing and decontaminate your protective mask using soap and water. Replace the filters if directed.

19-93. If vomiting occurs, the mask should be lifted momentarily and drained. While doing this, keep your eyes closed, and hold your breath. The mask is then replaced, cleared, and sealed.

#### Medical Assistance for Toxin Poisoning

19-94. If you think you might have been exposed to harmful substances, it's important to get medical help right away. Seeing a doctor quickly can ensure you get the care you need.

## First Aid in a Radiological and Nuclear Environment

19-95. Three types of injuries may result from nuclear detonation. These are thermal, blast, and radiation injuries. Many times, the casualty will have a combination of these types of injuries. First aid for thermal and blast injuries is provided based on observable injuries, such as burns, hemorrhage, or fractures.

19-96. The signs and symptoms of radiation illness in the initial phase include the rapid onset of nausea, vomiting, and malaise (tiredness) with first aid measures focused on those observable symptoms and decontamination. If radioactive iodine is released, Service members may be directed to take potassium iodide from the initial issue medical CBRN defense materiel. Potassium iodide is only effective if taken within four hours before or after exposure to radiation from a nuclear or radiological incident. For additional information on first aid in a radiological and nuclear environment see paragraph 1-85 on page 16 or refer to DODM 3150.8, JP 3-11, or ATP 4-02.83.
