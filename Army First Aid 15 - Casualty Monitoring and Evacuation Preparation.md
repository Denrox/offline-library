# Chapter 15: Casualty Monitoring and Evacuation Preparation

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Casualty Monitoring

15-1. After the first responder and CLS have treated the casualty in CUF and TFC phase of TCCC they may be required to continue to monitor the casualty and prepare the casualty for evacuation. The first responder and CLS cannot treat a casualty and then forget about them. The casualty must be continually monitored and reassessed prior to the casualty’s evacuation. The continued monitoring utilizing the MARCH-PAWS sequence will allow the first responder and CLS to identify changes in the casualty’s condition and initiate those interventions necessary to prevent unnecessary death.

15-2. Casualty monitoring follows the same MARCH-PAWS process and will guide the first responder and CLS through their reassessments. After the initial casualty assessment and any treatments that are performed, the casualty must be reassessed every 15 minutes, or if seriously wounded every five to 10 minutes. The reassessment should start by reexamining any massive bleeding issues or interventions, should proceed through the rest of the MARCH-PAWS sequence, and be repeated until that casualty is transferred to the care of medical personnel.

15-3. The first responder or CLS should next evaluate any changes in respiratory status, look for signs or symptoms of shock, hypothermia, or head injuries. Also, monitor the casualty's respiratory rate, pulse, and level of consciousness. Assessing the casualty's respiratory status involves checking the rate and the quality of the respirations. By looking, listening, and feeling for breaths on the cheek, the respiratory rate can be determined (documented in the number of breaths per minute) and the respiratory effort (shallow breaths, difficulties moving air in and out, and loss of air movement on one side of the chest).

15-4. If a casualty becomes unconscious or breathing rate drops below eight respirations within one minute, insert an NPA. The casualty must be reassessed for tension pneumothorax and treated as necessary by performing an NDC. The NDC must be reassessed to confirm that it was successful. Document the rate, respiratory effort, and time assessed on DD Form 1380. If the casualty's respiratory status begins to change, reassess their status using the same approach used in the tactical trauma assessment.

15-5. The level of consciousness is best expressed by addressing the casualty's response using the AVPU method. Document any AVPU assessments or any changes in the casualty's status on the DD Form 1380. This documentation helps medical personnel better understand the casualty's situation.

**_Note._** If the casualty is not ALERT, indicating decreased mental status, the casualty should not have weapons or communications equipment.

15-6. Assessing the casualty's circulation status is done by checking for pulses. Depending on the casualty and their injuries, the first responder and CLS can check the casualty's pulse at the carotid artery (neck) or radial artery (wrist). The first responder and CLS should use their index and middle fingers, NOT their thumb, to check pulses (figure 15-1 on page 152). Document pulse rates, locations, and time taken on the DD Form 1380.

**_Note._** The absence of a radial pulse is an indication that the casualty is in shock.

15-7. When requesting MEDEVAC, several preparatory actions are crucial for ensuring the casualty's safety and stability during transport. These steps include:

- Securing the Casualty's Weapons and Equipment: It is vital to secure all weapons and equipment according to the SOPs or mission requirements. Due to the potential inability of casualties to manage their own equipment owing to injuries or altered mental status, it is essential to secure these items to prevent accidents. If possible, weapons should not accompany the casualty during evacuation. If securing the weapon is not feasible, ensure it is cleared and rendered safe.

- Selecting Appropriate Litter: Choose a litter that aligns with both the casualty’s specific needs and the mission profile to facilitate a safe and efficient evacuation.

- Preparing Equipment for Evacuation: Organize and prepare any equipment that is needed to accompany the casualty. This includes medical supplies or specialized gear necessary for their condition.

- Anticipating Environmental Factors: Consider environmental challenges that might impact the casualty’s stability during evacuation. This includes extreme noises, vibrations from the transport vehicle, high winds from propeller wash, and exposure to cold temperatures due to altitude or the environment.

- Developing a Mitigation Plan: Formulate a plan to address each potential environmental threat to maintain the casualty's stability throughout the evacuation process.

15-8. It is important to note that medical personnel receiving the casualty may not be familiar with the equipment or have the means to secure it. Thus, preparing adequately and ensuring everything is in order prior to evacuation is critical for the safety and care of the casualty.

## Pre-evacuation Procedures, Communication, and Documentation

15-9. Pre-evacuation procedures bridge both TFC and Tactical Evacuation Care. Immediate life-threatening hemorrhage control, followed by the prevention and treatment of other injuries and complications have all been completed before most pre-evacuation procedures are initiated. Although, some of the communication and documentation may be ongoing during the TFC phase.

### Communicating with the Casualty

15-10. When providing care, the first responder or CLS should always talk to the injured person. Getting hurt can cause a lot of worry and fear, on top of the stress from combat. It helps to speak openly with them about what injuries they have and what you're doing to help. Let them know that you're doing everything possible for them and assure them that they're in good hands. This kind of support can help lessen their anxiety.

15-11. It's important to be truthful about the injuries but keep a hopeful tone when talking about rescue and treatment. Chatting with the injured person is not only comforting but also lets you check how they are feeling mentally. Explaining what you're doing as you provide care can boost both your own confidence and the injured person's trust in you.

### Communicating with Leaders

15-12. Communicate with leaders as soon as possible and throughout casualty treatment. Leaders need to understand the impact of casualties on the mission. Communicate with medical providers about details of the casualty injuries. This includes 9-line communication and ongoing MIST reports. Communication is also important, as the injured casualty may affect the success of the mission or change the tactical landscape.

### Medical Documentation

15-13. Medical documentation may be difficult to accomplish in tactical prehospital settings, but it is important to the casualty's subsequent care that every effort should be made to document the care provided by first responders and medics throughout the trauma care continuum from point of wounding and injury to definitive care at the hospital. A DD Form 1380 is provided in each first aid kit.

15-14. Based on the principles of TCCC, this card provides an easy way to document initial lifesaving care provided at the point of wounding. The card also serves as a prompt to remind first responders and CLS of the assessment and treatment steps of the MARCH-PAWS sequence. DD Form 1380 is relatively selfexplanatory once personnel have been trained on using the form.

15-15. The MIST information about the casualty informs the MEDEVAC request and can be collected simultaneously with the other required information. The MIST report transmits medical information to the receiving combat medic, evacuation platform, and MTF to better prepare for specific inbound casualties. A MIST report conveys additional evacuation information that may be required by commanders.

15-16. Transitioning casualty care to another medical team is best accomplished with an oral discussion of the casualty's status, along with the written documentation on the DD Form 1380. When an oral hand-off is not a viable option, the written information may be the only way receiving medical personnel will know what the provider has done to help the casualty and what the next steps should be to provide the best care going forward.

15-17. The DD Form 1380 (figure 15-2 on page 154) should be completed on every casualty before handoff to another provider or evacuation to an MTF. DD Form 1380 will follow the patient and will be used until the patient arrives at a role of care where a DD Form 3019 ( _Resuscitation Record)_ , can be used.

**_Note 1_** . This document should remain with the casualty throughout the prehospital evacuation system and become part of the casualty’s medical record upon reaching an MTF. The data from the DD Form 1380 will also be uploaded into the DOD Trauma Registry maintained by the JTS.

**_Note 2._** Many commercially available casualty cards do not replicate all information requirements of the DD Form 1380. While the DD Form 1380 may be reproduced locally, it should be on waterproof paper or laminated with a means of attaching it to the casualty. It is best to have an indelible or permanent marker available for writing on the TCCC Card.

15-18. All Service members should have at least one or more DD Form 1380s within their first aid kit. For additional information and instructions on completing the DD Form 1380 refer to AR 40-66. Use the following steps to complete a DD Form 1380:

- Remove the DD Form 1380 from the casualty's first aid kit.

- Complete all entries as fully as possible.

**_Note._** As the DD Form 1380 is the first, and sometimes the only record of treatment of combat casualties, accuracy and thoroughness of information provided is of the utmost importance.

- Front of DD Form 1380 (figure 15-2):

   - Battle Roster # - Write first letter of the casualty's first name, then write first letter of the casualty's last name, write the last four numbers of the casualty's identification number. In figure 15-2, the Service member’s battle roster number is “BL6553.”

   - Evacuation (EVAC) - Mark an “X” on the casualty's evacuation precedence (Urgent; Priority; or Routine).

   - Name - Write casualty's name (Last, First).

   - Last 4 - Write last four numbers of casualty's identification number.

   - Sex - Mark an “X” on the casualty's sex Male (M) or Female (F).

   - Date - Write date of injury in DD-MMM-YY format. For example, “29-JUN-13.”

   - Time - Write 24-hour time of injury and indicate whether Local (L) or Zulu (Z) time. For example, “1300Z.”

   - Service - Write casualty's branch of service (United States Army [USA], United States Air Force [USAF], United States Coast Guard [USCG], United States Navy [USN], United States Marine Corps [USMC], and United States Space Force [USSF]. For U.S. civilians write “U.S. CIV”. For non-U.S. personnel, write “NON-U.S.” or a standard abbreviation for the casualty's nationality.

   - Unit - Write casualty's unit name.

   - Allergies - Write casualty's known drug allergies. If no drug allergies, write no known drug allergies (NKDA).

   - Mechanism of Injury - Mark an “X” on the mechanism or cause of injury (mark all that apply) (artillery, blunt, burn, fall, grenade, gunshot wound [GSW], improvised explosive device [IED], landmine, motor vehicle crash or collision [MVC], rocket-propelled grenade -propelled grenade [RPG], and other (specify).

   - Injury - Mark an “X” at the site of the injury(s) on the body picture. For burn injuries, circle the burn percentage(s) on the figure. If multiple mechanisms of injury and multiple injuries, draw a line between the mechanism of injury and the anatomical site of the injury.

   - TQ: R Arm - If a TQ is applied to the right arm, write the type of TQ (hasty or deliberate) used, and the time of TQ application.

   - TQ: L Arm - If a TQ is applied to the left arm, write the type of TQ (hasty or deliberate) used, and the time of TQ application.

   - TQ: R Leg - If a TQ is applied to the right leg, write the type of TQ (hasty or deliberate) used, and the time of TQ application.

   - TQ: L Leg - If a TQ is applied to the left leg, write type of TQ (hasty or deliberate) used, and the time of TQ application.

   - Time - Write the time that vital signs are taken.

   - Pulse (rate and location) - Write casualty's pulse rate.

   - Blood Pressure - Write casualty's blood pressure.

   - Respiratory Rate - Write casualty's respiratory rate.

   - AVPU - Write casualty's level of consciousness.

   - Pain Scale (0-10) - Write casualty's level of pain in numeric rating scale of 0 to 10, with 0 being no pain and 10 being the worst pain.

- Back of Card (figure 15-2 on page 154): ▪ Battle Roster # - Write first letter of casualty's first name, then first letter of casualty's last name, write the last four numbers of the casualty's identification number. In figure 15-2, the Service member’s battle roster number is “LD2270.”

   - Evacuation (EVAC) - Mark an “X” on the casualty's evacuation precedence (Urgent, Priority, or Routine).

   - C - Mark an “X” for all Circulation hemorrhage control interventions. For TQ, mark category (Extremity, Junctional, and, or Truncal) and write name of TQ(s) used. For dressings, mark category (Hemostatic, Pressure, and, or Other) and write type of dressing(s) used.

   - A - Mark an “X” for all airway interventions (Intact, NPA, cricothyroidotomy (known as CRIC), endotracheal (known as ET) tube, supraglottic airway (SGA) and write the type of device(s) used.

   - B - Mark an “X” for all Breathing interventions oxygen (O2), needle decompression (NeedleD), Chest-Tube, (Chest-Seal) and write the type of device(s) used.

   - C: Fluid - Circulation resuscitation interventions. Write name, volume, route, and time of any fluids given.

   - C: Blood Product - Circulation resuscitation interventions. Write name, volume, route, and time of any blood products given.

**_Note._** When more space is needed for documentation, attach another DD Form 1380 to the original by safety pin or other means. The second form will be labeled DD Form 1380 #2, showing the Service member’s name and unit.

   - Analgesic Medications - Write name, dose, route, and time that any analgesics are given.

   - Antibiotic Medications - Write name, dose, route, and time that any antibiotics are given.

   - Other Medications - Write name, dose, route, and time of any other administered medications.

   - Other - Mark an “X” for other treatments administered (combat pill pack, eye shield (mark right (R) or left (L)), splint, hypothermia prevention) and the type of device(s) used.

   - Notes - Use this space to record any other pertinent information or clarifications.

   - First Responder Name - Print the first responder's name (Last, First).

   - First Responder Last 4 - Write the last four numbers of the first responder's social security number or identification number.

- Attach the completed DD Form 1380 to the casualty in a visible area where follow on medical staff will see it.

**_Note._** Do not attach DD Form 1380 to the casualty's body armor, as this equipment most likely will be separated from the casualty once they arrive at the MTF.

15-19. First responders and CLS personnel are advised to fill out the DD Form 1380 to the best of their ability. DD Form 1380 is crucial as it records evidence of initial treatment and provides subsequent levels of medical care with essential information. This documentation is vital for medical personnel to assess and determine the appropriate continuation of care for the casualty. This process ensures that each step of medical intervention is informed and based on prior actions taken, facilitating a cohesive and effective treatment pathway.

### Medical Evacuation Request

15-20. Every Service member should be able to initiate a 9-line MEDEVAC request. The standard MEDEVAC request has nine lines. However, lines one through five are required before a MEDEVAC can be launched. Using a phonetic alphabet and following the unit's normal communications procedures, call in:

- Grid location.

- Radio frequency and call sign.

- Number of casualties by precedence.

- Equipment that needs to be brought by the evacuation team.

- Number of litter and ambulatory casualties.

15-21. Before initiating a 9-line MEDEVAC request, collect all the information needed. When calling in, be sure to follow all appropriate communication protocols and guidance.

15-22. Depending on operational, mission variables, and availability, evacuation options may involve MEDEVAC, CASEVAC, or a combination of the two. During CASEVAC, casualties are moved without medical regulating and do not possess a protected status under the Geneva Conventions. As such, CASEVAC platforms may be engaged by enemy forces. With MEDEVAC, patients may be regulated or unregulated and the ambulance is properly marked, MEDEVAC cannot be used for nonmedical missions without losing its protected status. The 9-line MEDEVAC request is communicated through the designated evacuation system or Patient Evacuation Coordination Cell, depending on orders and the tactical SOP.

15-23. When a Service member requests a MEDEVAC, they are not directly coordinating with a medical provider but are explaining the requestor’s evacuation requirements with someone who coordinates the movement assets. Although they still require some general information about the status of the casualty, much of the information that they need to coordinate evacuation is not clinical and relates to logistical and operational issues.

15-24. Communicate with medical providers on the evacuation asset if possible and relay the MIST report. Provide additional information as appropriate. For additional information on MEDEVAC request refer to ATP 4-02.2.

## Evacuation Equipment and Casualty Packaging

15-25. Essential evacuation equipment (for example, litters, packaging materials, and BVMs) should be prepared by other unit personnel while medical treatment continues and coordinating with the casualty treatment team to save time. For example, other unit members can prepare litters while treatment is being provided. Do not delay getting casualties onto litters. The first responder and CLS can better prevent hypothermia by getting casualties off the ground.

### Litters

15-26. Casualty movement in TFC may be better accomplished using litter due to the tactical situation and the need to move casualties rapidly. The litter exists only to facilitate casualty movement, and the casualty can be placed in the best position that facilitates their care and comfort. Casualties do not have to be placed on their backs on a litter. It is easier to move them if they are already on the litter; however, they must be secured to the litter before movement. Litter is also usually better for moving a casualty long distance.

15-27. All unit members should know how to open and set up litters and rehearse their use during pre-mission training. All unit members should know who will carry litter or where litter is located on vehicles.

15-28. Selecting the proper litter is dependent on several different factors:

- The evacuation platform.

- The terrain at the location of pick-up.

- The casualty’s injuries.

- The availability of different types of litters.

15-29. Two of the more common litters often used are the quad-folding litter and the lightweight flexible stretchers. Each of them has advantages and disadvantages and maybe the proper litter depends on the situation. For example, flexible litter can be moved using a one-person drag (terrain permitting) but are more difficult to carry longer distances than a quad-folding litter.

15-30. Each unit will have litters that have been proven to support their mission profile, and it is important to be aware of the limitations and advantages of each litter at your disposal before being deployed in a situation where you will require them.

### Casualty Packaging

15-31. When preparing a casualty for evacuation, the first responder or CLS need to anticipate the environmental factors that could prevent the casualty from remaining stable. Examples include extreme noises, vibrations, high winds from propeller washing, and exposure to cold environments from either altitude or low ambient temperatures. A plan should be developed to mitigate each of those potential threats when preparing for your casualty.

15-32. All loose ends of bandages, medical equipment, and hypothermia prevention materials should be secured. The first responder or CLS should prevent dressings and other medical items from being blown around by the rotor wash or becoming entangled with other equipment.

15-33. Loose materials can catch on anything from tree limbs to body-armor of litter bearers, to parts of the aircraft or vehicles. Any snag like this can cause delays in evacuation or even further injury to casualties or providers. Blankets and foil-based hypothermia materials are especially susceptible to being caught in the wind.

15-34. Hypothermia prevention equipment should be tucked and secured beneath the casualty and litter straps. Loose edges can be caught up in wind or rotor wash or snagged on objects in the helicopter as the casualty is loaded aboard.

15-35. The TQs should remain visible and not be hidden. The DD Form 1380 should be attached to the casualty.

#### Evacuation Considerations for Suspected Spinal Injuries

15-36. A spinal injury should be expected in certain situations, like fast-roping falls or being near a significant blast. In those cases, when tactically feasible, ensure the C-spine is immobilized and the casualty is kept straight during evacuation. Keep the litter type and evacuation platform in mind and ensure the evacuation ground or air vehicle can accommodate the selected litter.

#### Walking Wounded

15-37. Not all casualties require a litter for evacuation. For those casualties who are still ambulatory, provide instructions or assistance as needed. Depending on the nature of their injuries, they may be able to assist with carrying litters or providing security.

15-38. It is best to guide disoriented or visually impaired casualties by having them place their hand on the shoulder of the casualty in front of them, or a nonmedical attendant as they move to the evacuation platform. Instruct them on repeatedly checking their own wounds and dressings to ensure that bleeding remains controlled throughout the evacuation process.

## Casualty Staging

15-39. A particular importance when moving more than one casualty is the process of staging the casualties. Based on the guidance from the MEDEVAC personnel, certain casualties have priority and may need to be loaded last, so that they are the first ones offloaded at the destination.

15-40. If not given specific guidance, place the most serious casualty in a position where they are loaded last, and have casualties who may be less severely injured loaded before them. The first responder or CLS may also need to assist in marking the LZ, providing security, or assisting in marshalling the aircraft or litterbearer teams.

## Evacuation Care After-action Review

15-41. It is critical that documentation of personnel, injury, and treatment be annotated using DD Form 1380 and should be completed prior to any handoff of the casualty. If care cannot be documented using the DD Form 1380 at the point of care by the first responder or CLS, it should be documented using the TCCC after action review, found on the JTS website, as soon as possible (within 72 hours) after treatment. The nonmedical first responder or CLS should follow up with the supporting unit medical personnel to complete and submit the TCCC after action review.

In the operational environment, Service members are exposed to risks and receive injuries beyond those caused by an operational event. First aid skills play a vital role in ensuring the safety and well-being of Service members. As personnel participate in outdoor activities and training, the potential for accidents or injuries increases. Having a solid foundation in first aid equips Service members with the necessary knowledge and abilities to respond swiftly and effectively in times of emergencies. Whether it's treating minor cuts and bruises, providing initial care for more serious injuries, or even saving lives, the importance of first aid skills cannot be overstated. By acquiring these skills, Service members not only become more responsible and capable individuals, but they also contribute to creating a safer environment for themselves and others.
