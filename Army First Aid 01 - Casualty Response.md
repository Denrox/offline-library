# Chapter 1: Casualty Response

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Casualty Response Overview

1-1. **_Casualty response_ is the immediate and organized care, evacuation and reporting of those injured or killed in action.** Casualty response is a leader driven system designed to synchronize the planning, rehearsal, and execution of individual and collective tasks required to complete the mission while improving casualty outcomes. Due to the nature of military operations both at home and in combat, nonmedical Service members must sometimes rely on themselves and other Service members to render first aid until medical assistance arrives. Self-aid, buddy aid, and combat lifesaver (CLS) support is normally the first care a casualty receives in an operational setting and is provided until medically trained personnel (combat medic, corpsman, or medical technician) arrive to begin treatment.

1-2. First responder care includes self-aid, buddy aid, CLS tasks, and Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC). Care provided by first responders is not limited to Service members but is provided to all those requiring initial treatment, stabilization, and when necessary, movement to higher roles of care for further treatment.

1-3. First responder care varies by Service, with the core training focused on TCCC. The application of TCCC occurs anywhere in the pre-hospital setting and can be tailored by the Joint Trauma System (JTS) to support Service-specific mission requirements. The TCCC training has four individual tiers or sets of responsibilities (see table 1-1 on page 2):

- Self-aid or buddy aid.

- Combat lifesaver.

- Combat medic or corpsman.

- Combat paramedic and provider.

1-4. Department of Defense Instruction (DODI) 1322.24, directs standardized combat casualty care training for all Service members and Department of Defense (DOD)-expeditionary civilians. According to DODI 1322.24, all Service members will be trained and proficient in basic lifesaving TCCC skills. The TCCC skills replace legacy military first aid skills. This evidence-based approach to combat casualty care reflects the lessons of nearly two decades of war and has the potential to reduce preventable prehospital trauma-related deaths.

1-5. The JTS established the standard of care for TCCC or prehospital medicine in the operational environment for the DOD. The JTS is part of the Defense Health Agency (also known as DHA) providing clinical practice guidelines (CPG)s and performance improvement for all levels of military trauma care. The Committee on Tactical Combat Casualty Care (CoTCCC) also supports the JTS. The committee is composed of representatives from the Army and Air Force and are responsible for developing CPGs for the delivery of TCCC in the operational environment.

## Terminology

1-6. A thorough understanding of the fundamentals of casualty response can save a life, prevent permanent disability, or reduce long periods of hospitalization. Knowledge of key terms used in Army and joint doctrine helps Service members better understand and appreciate the role they play in providing first aid in tactical and nontactical environments. Knowledge of the fundamentals of casualty response and key terminology also helps Service members understand when and how to provide first aid in all settings.

1-7. A _casualty_ is any person who is lost to the organization by having been declared dead, duty status – whereabouts unknown, excused absence-whereabouts unknown, missing, ill, or injured. (Joint Publication [JP] 4-02). The casualty classification is given to a Service member prior to treatment by medical personnel. A Service member who is wounded and conducts self-aid or is given treatment by a CLS is still classified as a casualty. That Service member is then moved to a casualty collection point (CCP) where medical personnel conduct triage and treatment of that casualty. Once treated by medical personnel, the Service member is now classified as a patient and enters the roles of medical care. Once a casualty is deceased, they become human remains and should be separated from the patient flow. The requirements for handling human remains are different and specifically explained in ATP 4-46. Service specific procedures should be followed when providing aid, evacuation, and reporting of combat casualties.

1-8. A _casualty collection point_ is a location that may or may not be staffed, where casualties are assembled for evacuation to a medical treatment facility with medical personnel (ATP 4-02.2). The CCPs are normally predesignated along the axis of advance or evacuation routes. Forward of the Role 1 battalion aid station (BAS), the combat medic or corpsman, CLS, and Service members take casualties to the CCPs. A _battalion aid station_ is the forward-most medically staffed treatment location organic to the maneuver battalion (ATP 4-02.4). These points facilitate acquisition of casualties by supporting ambulance teams and reducing evacuation time. When used by the BAS, CCPs help preserve BAS mobility, preclude carrying casualties forward, and reduce evacuation time to the sustainment area.

1-9. _Casualty evacuation_ is the unregulated movement of casualties aboard any vehicle. Also called CASEVAC (JP 4-02). The Army defines _casualty evacuation_ as the movement of casualties aboard nonmedical vehicles or aircraft without en route medical care (ATP 4-02.13). If a casualty is moved to a medical treatment facility (MTF) in a nonmedical vehicle without en route care provided by a medically trained Service member (such as a combat medic or corpsman) then the TCCC should be continually performed while the casualty is being transported. Casualties transported in this manner do not normally receive en route medical care. If the casualty is acquired by a dedicated medical vehicle with a medically trained crew, the role of first aid ceases, and the casualty becomes the responsibility of the Military Health System chain and is then referred to as a patient.

1-10. The core principles of casualty response include self-aid or buddy aid (first responder care), medical treatment (provided by combat medics, corpsmen, or medical technicians), and leader responsibilities (such as security, scene control, establishing a CCP, monitoring personnel, managing casualty movement, fulfilling reporting requirements, and coordinating evacuation). These principles must be thoroughly rehearsed and executed to maximize survivability in any operational environment. While these principles remain constant, the specific conditions in which they are applied can vary widely and are relevant to both military operations and civilian incident responses. This adaptability ensures that the response strategies are effective in different settings and enhances the overall efficacy of emergency management.

1-11. Joint Publication 4-02 defines _combat lifesaver_ as a Nonmedical Department of Defense person who has received additional trauma training and equipment, providing enhanced medical treatment beyond selfaid or buddy aid. (JP 4-02) **.** The United States (U.S.) Army defines a **_combat lifesaver_ as a Soldier trained and equipped to provide enhanced first aid as a secondary mission, known as CLS.** Enhanced first aid is the measures which require an additional level of training above self-aid and buddy aid. The CLS provides

this level of enhanced first aid and treats the most common causes of death on the battlefield, which are massive hemorrhage, airway and/or respiratory problems. The CLS also taught the skills to prevent complications and treat others associated but not life-threatening injuries. A minimum of one individual per squad, crew, team, or equivalent-sized unit should be trained. The primary duty of this individual does not change. The additional duty of a CLS is to provide enhanced first aid for injuries based on their training before the combat medic or corpsman arrives.

1-12. **_First aid_ is the immediate lifesaving measures performed on casualties or military working animals.** Every Service member is trained to be proficient in a variety of specific first aid procedures on injured personnel. This training enables the Service member or a buddy to apply immediate first aid measures to alleviate a life-threatening situation on an injured person. Select Soldiers are trained to perform first aid on military working animals. For additional information on first aid for military working animals refer to the deployed medicine website.

1-13. A _first responder_ is anyone who provides initial and immediate treatment to self or others. (JP 4-02) First responders play a crucial role in casualty response by providing immediate aid, including hemorrhage control and airway management, and improving the chances of survival for wounded personnel in the operational environment.

1-14. _Medical evacuation_ is the timely and effective movement of the wounded, injured, or ill to and between medical treatment facilities on dedicated and properly marked medical platforms with en route care provided by medical personnel. Also called MEDEVAC (ATP 4-02.2).

1-15. A _medical treatment facility_ is a facility established for the purpose of furnishing medical and/or dental care to eligible individuals. Also called MTF (JP 4-02). The Army defines a _medical treatment facility_ as any facility established for the purpose of providing medical treatment (FM 4-02). This includes battalion aid stations, Role 2 facilities, dispensaries, clinics, and hospitals.

1-16. Medical treatment is the care and management of wounded, injured, or ill Service members by medically trained, certified or credentialed personnel. It may include emergency medical treatment (EMT), advanced trauma management, and resuscitative and surgical intervention.

1-17. Joint services define a _patient as_ a sick, injured, wounded, or other person receiving medical care, dental care, or other medical treatment (JP 4-02). The U.S. Army defines a patient as a wounded, ill and injured Soldier who receives medical care or treatment from medically trained personnel (ATP 4-02.10). Once the casualty has entered the medical continuum of care (combat medic, corpsman, evacuation crew, or MTF), the role of first aid in the care of the casualty ceases and the casualty becomes the responsibility of the Military Health System. Once the casualty has entered the Military Health System they are referred to as a patient.

1-18. _Tactical Combat Casualty Care_ is the deliberate integration of tactics and medicine to treat casualties during combat. Also called TCCC (JP 4-02). The unit standard operating procedure (SOP) and operation order should address TCCC procedures and casualty evacuation (CASEVAC) in detail to include aid for chemical casualties with particular emphasis on lifesaving tasks. The SOP should cover the duties and responsibilities of essential personnel, the evacuation of chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) contaminated casualties (on routes separate from non-contaminated casualties), and priority for operating essential weapons and positions. The SOP should specify preferred and alternate methods of evacuation, and the SOP should make provisions for retrieving and safeguarding the weapons, ammunition, and equipment of casualties. Slightly wounded personnel are treated at the appropriate role of care and returned to duty as soon as possible. CASEVAC and medical evacuation (MEDEVAC) should be rehearsed like other critical parts of an operation.

1-19. _Triage_ is the system of sorting and prioritizing casualties based on the tactical situation, mission, and available resources (FM 4-02). Casualties are sorted and categorized in one of the treatment categories (immediate [I], delayed [D], minimal [M], and expectant [E]) prior to being taken to the treatment areas. This sorting occurs at the triage point and is called the "IDME" or the "DIME." See paragraph 3-87 on page 38 for additional information on the triaging of casualties.

## Casualty Collection Point Operations

1-20. When designating a CCP, the unit commander determines if medical personnel will be at the CCP. This decision is based upon the assessment of risk versus the availability of personnel. Medical personnel may not be available to staff these points, and CLS and ambulatory patients may be required to perform self-aid, buddy aid, or CLS support. The CCPs should be identified on watercraft, operational overlays, and planned by phase for operations. The CCP planning considerations include site security, proximity to the landing zone (LZ), cover and concealment, and access to evacuation routes. Leaders should provide guidance on aid and litter teams, distribution of casualty equipment, and proper avoidance of choke points. Medical personnel are responsible for activity inside the CCP, and the supported unit leaders are responsible for activity outside the CCP.

1-21. It is essential for units to meticulously plan the movement of casualties from the point of injury (POI) to supporting Role 3 MTF. Medical staff and unit leaders must be well-versed in the flow of casualties, extending their understanding at least two levels above their own. This encompasses knowledge of medical regulating, casualty tracking, and hospitalization protocols. For instance, platoon leaders and their medics should clearly know the path a casualty takes once they depart from the company CCP and BAS. Similarly, company leaders and senior medics should be familiar with the trajectory of a casualty beyond the BAS and through the Role 2 MTF.

1-22. Moreover, it is vital to consider potential changes in the designated Role 3 MTF, which may occur when crossing a phase line or when the capacity of a facility is reached. Medical personnel must stay informed about these variables and regularly update themselves on their current status. This will ensure that the casualty care and evacuation chain remain efficient and responsive to dynamic operational conditions.

1-23. Leaders must be able to answer the following questions to establish the tactical casualty flow:

- Where are the casualties being evacuated to?

- Will ground, air, or water assets conduct evacuation to the CCP?

- How will evacuation be conducted to the CCP?

- What are the distances and times of travel?

- Will expected casualties be able to make it that far? If not, what parts of the plan need to be corrected?

- Who will evacuate the casualties (CASEVAC or MEDEVAC)?

- Are medical assets positioned to ensure continuity of care?

### Site Selection

1-24. The CCP layout (see figure 1-1 on page 6) site is addressed during the planning phase of the military decision-making process, or troop leading procedures (commonly known as TLPs). Tactical battle plans will vary depending on the type and number of units incorporated in the plan. Mission variables are always a factor for CCP site selection during operations.

**Figure 1-1. Casualty collection point layout in an open area or field**

1-25. The location of the CCP will depend on the unit location, the tactical situation, and the number of casualties to be evacuated. The unit first sergeant determines the location of the company CCP, or the platoon sergeant for the platoon CCP with guidance from the respective combat medics. Battle drills and the tactical SOP should be established on how casualties will be moved from the POI to the CCP.

1-26. The company CCP should be in a covered and concealed position in the company trains area while the platoon CCP should be located at the platoon’s rear. The CCP should be:

- Away from natural or enemy choke points.

- Covered and concealed.

- Expandable if casualty load increases.

- In a building or on a hardstand (exclusive CCP building limits confusion).

- In an area with good drainage.

- Inside the perimeter and allow for passive security.

- Near lines of patient drift, or natural routes along which wounded Soldiers may be expected to go back for medical care from a combat position.

- Near and have access to evacuation routes (foot, vehicle, and aircraft).

- Near templated areas of expected high casualties.

- Next to objective choke points.

- Reasonably close to the fight.

- Trafficable to evacuation assets.

**_Note._** “Lines of Drift” are paths of least resistance that offer the greatest ease while considering obstacles and modes of transit to the objective.

1-27. Establishing a CCP in a building (see figure 1-2 on page 7) may be the only choice when conducting operations in urban areas. After the unit ensures that the building is cleared and secured, leaders—

- Determine the structural, environmental, medical, and CBRN hazards prior to receiving casualties.

- Determine location of control point and triage.

- Minimize congestion.

- Remove or relocate furniture or obstructions.

- Consider rigid litter or non-rigid pole-less litter for movement.

- Use largest rooms possible.

- Separate treatment categories by room. If triage categories are separated by walls, it is more difficult to monitor all the casualties.

- Color-code and mark rooms by treatment categories.

1-28. The CCP site selection is addressed during troop leading procedures and the CCP SOP should be rehearsed. The CCP should be established with:

- A day and night marking system.

- A triage point for entry into and exit from the CCP to track casualties and control access.

- Properly marked evacuation categories for day and night (non-tactical or tactical) operations.

- A killed in action (KIA) area away from the other casualties.

- A medical resupply area.

- A helicopter landing zone (commonly known as HLZ).

1-29. The first sergeant or platoon sergeant is responsible for casualty flow and everything outside the CCP. They—

   - Provide for CCP structure and organization (color coded per tactical SOP for both day and night).

   - Maintain command and control and situational awareness.

   - Control aid and litter teams.

   - Ensure security is established and maintained.

   - Remove, collect, identify, organize, and maintain casualty equipment outside of the treatment area.

   - Track casualties and equipment into and out of CCP.

   - Establish accountability and send reports to higher.

   - Move casualties through CCP entrance and exit control point.

- 1-30. Medical personnel are responsible for operations inside the CCP. Medical personnel assigned to the CCP include the:

• Triage officer or noncommissioned officer who sorts and organizes the casualties into appropriate treatment categories.

- Medics, corpsman, or medical technicians who organize medical equipment and supplies.

- • Medical personnel, CLS, and aid and litter teams who assist with treatment and packaging of casualties.

1-31. Once triaged, casualties categorized as "Minimal" should ideally stay with their assigned unit or assist with security at the CCP, if possible. Deceased personnel are designated as KIA. Their remains should stay with the assigned unit until mortuary affairs personnel arrive, or until the remains are transferred to a morgue. The mortuary affairs SOP should clearly outline the handling of the remains, considering theater-specific requirements, environmental factors, and the religious beliefs of the Service member.

1-32. It is also important to note that the remains of deceased personnel should not be moved with patients on MEDEVAC ambulances. This practice helps maintain the dignity of the deceased and ensures the focus remains on providing urgent care to the living casualties during MEDEVAC.

## Casualty Response for United States Army Units

1-33. The treatment and evacuation of casualties are difficult tasks for an organization to execute due to the operating distance away from its higher headquarters. Units should allocate adequate assets to the platoon to assist in casualty treatment and evacuation. Platoon medics evaluate and treat sick and injured Soldiers and assist the unit in the evacuation of casualties.

### Casualty Care

1-34. When casualties occur, the platoon must provide aid to those wounded in action. Combat medics, who are assisted by nonmedical personnel, provide EMT to alleviate potentially life-threatening injuries and to ensure maximum survivability. This support is commonly provided by enlisted personnel and includes first aid (self-aid or buddy aid), enhanced first aid by the CLS, and EMT by the platoon medic.

#### Wounded in action

1-35. The ability of fellow Soldiers to provide aid to wounded comrades is heavily influenced by the tactical situation. Minimizing casualties is more achievable when Soldiers concentrate on defeating or neutralizing the enemy responsible for causing the injuries.

1-36. During combat, it is crucial to move casualties to a covered and concealed position where first aid can be administered safely. Once the immediate threat subsides, squad leaders should coordinate the evacuation of the wounded to the platoon CCP, typically located to the rear of the platoon's position. At the CCP, the platoon medic will perform triage, stabilize the injured, and initiate their transfer to more comprehensive medical facilities. For detailed operations at the CCP, refer to paragraph 1-20 on page 4.

1-37. Before evacuating casualties to the CCP or further, leaders must ensure that:

- The casualty's personal protective equipment, such as protective masks, accompanies them.

- All vital operational, sensitive items, and equipment are removed from the casualty.

- Medical documentation and identification tags are securely attached to each casualty to streamline medical treatment and tracking processes.

1-38. Additionally, the removal of equipment should include items like automated network control devices, global positioning systems, maps, position-locating devices, and laser pointers. Each unit should have an SOP for managing the weapons and ammunition of its wounded personnel, ensuring these measures are systematically applied, and adhere to safety and operational security standards.

#### Killed in Action

1-39. The platoon leader designates a location for the collection of KIAs. Personal effects remain with the human remains. While treating the human remains with dignity and respect, squad leaders remove, and safeguard all equipment, and government issued items until they can turn the equipment over to the platoon sergeant. The platoon sergeant turns over the human remains to the first sergeant. The platoon should not transport human remains on the same vehicle as wounded Soldiers. The human remains are normally transported to the rear on empty resupply trucks but are dependent on unit SOP.

1-40. Commanders and first sergeants establish command procedures to ensure the Soldier’s next of kin are properly notified in accordance with (IAW) ATP 4-46/MCRP 3-40G.3/NTTP 4-06/AFTTP 3-2.51. The potential for unofficial communications exists with the KIA and casualty operations. The use of cell phones and computers in proximity to the area of operation enables many Soldiers to contact their home station regarding the casualty. This kind of communication is unofficial and unacceptable. The next of kin for Soldiers wounded in action or KIA should not receive notification through unofficial means. Therefore, the use of cell phones and computers in proximity to the area of operation should be subject to a communication blackout until the next of kin is notified by a casualty notification officer. No internet or phone calls home should be permitted during the communication blackout.

### Evacuation Considerations

1-41. Conducting CASEVAC is a fundamental unit responsibility and a key aspect of sustainment operations. To effectively execute CASEVAC, unit leaders should consider the following points to optimize their planning and response:

- Know the Evacuation Platform:

   - Determine if a medical provider is onboard.

   - Check for the presence of necessary monitoring equipment.

   - Ascertain the capacity of the platform regarding how many ambulatory and littler casualties it can evacuate.

- Understand Platform Specifications:

   - Large Platforms: Evacuate multiple casualties, with routine cases loaded first, followed by priority, and critical (urgent) cases last, ensuring they are first off at the destination.

   - Small Platforms: Prioritize evacuation of critical (urgent) and priority cases first, with routine cases evacuated as space allows.

   - Verify that all securement devices are functional and appropriate for the type of litter and evacuation platform being used.

- Packaging Requirements for the Evacuation Platform:

   - Identify the types of litters available.

   - Determine if the litter is stirrup-based verses non-stirrup-based pole-less and is compatible with the evacuation platform.

   - Ensure there is enough equipment to prevent hypothermia and keep the casualty warm and dry.

- Secure the Casualties and Determine the Loading Sequence on the Evacuation Platform:

   - Implement procedures for tying down or securing litters to the vehicle, ensuring safety during transportation.

   - Ensure a clear and prioritized loading sequence to streamline the evacuation process.

   - Plan and coordinate the movement of casualties to the evacuation platform efficiently.

1-42. By addressing these areas and integrating enhancements such as leading in conducting CASEVAC and ensuring all litters are securely tied down, leaders can improve the efficiency and safety of evacuation operations, ultimately enhancing casualty care during critical situations.

#### Medical Evacuation

1-43. A MEDEVAC may be provided by dedicated air or ground ambulances. The MEDEVAC includes the provision of en route medical care, whereas CASEVAC may not provide medical care during movement. For more information on MEDEVAC refer to ATP 4-02.2. If dedicated MEDEVAC platforms (ground and air) are available, casualties should be evacuated on these conveyances to ensure they receive proper en route medical care. If both MEDEVAC and CASEVAC are used, the most seriously injured should be evacuated on MEDEVAC platforms while the less seriously injured are moved by CASEVAC.

1-44. When possible, medical platoon ambulances provide evacuation and en route care from POI or the platoon or company’s CCP to the BAS. The ambulance team supporting the company works in coordination with the senior combat medic supporting the platoons. In mass casualty situations, nonmedical vehicles may be used to assist in CASEVAC as directed by the company commander or leader. However, plans for use of nonmedical vehicles to perform CASEVAC should be included in the unit SOP and briefed at the sustainment and combines arms rehearsals.

1-45. Ground MEDEVAC is the primary means of evacuation during large-scale combat operations due to the operational environment and the greater availability of ground ambulances. Aeromedical evacuation is the preferred method of evacuation due to the greater speed and range of air ambulances. Mission variables should be considered before executing aeromedical evacuation. When selecting a site for a LZ, the LZ should be relatively flat and free of hazards such as power lines. Loose debris should be removed to avoid objects being sucked into engines or causing injury due to the rotor wash. The platoon provides local security of the LZ until the evacuation is complete.

1-46. The senior military person present determines whether to request evacuation. The decision to request MEDEVAC is based on the advice of the senior medical person at the scene, the patient’s condition, the tactical situation, and availability of a dedicated medical platform. Casualties will be picked up as soon as possible, consistent with available resources and pending missions. The following are evacuation priority categories of precedence and criteria used in their assignment:

- Priority I – Urgent is assigned to emergency cases being evacuated as soon as possible and within a maximum of one hour to save life, limb, or eyesight; to prevent complications of serious illness; or to avoid permanent disability.

- Priority IA – Urgent Surgical is assigned to patients that should be evacuated as soon as possible and within a maximum of one hour who must receive far forward surgical intervention to save life, limb, or eyesight and stabilize for further evacuation.

- Priority II – Priority is assigned to sick and wounded personnel requiring prompt medical care. The precedence is used when the individual should be evacuated within four hours or if the medical condition could deteriorate to such a degree that the casualty becomes urgent, or whose requirements for special treatment is not available locally; the individual will suffer unnecessary pain or disability.

- • Priority III – Routine is assigned to sick and wounded personnel requiring evacuation, but condition is not expected to deteriorate significantly. The sick and wounded in this category should be evacuated within 24 hours.

- Priority IV – Convenience is assigned to patients for whom evacuation by medical vehicle is a matter of medical convenience rather than necessity.

**_Note._** The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) Standardization Agreement (STANAG) 3204 has deleted the category of Priority IV - CONVENIENCE. There is a requirement for it in an operational environment and is still included in the United States Army evacuation priorities.

#### Medical Evacuation Request

1-47. Immediately following an attack and upon learning of a casualty within the platoon, the vehicle commander, squad or section leader, and platoon sergeant will start to prepare an evacuation request. There is no difference between a ground or air evacuation request. Units transmit a 9-line MEDEVAC request, report number M030 IAW FM 6-99 to the command post. Units use Graphic Training Aid 08-01-004, _MEDEVAC Request Card_ , for the correct reporting lines and brevity codes for United States Forces. Units use this report to request evacuation of sick and wounded personnel. This report is like the NATO MEDEVAC request (9-liner MEDEVAC) IAW STANAG 2020 and STANAG 2627. The NATO 9-liner MEDEVAC request is used only when specifically directed in the orders process.

1-48. Along with the standard 9-line MEDEVAC request format, the higher role of care may require a mechanism of injury, injury type, signs and symptoms, and treatments (MIST) report, which should be provided by the platoon medic or CLS on-site. The Department of Defense (DD) Form 1380 ( _Tactical Combat Casualty Care (_ TCCC _) Card)_ , when complete, is the MIST report. For more information on MEDEVAC request process see paragraph 3-62 on page 34, or refer to ATP 4-02.2.

1-49. For casualties in areas not supported by MEDEVAC or when MEDEVAC assets are unavailable, various multimodal patient movement (MMPM) or CASEVAC assets are employed to conduct the evacuation mission, with MMPM being the preferred method of evacuation.

#### Casualty Evacuation

1-50. Service members may have to be available to perform or assist in an extraction, rescue, and evacuation of casualties. There are multiple extrication devices, tactical situations, environmental situations, and maneuvers to accomplish appropriate movement for a casualty. For detailed information on casualty movement, refer to ATP 4-02.13.

### Warning

**Casualties transported in this manner may not receive proper en route medical care or be transported to the appropriate MTF to address the patient’s medical condition.**

**If the casualty’s medical condition deteriorates during transport, or the casualty is not transported to the appropriate MTF, an adverse impact on the casualty’s prognosis and long-term disability or death may result.**

1-51. CASEVAC is the responsibility of the unit to transport less severely injured casualties when MEDEVAC resources are limited. The planning of CASEVAC is synchronized with existing medical support plans to ensure a streamlined process. This involves organizing, preparing, coordinating, and practicing the evacuation procedures in advance to guarantee efficiency. The plan ensures that teams trained in first aid are adept at handling stretchers, working around medical posts, and correctly assessing triage categories. Whenever possible, non-medical vehicles, aircraft, and watercraft used for transporting casualties should be supported by CLS or medical staff to provide necessary care during transport.

1-52. Sufficient space may not be available to permit a caregiver to accompany or assess the casualties. Refer to ATP 4-02.13 for more information. The type of en route monitoring and medical care or first aid provided is limited by the following factors:

- Skill level of the individual providing care. The combat medic is qualified to provide EMT, while the CLS is trained to provide enhanced first aid. The combat medic can provide emergency medical interventions, whereas the CLS can only monitor the casualty and ensure the basic lifesaving first aid tasks are accomplished.

- Medical equipment available.

- Number of casualties being transported.

- Accessibility of casualties. If a nonstandard evacuation vehicle is loaded with the maximum number of casualties, the combat medic or CLS may not be able to attend to the casualties while the vehicle is moving. If the condition of a casualty deteriorates and emergency measures are required, the vehicle may need to be stopped for the combat medic or CLS to provide additional care.

### Multimodal Patient Movement

1-53. When medical personnel provide care during the transport of casualties on non-medical platforms, it's called MMPM. MMPM involves moving casualties efficiently and providing care en route using available transportation methods, as outlined in JP 4-02. MMPM platforms can include maritime, land, or air vehicles, and they may serve as the primary or secondary mode of transportation between care facilities. However, utilizing these platforms means accepting increased risks for both patients and transport teams due to their combatant status. To ensure safe patient movement, MMPM platforms may carry standardized medical kits, emphasizing the importance of thorough planning and preparation.

### Unit Reporting

1-54. A casualty report is filled out when a casualty occurs, or as soon as the tactical situation permits. The casualty report is usually completed by the Soldier’s squad leader and turned into the platoon sergeant, who forwards it to the first sergeant. The report should include a brief description of how the casualty occurred (including the place, time, and activity being performed) and who or what inflicted the wound is included. If

the squad leader does not have personal knowledge of how the casualty occurred, they get this information from Soldiers who have the knowledge. For further discussion on human resources support to casualty operations, refer to ATP 1-0.1 _._

1-55. As casualties occur, the nearest observer informs the platoon sergeant who then informs the first sergeant via the most expedient method available (such as a free text within Mission Command Systems or a radio voice). The first sergeant submits a personnel status report to the Battalion S-1 section. This report documents duty status changes on all casualties. Casualties are taken to the CCP for classification of injury type (urgent, priority, routine, or return to duty), evacuation, and integration into the medical treatment system.

#### Medical and Personnel Accounting

1-56. When a Soldier becomes a casualty, the platoon combat medic or senior combat medic records the medical treatment the Soldier receives on the DD Form 1380. The BAS and the Role 2 read the Soldier’s DD Form 1380, when they treat the Soldier. The Battalion S-1 should electronically receive a notification message to update the Soldier’s patient tracking status. In turn, this message should be forwarded to the Soldier’s company. In this manner, the casualty’s location can be determined, and the Soldier can be properly accounted for by their unit.

#### Personnel Reports

1-57. Units employ personnel reports to inform the command group about casualties and to initiate requests for replacement personnel. Typically, a request for replacements is made immediately following a casualty report. This systematic approach ensures that the command group remains fully informed about personnel status and can quickly respond to the need for additional forces to maintain operational effectiveness. The prompt communication of these details is crucial for strategic planning and the swift adaptation to evolving battlefield conditions.

#### Personnel Battle Loss Report

1-58. Units transmit a Casualty Report (known as CASREP), report number C001, also known as the personnel battle loss report, IAW FM 6-99 to the command post as casualties occur. This report is like the NATO notification of casualty (stage 1) (NOTICAS1) IAW STANAG 2020. The unit completes a DA Form 1156 ( _Casualty Feeder Card)_ with witness statements and submits the entire packet to the first sergeant. The personnel battle loss report is an interim report to update information sent in the last personnel daily summary report.

1-59. The DA Form 1156 is used to report those Soldiers who have been killed and recovered or those who have been wounded. This form is also used to report Soldiers who are KIA, missing, captured, or not recovered. The Soldier with the most knowledge of the incident should complete the witness statement. This information is used to inform the Soldier’s next of kin, and to provide a statistical base for analysis of friendly or enemy tactics. For more information on the Army casualty program refer to Army Regulation (AR) 638-8.

1-60. Once the casualty’s medical condition has stabilized, the company commander may write a letter to the Soldier’s next of kin. During lulls in the battle, the platoon forwards casualty information to the company headquarters. The first sergeant ensures a completed DA Form 1156 is forwarded to the Battalion S-1, who then enters the data into the defense casualty information processing system.

### Casualty Response Rehearsals

1-61. During the troop leading procedures, the organization rehearses the CCP plan per unit SOP. Unit leaders and planners include the leaders from the medical platoon in the military decision-making process, orders production and in CCP rehearsals. This ensures the evacuation plans mutually support the unit’s mission and the medical tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) are understood. Some areas to concentrate on when conducting a casualty response rehearsal are:

- Alerting aid and litter teams, and conduct:

   - Alert and movement.

   - Evacuation equipment preparation.

   - Clearing and securing weapons.

- CCP members conduct:

   - Clear and secure CCP location.

   - Triage Point.

   - Marking and tagging.

   - Accountability and reporting.

   - Equipment removal, tagging, and consolidation.

1-62. Casualty response is a portion of the company’s sustainment plan that the platoon and squad must plan, prepare, and execute. Concurrently with other operational planning, the platoon develops its sustainment plan during mission analysis and refines it in the war gaming portion of the troop leading procedures; and should describe the duties and responsibilities of personnel in relation to casualty response. Rehearsals are normally conducted at each echelon of command to ensure the smooth, continuous flow of materiel and services.

### Duties and Responsibilities

1-63. Sustainment responsibilities for the maneuver platoon and squad include requesting support requirements through the company and ensuring sustainment operations are properly executed when support elements arrive in the platoon area. The platoon sergeant is normally in charge of these functions, with guidance and oversight provided by the platoon leader. The platoon sergeant must submit accurate personnel and logistical reports, along with any other necessary information and requests.

**_Note._** The duties and responsibilities of leaders and platoon members must be executed even in the absence of a particular leader to ensure mission accomplishment is IAW the commander’s intent.

#### Medical Planners

1-64. During the planning phase, battalion medical personnel and planners play a critical role by:

- Providing recommendations and guidance to leaders on the Army Health System support plan.

- Collaborating with unit leaders to coordinate various aspects, including:

   - Identifying CCP locations for subordinate units throughout different phases.

   - Organizing medical tasks and their distribution.

   - Developing plans for on-objective ground evacuation and off-objective air and ground evacuation.

   - Ensuring security measures for HLZ and evacuation platforms.

   - Assessing the need for additional support from subordinate units.

   - Establishing primary, alternate, and contaminated ground evacuation routes.

   - Determining patient decontamination requirements.

   - Coordinating external medical support provided to the battalion.

   - Identifying instances during the operation when CASEVAC may supplement MEDEVAC ambulances.

- Establishing connections with tactical operations to ensure seamless coordination.

1-65. During the execution phase, battalion medical personnel and planners carry out essential tasks with efficiency and care. They prioritize patient care by triaging, treating, monitoring, and preparing individuals for evacuation as needed. Delegation of treatment is managed effectively to ensure resources are used optimally. In situations requiring additional support, they can request assistance from other medical or platoon assets. Furthermore, they provide valuable guidance and recommendations to leaders on casualty management, aiding in informed decision-making during challenging circumstances.

#### Platoon Leader

1-66. The platoon leader is responsible for their platoon’s execution of the sustainment plan at platoon level. They plan and relay support requirements for mission accomplishment to the company headquarters where it is consolidated and passed on.

1-67. The platoon leader, assisted by the platoon sergeant, is responsible for developing their sustainment plan. This starts with checking their current Class VIII supplies in order to find out exactly what is available. Knowing what they have on hand helps them accurately estimate their support needs. It’s crucial for the commander to be aware of these supplies so they can prepare for treating expected casualties and identify critical supplies. This verification is key not only to confirm the sustainment plan's accuracy but also to ensure that the platoon’s support requests are made as early as possible. Based on this inventory and their maneuver plans, the platoon leader can then create and submit their sustainment execution plan and support requests.

1-68. The sustainment plan should address key operational considerations, including:

- Types of support: Determine the specific medical support needed based on operational requirements and tactical factors.

- Quantities: Assess the quantities of support required, including potential needs for Class VIII resupply or adjustments to preconfigured Class VIII packages.

- Threat assessment: Analyze the composition, disposition, and capabilities of the expected enemy threat, considering factors such as anticipated contact locations, expected casualties, and the impact of enemy special weapons capabilities.

- Terrain and weather: Evaluate how terrain and weather conditions will impact the sustainment plan, including considerations for securing maintenance areas and CCPs, determining CASEVAC routes, and planning for contaminated personnel and equipment evacuation.

- Time and location: Identify when and where sustainment support will be needed, including— selecting optimal locations for CCPs, enemy prisoner of war collection points, and determining travel times to medical treatment facilities.

- Requirements: Define support requirements by element and type, including prioritization of medical support and emergency resupply.

- Risk assessment: Assess whether lulls in the battle will provide opportunities for support elements to conduct resupply operations safely.

- Resupply techniques: Select the most suitable resupply technique based on information gathered during the sustainment planning process.

1-69. During the planning phase, unit leaders undertake various crucial tasks to ensure effective medical support:

- Allocate aid and litter teams and CASEVAC equipment to facilitate timely response to casualties.

- • Conduct rehearsals for casualty responses to ensure readiness and effectiveness in handling medical emergencies.

- Perform precombat inspections of medics, platoon casualty response kits, and CLS bags to verify equipment and readiness. Casualty response kits are unit dependent tailored to their specific mission that would include appropriate litters, litter straps, panel markers, hypothermia prevention kits or extra medical supplies.

- Establish an accountability and reporting plan to track medical resources and casualties accurately.

- • Determine the CASEVAC plan for each phase of the operation, including CCP, helicopter landing zone, and ambulance exchange point locations.

- Organize the gathering and distribution of casualty equipment and sensitive items as needed throughout the operation.

- Establish accountability procedures and submit reports to higher commands as required.

- Rehearse the proper management of KIA remains to ensure respect and dignity in handling.

- Identify and ensure the security of CCPs, HLZs, and ambulance exchange point locations to prevent disruptions to medical operations.

- Determine the task organization and distribution of medical personnel to optimize their effectiveness and coverage.

- Prepare evacuation requests and establish CASEVAC link-up points to facilitate smooth patient transfers.

- Assist medics with EMT augmentation and provide guidance in directing aid and litter teams to maximize medical support capabilities

#### Platoon Sergeant

1-70. The platoon sergeant holds a pivotal role as the primary sustainment planner, coordinator, and operator within the platoon, reporting directly to the platoon leader. They execute logistical plans, relying on established SOPs from both the platoon and company. Working closely with the platoon leader and squad leaders during mission preparations, the platoon sergeant determines specific support requirements aligned with the tactical plan and ensures their proper arrangement. Additionally, the platoon sergeant:

- Coordinates human resources support with the company first sergeant, covering personnel accountability reports, casualty reports, replacement operations, and other administrative tasks.

- Supervises the evacuation of casualties, unprivileged enemy belligerents, and damaged equipment.

- • Oversee the collection, initial identification, and evacuation of human remains to the mortuary affairs collection point.

1-71. As the second-in-charge, the platoon sergeant primarily follows instructions from the platoon leader. However, they typically establish and operate the unit's CCP, which involves directing aid teams and managing the CASEVAC process during operations. They also maintain the platoon's strength level information by consolidating casualty reports and orienting replacements.

1-72. In the event of wounded soldiers requiring evacuation, the platoon leader or platoon sergeant can take various actions:

- Coordinate ground evacuation with the nearest company.

- Request attachment of a dedicated ambulance from the company or battalion for operations forward of the larger element.

- Use organic platoon assets for evacuating wounded soldiers.

- Coordinate for Army aeromedical evacuation and aerial CASEVAC through the company or battalion channels.

#### Squad Leader

1-73. It is the squad leaders’ responsibility to ensure casualties receive immediate TCCC and then immediately notify the platoon leader or platoon sergeant of all casualties. The use of CLS is critical. As a minimum, one member of each squad should be trained and equipped as a CLS. The squad leader is responsible for ensuring the casualty feeder card is filled out properly and provided to the platoon sergeant, as well as requesting evacuation for their personnel.

#### Combat Medics

1-74. EMT procedures performed by combat medics encompass critical interventions such as airway management, establishing intravenous or intraosseous access, hemorrhage control, shock prevention or treatment, splinting fractures, hypothermia management, and medication administration for pain or infection.

1-75. Combat medics undergo training under the supervision of the battalion surgeon or physician assistant, focusing on medical tasks, while the medical platoon leader and platoon sergeant concentrate on tactical training. The platoon combat medic assumes responsibilities such as triaging injured personnel, conducting sick call screening, assisting in evacuations, training CLS, requisitioning medical supplies, recommending CCP locations, providing guidance on operational public health, and supporting CLS as needed.

1-76. Combat medics are assigned to the battalion medical platoon and attached to the maneuver companies, typically with one combat medic per platoon and one senior combat medic per company, strategically located for rapid medical treatment of casualties.

1-77. The company senior combat medic is positioned with the company trains, located 500 to 1000 meters. or one terrain feature to the rear of the company, ensuring immediate recovery and medical support, while remaining concealed from direct enemy fire. The company’s first sergeant or executive officer supervises evacuation activities and sustains operations within the platoon.

1-78. During the planning phase, the senior combat medic or platoon combat medic provides recommendations and plans for medical support, Army Health System support, casualty response, and

evacuation. They also coordinate CCP locations, medical task organization, evacuation plans, and conduct precombat inspections.

1-79. During the execution phase, the senior combat medic or platoon combat medic implements triage, treatment, and monitoring, delegates treatment, requests assistance from other medical or unit assets, and offers guidance on casualty management and evacuation to support effective medical care in combat situations.

#### Combat Lifesavers

1-80. Immediate, far-forward medical care is critical on a widely dispersed and dynamic battlefield to prevent casualties from succumbing to wounds. Due to the unpredictable nature of combat, medical personnel may not always reach and treat wounded soldiers promptly. CLS play a crucial role in bridging this gap, as they can stabilize various types of casualties and mitigate the deterioration of a wounded soldier's condition until medical personnel arrive. Acting as CLS is a secondary mission conducted when the tactical situation allows, and they render enhanced first aid when a casualty occurs, either through self-aid, buddy aid, or CLS intervention.

1-81. The CLS performs tier 2 TCCC skills, serving as a link between the tier 1 skills taught to all Soldiers and the advanced medical training, provided by combat medics or higher-level medical providers. This capability is organic and aims to fill the gap between basic life-saving skills and advanced medical care. Training CLS should be a priority for platoons to ensure that they can replicate essential medical techniques in the absence of a combat medic. Each squad or equivalent-sized unit should have at least one certified CLS, who must recertify annually according to AR 350-1.

1-82. The primary role of a CLS is to address the most common causes of death on the battlefield, such as a massive hemorrhage, airway, and/or respiratory issues. They are trained to provide immediate care to save lives, including controlling bleeding and performing needle chest decompression for tension pneumothorax. The CLS may assist combat medics in patient care, evacuation preparation, or support medical personnel during prolonged care situations when not engaged in combat duties.

1-83. A significant advantage of the CLS is their proximity to injured squad or crew members. During large scale combat operations, the battlefield is often spread out and fast-moving, making rapid casualty treatment challenging. CLS ensure that squad CLS bags and litters are properly maintained, identify medical supply shortages, participate in casualty treatment drills, use enhanced first aid skills in the field, and are familiar with CCP locations and tactical SOP for establishing them.

### Contaminated Casualty Care

1-84. In a CBRN environment, Soldiers may need to employ self-aid, immediate lifesaving interventions, and decontamination measures. This includes administering correct antidotes for nerve agent exposure and assuming the appropriate Mission-Oriented Protective Posture level. Further guidance on contaminated casualty care can be found in Chapter 20 of this publication and refer to ATP 4-02.7/AFTTP 3-42.3.

1-85. Buddy aid is crucial when individuals are unable to provide self-aid. In a CBRN environment, buddy aid actions involve:

- Treating immediate life-threatening injuries.

- Identifying the type of contamination (chemical, biological, or radiological).

- Administering appropriate medical CBRN defense material (prioritizing the casualty's own supplies).

- Decontaminating exposed skin and contaminated gear using various methods such as Reactive Skin Decontamination Lotion (RSDL) or the M291 Skin Decontamination Kit, which includes dry earth, bleach and water for chemical contamination, soap and water for chemical, biological, and radiological contamination, and brushing or vacuuming for radiological contamination.

- Assisting the casualty to do the remaining protective clothing, if required.

- Promptly moving the casualty to a decontamination point for further care.

1-86. For comprehensive guidance on patient decontamination procedures, refer to ATP 4-02.7/AFTTP 3- 42.3.

## Casualty Response for United States Marine Corps Units

1-87. United States Marine Corps (USMC), Marine air-ground task forces require unit SOPs and operation orders address TCCC procedures, CASEVAC, and in-detail aid for chemical casualties with particular emphasis on lifesaving tasks. Wounded personnel should be treated at the appropriate role of care and returned to duty when possible. Hospital corpsmen evaluate sick and injured Marines, provide treatment, and arrange evacuation if necessary. CASEVAC and MEDEVAC are rehearsed like other critical parts of any operation. Casualty management begins at the POI or onset of illness throughout triage, treatment, and transport to the next role of medical care to include outside Service capabilities as necessary.

### Casualty Care

1-88. Health Service Support (HSS) is the employment of medical forces in support of the warfighter to deliver on-demand healthcare capabilities and ensure a healthy, fit, and medically ready force; counters the health threat to the deployed force; provide critical care for and management of combat casualties.

1-89. The HSS directly supports the National Military Strategy of forward presence and power projection. It also strengthens the warfighting commander by providing essential care in theater and MEDEVAC of casualties across the continuum of care to MTFs in the continental United States for definitive care without sacrificing the quality of care.

#### Mission

1-90. The HSS mission is to minimize the effects that wounds, injuries, and diseases have on unit effectiveness, readiness, and morale. The mission is accomplished by an aggressive and proactive preventive medicine program that safeguards personnel against potential health risks and by establishing an HSS system that provides appropriate care from the POI or illness to the appropriate taxonomy of care.

#### Principles

1-91. The HSS principles are guides for planning, organizing, managing, and executing HSS. Seldom will all principles exert equal influence in each situation and the HSS is most effective when it can identify the priority principles and bring them to bear in support of the commander's mission. Refer to JP 4-02 for more information. The Marine Corps HSS system encompasses:

- Conformity. The medical plan must integrate and comply with the commander's plan.

- Proximity. The medical plan must provide HSS as close to combat operations as the tactical situation permits.

- Flexibility. The medical plan must shift HSS resources to meet changing requirements.

- Mobility. The medical plan must anticipate requirements for rapid movements of HSS units to support combat forces during operations.

- Continuity. The medical plan must provide optimum, uninterrupted care and treatment to the wounded, injured, and sick.

- Coordination. The medical plan must ensure that HSS resources in short supply are efficiently employed and used effectively to support the planned operations.

#### Casualty Care and Management

1-92. Marine Corps HSS deploys through task-organized units of employment to provide care throughout the continuum of healthcare. The taxonomy continuum of healthcare includes the following capabilities:

- Policy and resource acquisition.

- Prevention and protection.

- First responder.

- Damage Control Resuscitation.

- Damage Control Surgery.

- Theater hospitalization.

- Definitive care.

• En-route care.

#### Roles of Care for Marine Corps

1-93. Health Services Support within an area of operations is organized into roles of care that extend from forward to the rear of the area of responsibility. Each role of medical capability is designed to provide the mobility and capability that is required to meet basic health care needs of the supported operational units, phased treatment, casualty holding, and preparation for the evacuation of the sick, wounded, or injured.

1-94. Marine Corps operating forces' HSS is flexible to meet the demands of the mission, enemy forces, terrain, and other tactical situations. Furthermore, each role of care provides treatment capabilities in support of the role of care below it. Each capability of the HSS system is limited by four interacting factors:

- Urgency of the patients’ needs.

- Unit mobility.

- Capabilities of HSS personnel, equipment, and supplies.

- Workload of each HSS unit is related to its treatment capacity.

#### Point of Principal Treatment

1-95. The HSS system provides a continuum of care that begins at the POI and ends at the appropriate treatment facility. Casualties are evacuated through the HSS system until they arrive at a facility with the capability, time, and bed capacity to begin definitive intervention to return them to duty or prepare them for further evacuation. This is known as the point of principal treatment. The site of the principal treatment is determined simultaneously by the patient’s diagnosis, HSS unit’s capabilities, and workload.

#### Role 1 Care

1-96. Role 1 care is the first medical attention that military personnel receive and is also referred to as unit level medical care. This role of care includes immediate lifesaving measures, disease and nonbattle injury prevention and care, combat and operational stress preventive measures, and patient location and acquisition (collection).

##### _First Responder Self/Buddy Aid_

1-97. The first responder provides unit level medical care. This is comprised of personnel from a unit and its organic BAS or squadron aid stations. In the case of organizations without organic medical elements, unit level care is provided by medical elements at the regimental, group, or support squadron level or other designated medical elements. TCCC has three phases and occurs during a combat mission and is the military counterpart to prehospital EMT.

1-98. TCCC in the military is predominantly administered by enlisted personnel, encompassing a range of roles such as first responders, self-aid, buddy aid (first aid), CLS, unit corpsmen, independent duty corpsmen, and other medical personnel. TCCC emphasizes addressing the most probable threats, injuries, and conditions encountered in combat, focusing on a concise selection of interventions aimed at mitigating the most critical of these threats and conditions.

##### _Combat Lifesaver for Marine Corps_

1-99. The CLSs for the Marine Corps are nonmedical military personnel selected by their unit commander for additional training beyond basic first aid procedures. A minimum of two Marines should be trained per table of organization corpsmen. The primary duty of a CLS does not change. The additional duty as a CLS is to provide enhanced first aid for injuries, based on his training, before the corpsman arrives or to assist the corpsman. CLS training is normally provided by medical personnel organic to sustainment units. The senior medical person designated by the commander manages the CLS training program.

##### _Unit Hospital Corpsman_

1-100. The company or squadron corpsman represents the first point where a sick, injured, or wounded Marine receives care from trained medical personnel. If emergency or lifesaving measures are required before a Hospital Corpsman (HM) arrives, they must be performed by fellow Marines trained as a CLS, first responder self/buddy aid. Care from unit HM includes primary and secondary assessments, followed by emergency or lifesaving measures (such as establishing and maintaining an airway, control of bleeding, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), treatment for shock, and fracture stabilization). The HMs duties include basic medical skills, use of medical equipment and supplies, and initiation of requests for assistance and evacuation.

##### _The Aid Station_

1-101. The aid station is the Role 1 for the Marine Corps. Treatment at the aid station is distinguished by the skills of a physician, physician assistant, and an independent duty corpsman. Treatment at an aid station is provided based on a more comprehensive evaluation and treatment plan, which may include restoration of airway, use of intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and application of splints and bandages. These elements of medical management prepare patients for return to duty or evacuation to the appropriate role of care.

#### Assault Echelon

1-103. During the assault phase, HSS capabilities ashore are limited to organic elements within the Marine Air-Ground Task Force. First response medical care for assault forces is provided by self-aid, buddy aid, and hospital corpsmen of the landing force rifle platoons. When the tactical situation permits, BASs are established, and additional medical professionals provide care. The BAS is normally divided into two sections, with assigned battalion nonmedical litter bearers divided between them. One section lands with the battalion combat train and provides in-close support to the assault force. The second section lands with the field train and establishes interim evacuation stations until relieved by follow-on HSS elements.

1-104. Evacuation stations are typically expanded and staffed by support and medical battalion personnel, drawing assets from the shock trauma platoon or triage/evacuation platoons of the surgical companies. When established with the landing force support party (also known as LFSP), the supporting medical battalion

constitutes the beach evacuation section(s) of the landing force support party. The primary role of a BAS is to treat or evacuate assault force casualties to a designated casualty receiving and treatment ship. If the supporting medical battalion personnel come ashore with the landing force support party, they may supplement the beach evacuation section(s).

1-105. When evacuation stations attached to the landing force support party become operational ashore, established BASs are relieved to conduct their primary missions in supporting their parent battalions. The expansion of the HSS facilities follows the landing of supporting evacuation stations. The HSS assets are typically established at the beach service support area and follow-on combat service support areas, logistic support areas or forward arming and refueling points.

#### Assault Follow-on Echelon

1-106. While most logistic support capabilities during the assault follow-on echelon continue to be sea-based, projected HSS capabilities ashore expand along with the logistics combat element. Capabilities could be additional forward resuscitative surgery systems or shock trauma platoon with mobile combat logistics companies. When progress of assault units is such that the beachhead is relatively secure, HSS is enhanced from follow-on forces.

#### Follow-on Forces

1-107. The HSS shifts its posture to achieve shore-based health care consistent with the expected combat intensity and duration of sustained operations ashore, independent of sea-based facilities. This phasing is achieved by upgrading capabilities ashore by consolidating HSS capabilities once they come ashore. If a sustained land campaign is envisioned, additional HSS will normally be provided by theater hospitalization (expeditionary medical facilities, hospital ships, or other Service-equivalent facilities such as combat support hospitals and expeditionary medical support).

#### Casualty Evacuation

1-108. Timely movement of casualties from the operational environment is important for safety, care for the wounded, and troop morale. Unit leaders are responsible for coordination of CASEVAC from the POI to the appropriate role of care. The owning commands are responsible for CASEVAC from POI or illness to casualty treatment stations, and for casualty movement from the first responder capability to forward resuscitative care (also known as FRC) capabilities.

1-109. CASEVAC can be accomplished through dedicated, designated, or opportune ground or air transportation provided by a combination of litter carries, manual carries, ground transportation, and limited air transport (fixed- or rotary-wing).

1-110. When conducting operations ashore, the patient evacuation team (PET) coordinates patient movement of the patient with requesting unit by ground or air transport or a combination of the two, from POI to the appropriate forward resuscitative care and between various roles of care. The direct air support center and PET receive patient evacuation requests via the 9-line request (see paragraph 3-62 on page 34) over the tactical air request, helicopter request net, or alternate communication means. The PET then determines the appropriate means of patient movement and designated HSS facility.

1-111. If air is the appropriate evacuation means, the direct air support center coordinates air support. If ground evacuation is required or more appropriate, the PET informs the requesting unit to coordinate patient movement with the logistics staff section or logistics combat element. The PET will continue to assist as necessary and track all patient movement through completion of the mission. Depending on the size of the operation, the PET might only assume responsibility for patient movement from POI to forward resuscitative care, and the Marine logistics group HSS medical support operations center or higher regulating authority will assume movement above forward resuscitative care. This should be decided prior to deployment and written into the “Annex Q” (Medical Services) of the operational plan or operation order.
