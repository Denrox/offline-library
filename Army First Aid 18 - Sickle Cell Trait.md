# Chapter 18: Sickle Cell Trait

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Sickle Cell Trait

18-1. Sickle cell trait (SCT) is an inherited genetic condition in which a person carries one copy of the sickle cell gene and one normal hemoglobin gene. It is different from sickle cell disease (SCD), which occurs when a person inherits two copies of the sickle cell gene. The SCT is usually asymptomatic and generally does not cause health problems. However, under certain conditions, such as extreme physical exertion, high altitudes, or dehydration, individuals with SCT may experience complications.

18-2. SCD is a genetic disorder that affects the red blood cells, which are responsible for carrying oxygen throughout the body. It is caused by a mutation in the gene responsible for producing hemoglobin, a protein that enables red blood cells to carry oxygen. In individuals with SCD, the hemoglobin gene mutation leads to the production of abnormal hemoglobin, known as hemoglobin S. This abnormal hemoglobin causes red blood cells to become rigid and take on a sickle or crescent shape instead of their normal disc shape. These sickle-shaped cells can get stuck in small blood vessels, leading to various complications.

18-3. For more information on SCT and SCD, refer to the National Institute of Health website. Service members with SCT can typically participate fully in any physical activity. Under certain circumstances Service members with SCT may suffer negative effects such as muscle weakness, pain, and collapse. This collapse is termed exercise collapse associated with sickle cell trait (ECAST).

18-4. ECAST refers to a specific syndrome that can occur in individuals with SCT during or immediately after strenuous exercise. It is characterized by symptoms such as dizziness, weakness, fatigue, and in rare cases, collapse, or loss of consciousness. The ECAST can occur due to a mismatch between oxygen supply and demand in the muscles during intense physical activity.

18-5. The ECAST is a relatively rare occurrence, and most individuals with SCT can engage in regular physical activities without any issues. However, to minimize the risk of complications, individuals with SCT are advised to take precautions during exercise, such as staying well-hydrated, avoiding excessive heat or cold exposure, and gradually increasing the intensity and duration of physical activity. It is also recommended to consult with a health care professional for personalized guidance and address any concerns related to exercise.

18-6. The ECAST, as a cause of collapse in Service members, is considered because the relative risk of death is significantly higher in-Service members with SCT than those without. Early recognition and proper treatment of ECAST can increase survival and decrease complications. For more information on ECAST, refer to the American Medical Society for Sports Medicine website.

### Risk Factors

18-7. While the exact causes of ECAST are not fully understood, there are several risk factors that can contribute to its occurrence. These include:

- Strenuous exercise: Engaging in high-intensity or prolonged physical activity can increase the risk of ECAST.

- Dehydration: Inadequate fluid intake or excessive fluid loss through sweating can contribute to the risk of ECAST.

- Heat and humidity: Exercising in hot and humid environments can further exacerbate the risk of ECAST.

18-8. Preexisting medical conditions and environmental factors that may increase the likelihood of an ECAST episode include:

- Poor Hydration.

- Exercise-induced asthma.

- Dietary supplements contain stimulants.

- Excessive heat exposure.

- High altitude.

- High humidity.

- Inadequate rest between intense exercise periods (for example, prolonged wind sprints).

- Intense exercise for an extended period.

- Pre-exercise fatigue due to illness.

- Returning from prolonged leave after little physical activity.

- Inadequate acclimatization to environment.

### Signs and Symptoms

18-9. Service members experiencing ECAST may have—

- Dizziness or lightheadedness.

- Weakness or fatigue.

- Nausea or vomiting.

- Shortness of breath.

- Muscle pain or cramping.

- Paleness or cyanosis (bluish discoloration) of the skin.

- Collapse or loss of consciousness (rare cases).

- Increased pain and weakness in the muscles, especially the large muscles of the legs, buttock(s), and low back.

- Muscle tenderness.

- Slumping to the ground.

- Rapid breathing.

- Slightly elevated body temperature (usually less than 103° Fahrenheit).

18-10. Some of the differences between the signs and symptoms of ECAST and other causes of exercise related collapse include:

   - ⚫ Service members with ECAST will have soft, weak muscles while Service members with muscle cramps will have firm, tight muscles.

   - ⚫ Service members with cardiac issues experiencing an ECAST event may not be able to speak or think normally.

   - ⚫ Service members with ECAST will have a slightly elevated body temperature while Service members experiencing exertional heat stroke will have a core body temperature above 104° Fahrenheit.

   - ⚫ Service members with ECAST will usually be able to speak and think normally, while Service members suffering from heat illness may appear to be confused.

- 18-11. If someone experiences symptoms of ECAST, the following first aid measures can be taken:

   - Stop the activity: Immediately cease any physical exertion and allow the person to rest.

   - Move to a cool and shaded area: Transfer the individual to a cooler environment to minimize heat stress.

   - Rehydrate: Encourage the person to drink fluids to rehydrate, preferably water or sports drinks.

   - • Elevate the legs: If the person is conscious and able to do so, elevate their legs to improve blood flow and circulation.

   - Monitor vital signs: Keep an eye on the person's breathing, heart rate, and level of consciousness. Seek medical assistance if the symptoms worsen or persist.

### Preventive Measures

18-12. It is crucial to note that ECAST is a relatively rare occurrence, and most individuals with SCT can engage in regular physical activities without complications. However, taking preventive measures, such as staying well-hydrated, avoiding excessive heat exposure, and gradually increasing exercise intensity can help reduce the risk of ECAST. If an individual with SCT experiences recurrent or severe episodes of ECAST, it is recommended to consult with a health care professional who specializes in SCD for further evaluation and personalized guidance.

18-13. Early identification of SCT in Service members is needed to prevent ECAST this is done with a blood test. Defense Health Agency Procedural Instruction (DHA-PI) 6025.14, _Active Duty Service member (ADSM) Erythrocyte Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) Deficiency and Sickle Cell Trait Screening_ ; DODI 6465.01, _Erythrocyte Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase Deficiency, (G6PD) and Sickle Cell Trait Screening Programs_ ; and All Army Activities 094/2020, _Implementation of Army Sickle Cell Trait Screening of All Soldiers_ establish the Department of Defense’s, Defense Health Agency’s, and the Army’s procedures to implement erythrocyte G6PD deficiency and SCT screening at—

- Entry to the Military Services (at appointment, enlistment, or induction).

- Pre-deployment.

- Other points as indicated for validation of results.

18-14. Safe exercise practices that decrease the risk of ECAST include:

- Participate in exercise and conditioning activities year-round to maintain physical fitness.

- Gradually increase exercise intensity when starting a training regimen or returning to training after an absence, such as illness, injury, or extended leave.

- Take adequate recovery time between repetitive sprints or drills (for example, wind sprints or intervals).

- Acclimate to the altitude or temperature at which training will occur.

- Exercise for shorter periods and with less intensity during hot or humid conditions.

- Hydrate before, during, and after exercise.

- Understand the signs and symptoms of ECAST. If any symptoms arise, report immediately to the Holistic Health and Fitness staff, unit leaders, or health care personnel.

## Duties and Responsibilities

18-15. Preventing ECAST is a primary and critical responsibility within the military, given the demanding physical exertion and operational requirements faced by Service members. ECAST can have severe consequences on both the health and operational readiness of military personnel. Recognizing the significance of these risks, the military assumes a proactive stance in fulfilling its responsibilities. This commitment involves the identification of command, personal, medical and first responder responsibilities.

### Command Responsibilities

18-16. Commanders ensure that—

- Every Service member in their command has been screened for SCT.

- Every Service member with a positive SCT screening is issued and wears a red medical warning tag and receives a formal counseling from a unit or MTF, and health care provider to discuss their SCTpositive result.

- Service members receive educational information and counseling that includes:

   - Erythrocyte G6PD and SCT deficiency diagnosis, causes, complications, treatment, and strategies to mitigate symptoms.

   - Materials and resources that enable the Service member to self-identify symptoms experienced during exercise and know when to seek immediate medical attention.

- Service members without record of an SCT screening is referred to a lab for screening unless the Service member can provide documentation of a prior screening. The date and results of the SCT screening are annotated in the Army Medical Protection System and recorded in the Service member’s electronic medical record.

- Unit or MTF health care providers—

   - Train all unit medical personnel to recognize the signs of ECAST.

   - Train unit medical personnel and leaders on actions to take during an ECAST event.

   - Conduct an initial patient evaluation, provide treatment, and determine whether to call for urgent MEDEVAC, or immediately transport the individual to the nearest MTF.

- Discrete means of identifying SCT positive Service members are developed and added to the unit tactical SOP. Identification is like those used to identify Service members who have sustained cold and or heat related injuries.

### Service Member Responsibilities

18-17. Service members with a positive SCT screen are responsible for—

- Obtaining and wearing their red medical warning tags and unit SOP directed markings.

- Seeking formal counseling includes educational materials and resources to aid in their ability to selfidentify symptoms experienced during exercise and know when to seek immediate medical attention.

- Gradually increasing exercise intensity when starting a training regimen or returning to training after an absence, such as an illness or leave.

- Taking adequate time to recover between repetitive sprints or drills (for example, wind sprints or intervals).

18-18. Appropriate casualty response measures for suspected ECAST are to—

- Identify if the casualty has SCT (looking for a red identification tag or unit specific marking).

- Check the casualty’s pulse, breathing, and mental status.

- Remove the casualty from the environment.

- Cool the casualty (as indicated by weather conditions).

- Notify nearest medical personnel to assess the casualty for a suspected ECAST event.

- Call for urgent MEDEVAC or immediately transport the casualty to the nearest MTF.

- Notify the receiving MTF that the Service member is suspected of experiencing an ECAST.

**_Note._** Trained medical personnel should consider fluid and oxygen resuscitation.

18-19. Before returning to duty, Service members who have collapsed during exercise showed signs or symptoms of ECAST will (if not already completed) be tested for SCT. Tests for other diseases such as diabetes, electrolyte abnormalities, muscle damage, and heart and lung conditions may also be needed.

18-20. Service members will meet with a unit or MTF health care provider to discuss:

- The signs and symptoms of ECAST.

- Family history.

- Environmental factors.

- Dietary supplements and medications that increase the risk of collapse.

- The importance of good hydration.

- The possibility of future ECAST episodes when exercising strenuously in high heat and humidity.

18-21. Service members must have no symptoms at rest and normal lab values before resuming exercise or training. The training must resume slowly and under a doctor’s supervision.

### First Responder, Stretcher Bearer, Medic, Corpsman, and Leader Training

18-22. The unit or MTF healthcare providers are tasked with training both first responders and medical personnel, such as combat medics or corpsmen, in the vital competencies of recognizing signs of ECAST. This training encompasses conducting rapid initial evaluations, providing immediate treatment, and making critical decisions regarding the need for urgent MEDEVAC or direct transport of the casualty to the nearest MTF.

18-23. Additionally, these healthcare providers also extend their training to unit leaders, equipping them with the necessary skills to identify the initial indicators of ECAST. This preparation is crucial for leaders to make informed decisions about whether to initiate an urgent MEDEVAC or to expedite the transportation of the affected individual to the closest medical facility, ensuring prompt and effective care.
