# Chapter 13: Fractures and Splinting

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Fractures

13-1. Prompt and appropriate management of fractures, or breaks in bones, is essential to minimize further harm and optimize the chances of survival for injured individuals. TCCC emphasizes a systematic approach to fracture care, incorporating principles such as rapid assessment, stabilization, and pain management. The goal is to provide efficient and effective treatment, considering the challenges of the tactical environment where resources may be limited, and evacuation to definitive care may be delayed. Mastery of fracture management in TCCC equips military personnel and first responders with the skills necessary to stabilize fractures, prevent complications, and facilitate the evacuation process, ultimately improving the overall outcomes for casualties in the field.

### Musculoskeletal System

13-2. The musculoskeletal system provides a structural framework for the body and, because bones are rigid, provides support and protection for vital organs and softer tissues. Skeletal muscles and bones work together to make body movement possible.

13-3. The musculoskeletal system is composed of—

- Bones.

- Joints.

- Muscles.

- Cartilage.

- Ligaments.

- Tendons.

#### ones

13-4. Bones are the substance that forms the skeleton of the body. It is composed chiefly of calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate. The human body has 206 bones. There are 80 axial (head and trunk) bones and 126 appendicular (upper and lower extremity) bones. Bones provide structural support for the body. Individual and groups of bones provide structure for the attachment of soft tissues and protect organs.

#### Joints

13-5. The human body's mobility hinges on its joints, where bones connect and facilitate movement. These pivotal junctures come in various forms, each serving distinct functions in enabling motion.

#### Muscles

13-6. The body's muscles serve as its powerhouse, providing the force needed for movement and various physiological functions. Within the body, there exist three main types of muscles, each with its unique roles and characteristics. These include skeletal muscles, which control movement in the extremities and outer body areas, cardiac muscles, found exclusively in the heart, and smooth muscles, lining the walls of arteries and the gastrointestinal tract.

#### Cartilage

**13-7.** Cartilage serves as a resilient and pliable tissue that provides cushioning and support between bones at the joints, facilitating smooth movement. In addition to its role in joint function, cartilage also forms essential structures elsewhere in the body, such as the larynx and the external portions of the ears, contributing to their flexibility and shape.

#### Ligaments

13-8. Ligaments play a crucial role in the body's structural integrity, serving as sturdy bands of connective tissue that link bones together at joints. They provide stability and support, helping to prevent excessive movement and potential joint injuries.

#### Tendons

13-9. Tendons serve as soft tissue connectors that attach muscles to bones, enabling movement and transmitting the force generated by muscles to the bones they are connected to. Despite their flexibility, tendons possess considerable toughness, providing durability to withstand the stresses of movement and physical activity.

### Types of Fractures

13-10. A fracture can be a closed or open fracture. A closed fracture (see figure 13-1) means that there is no external wound associated with the fracture. An open fracture (see figure 13-2) means that there is a break in the skin in conjunction with the fracture, often with protruding bone. The warning signs that an arm or leg might be fractured include:

- Significant pain along with marked swelling.

- An audible or perceived “snap.”

- Different length or shape of limb.

- Loss of pulse or sensation in the injured arm or leg.

- Crepitus (a crackling or popping sound under the skin).

## Equipment Used for the Treatment of Fractures

13-11. The CoTCCC endorses the use of the semi-rigid splint, a 36-inch-long malleable device comprised of moldable aluminum, encased in foam padding. This type of splint is designed for versatility in the field and is most commonly employed for stabilizing fractures. Its flexible yet sturdy structure allows it to be shaped according to the specific contours of the injury site, providing effective support and immobilization needed to prevent further injury during transport or until more definitive care can be administered.

## Tier 1 Skills for the Treatment of Fractures

13-12. During the TFC phase, it is highly advisable to splint all fractures if circumstances permit. Splinting is crucial to immobilize the affected area, preventing further damage, and minimizing pain for the casualty. Proper splinting techniques ensure that fractures are adequately supported until the injured individual receives comprehensive medical treatment. For detailed guidance on how to correctly apply splints, refer to paragraph 14-13, which provides specific instructions and considerations for effective splintering in a tactical environment.

## Tier 2 Skills for the Treatment of Fractures

13-13. The "S" in MARCH-PAWS protocol denotes splinting, a crucial step in managing both open and closed fractures. For more information on fractures see paragraph 14-10 on page 122. Splinting involves immobilizing the injured limb to prevent further movement, typically using semi-rigid splints or improvised materials like boards or tree limbs. It's essential to incorporate the joints above and below the fracture site for stability and secure the splint firmly with wraps, belts, or tape. Additionally, securing arm fractures to the shirt using the sleeve as a sling can provide added support. However, caution must be exercised to avoid excessive tightness, which can compromise circulation to the fingers or toes.

13-14. Caution must be taken when splinting to ensure that it is not wrapped too tightly. If wrapped too tightly, the splint may cut off circulation below the splint. If there is numbness, tingling or increased pain, or if the fingers or toes turn pale or bluish, loosen the bandage. Increased swelling may occur; continued monitoring of the splintered fracture is required.

13-15. When splinting, first responders or CLS should avoid—

- Manipulating the fracture site too much, resulting in pain, additional damage to blood vessels, and nerves.

- Securing too tightly and cutting off blood flow.

- Failing to immobilize the joint above and below the fracture when possible.

- Causing further injury.

- Making the casualty uncomfortable during transport or evacuation.

- Splinting near or over a wound that has not been properly treated.

### Splinting Leg Fractures

13-16. Care should be taken on cravat placement. First responders or CLS should not apply a cravat on the fracture site, as the pressure from the cravat could cause additional injury to the fracture site.

13-17. The ends of the splint should not be placed against the groin, as this could interfere with blood circulation. Extra padding is used for joints or sensitive areas, such as the groin.

13-18. Before splinting a suspected fracture, the first responder or CLS should—

- Identify the location of the fracture.

- Check the pulse below the fracture.

- Watch for signs of poor circulation, including coolness, numbness, or lack of a pulse.

- Ensure the ends of the rigid objects are not interfering with blood circulation.

- If there are any signs of poor circulation, loosen the splint, reposition, and reapply.

13-19. The first responder or CLS should follow the below when applying a leg splint:

- Apply the splint to the injured extremity with the limb in the position of function or a normal resting position if possible.

- Secure the splint in place with appropriate materials.

- Ensure the joints above and below the fracture are immobilized in the splint whenever possible.

- Recheck the pulse after applying the splint.

- Watch for signs of poor circulation, including coolness, numbness, or lack of a pulse. Ensure the ends of the rigid objects are not interfering with blood circulation. If there are any signs of poor circulation, loosen the splint, reposition, and reapply.

- Administering the CWMP as needed.

- Document all treatment on a DD Form 1380 and attach it to the casualty.

### Splinting Arm Fractures

13-20. Movement or manipulation of a fractured extremity may result in increased pain. The following will help to reduce the casualty’s pain:

- If possible, have the casualty support their injured arm while preparing the equipment.

- Mold the padded splint using the casualty's unaffected limb to avoid unnecessary manipulation.

- Use cravats or gauze bandages to immobilize the fracture and secure the limb to the body to minimize unnecessary movement during transport.

13-21. The first responder or CLS should follow the following when applying an arm splint:

- Place rigid objects so that one is on each side of the injured arm or forearm.

- Positioning the rigid objects so that the joint above the fracture and the joint below the fracture will be immobilized.

- Apply padding between the arm and the splint.

- Secure the splint with cravats, gauze bandages, strips of cloth, or other securing materials.

- If possible, place two cravats above and two below the fracture site.

- Use square knots to secure bandages tied away from the body on the splint.

- Check pulses lower to the affected extremity before and after the application of the splint.

- Administering the CWMP as needed.

- Document all treatment on a DD Form 1380 and attach it to the casualty.

## Expedient Techniques for Splints, Padding, Bandages, Slings, and Swathes

13-22. Splints may be improvised from such items such as boards, poles, sticks, tree limbs, or cardboard. If nothing is available for a splint, the chest wall can be used to immobilize a fractured arm, and the uninjured leg can be used to immobilize (to some extent) the fractured leg.

13-23. Padding may be improvised from such items as a jacket, blanket, poncho, shelter half, or leafy vegetation. Padding when splinting a fracture is essential for the well-being of the casualty. It promotes comfort, even pressure distribution, stability, protection against skin issues, and helps manage swelling, all of which contributes to an effective and safer management of fractures, especially in situations where immediate medical care may not be readily available.

13-24. Bandages may be improvised from belts, rifle slings, handkerchiefs, or strips torn from clothing, or blankets. Narrow materials such as wire, or cord should not be used to secure a splint in place. The application of wire or narrow material to an extremity could cause tissue damage and a TQ effect.

13-25. A sling is a bandage suspended from the neck to support an upper extremity. If a bandage is not available, a sling can be improvised by using the tail of a coat, shirt, or pieces of cloth torn from such items as clothing and blankets. The cravat is ideal for this purpose. Remember that the casualty’s hand should be higher than their elbow, and their fingers should be always showing. The sling should be applied so that the supporting pressure is on the uninjured side.

13-26. Swathes are any bands (pieces of cloth or straps) that are used to further immobilize a splinted fracture. Cravats are often used and are called swathe bandages. The purpose of the swathe is to immobilize; therefore, the swathe bandage is placed above or below the fracture, not on top of the fracture.

### Procedures for Splinting Suspected Fracture

13-27. Before beginning first aid procedures for a fracture, gather whatever splinting materials are available. Ensure that splints are long enough to immobilize the joint above and below the suspected fracture. If possible, use at least four ties (two above and two below the fracture) to secure the splints. The ties should be square knots and should be tied away from the body on the splint. Distal pulses of the affected extremity should be checked before and after the application of the splint.

13-28. Evaluate the casualty and be prepared to perform any necessary lifesaving measures. Monitor the casualty for development of conditions that may require you to perform necessary lifesaving measures.

### Warning

**Unless there is immediate life-threatening danger, such as a fire or an explosion, DO NOT move the casualty with a suspected back or neck injury. Improper movement may cause permanent paralysis or death.**

13-29. Locate the site of the suspected fracture by:

- Asking the casualty—

   - Where are you injured?

   - Do they have any pain?

   - Where is it tender?

   - Can you move the extremity?

**_Note._** With the presence of an obvious deformity, do not make the casualty move the extremity.

- Look for an unnatural position of the extremity.

- Look for a bone sticking out (protruding).

13-30. Prepare the casualty for splinting the suspected fracture.

- Reassure the casualty. Tell them that you will be providing first aid for them, and that medical help is on the way.

- Loosen any tight or binding clothing.

- Remove all jewelry from the injured part and place it in the casualty’s pocket. Tell the casualty you are doing this because if the jewelry is not removed and swelling occurs later, they may not be able remove it and further bodily injury could result.

- Boots should not be removed from the casualty unless they are needed to stabilize a neck injury, or there is actual bleeding from the foot.

13-31. Gather splinting materials. If splinting materials (splints, padding, and cravats) are not available, gather improvised materials. If splinting materials are not available and the suspected fracture cannot be splinted, then swathes or a combination of swathes and slings can be used to immobilize the extremity.

13-32. Pad the Splints. Pad the splints where they touch any bony part of the body, such as the elbow, wrist, knee, ankle, crotch, or armpit areas. Padding prevents excessive pressure on the area, which could lead to circulation problems.

13-33. Check the circulation below the site of the injury.

- Note any pale, white, or bluish-gray color of the skin, which may indicate impaired circulation.

- Check the temperature of the injured extremity. Use your hand to compare the temperature of the injured side with the uninjured side. The body area below the injury may be colder to the touch indicating poor circulation.

- Question the casualty about the presence of numbness, tightness, cold, or tingling sensations.

### Warning

**Casualties with fractures of the extremities may show impaired circulation, such as numbness, tingling, cold or pale to bluish skin tone. These casualties should be evacuated to medical personnel and treated as soon as possible. Prompt medical treatment may prevent possible loss of the limb.**

### Warning

**If it is an open fracture and the bone is protruding from the skin, DO NOT ATTEMPT TO PUSH THE BONE BACK UNDER THE SKIN. Apply a field dressing over the wound to protect the area.**

13-34. Apply the splint in place by—

- Splinting the fracture in the position found. DO NOT attempt to reposition or straighten the injury. If it is an open fracture, stop the bleeding and protect the wound.

- Covering all wounds with field dressings before applying a splint. Remember to use the casualty’s field dressing, before using your own.

- Placing one splint on each side of the fracture. Make sure that the splints reach, if possible, beyond the joints above and below the fracture.

- Tying the splints by—

   - Securing each splint in place above and below the fracture site with cravats, or improvised material. Improvised materials, such as gauze bandage, strips of cloth, belts, or whatever other materials you have, may be used.

   - With minimal motion to the injured areas, place and tie the splints with the bandages.

   - Push splint material through and under the natural body curvatures, and then gently position splint material, and tie in place.

   - Using square knots. Tie all knots on the splint away from the casualty (figure 13-3). DO NOT tie knots directly over the suspected fracture site.

**Figure 13-3. Square knots tied away from the casualty**

13-35. Check the splint for tightness—

- CHECK to ensure that the bandages are tight enough to securely hold splinting materials in place, but not so tight that circulation is impaired.

- RECHECK the circulation after application of the splint. Check the skin color and temperature. This is to ensure that the bandages holding the splint in place have not been tied too tightly. A fingertip check can be made by inserting the tip of the finger between the bandaged knot and the skin.

- MAKE any necessary adjustments without allowing the splint to become ineffective.

### Immobilizing a Fracture

13-40. Splinting or immobilizing a fracture is essential for providing immediate and effective care to casualties in a tactical environment. It contributes to stabilizing the injury, managing pain, preventing complications, and facilitating safe evacuation, ultimately improving the chances of a positive outcome for the injured individual.

#### Immobilizing an Upper Extremity Fracture

13-41. To splint an upper extremity fracture—

- Have the casualty sit up.

- Have someone support the fractured extremity.

- Remove all jewelry from the fractured extremity.

#### Immobilizing a Lower Extremity Fracture

13-43. To splint a lower extremity fracture—

- Have the casualty sit or lie down.

- Have another person manually immobilize the fractured extremity.

- Remove the footgear and expose the fracture site.

- Check distal (at the wrist) pulse and capillary refill on the injured extremity. Prepare the splint for application.

**_Note._** Measuring and forming of splints should be done on the unbroken extremity prior to placing on the broken one.

#### Immobilizing a Spinal Column Fracture

13-48. It is often impossible to be sure if a casualty has a fractured spinal column. Be suspicious of any back injury, especially if the casualty has fallen or if their back has been sharply struck or bent. If a casualty has received such an injury and does not have feeling in their legs or cannot move them, you can be reasonably sure that they have a severe back injury which is managed as a fracture. A neck fracture or injury to the back should always be suspected, and it is often impossible to be sure if a casualty has a fractured spinal column. If the spine is fractured (figure 13-28 step A on page 137), bending it can cause the sharp bone fragments to bruise or cut the spinal cord and result in permanent paralysis or death. The spinal column must maintain normal spinal position at the lower back (lumbar region) to help remove pressure from the spinal cord.

13-49. If the casualty is not to be transported until medical personnel arrive—

- Caution them not to move. Ask if they are in pain or, if they are unable to move any part of their body.

- Leave them in the position in which they were found. DO NOT move any part of their body unless they are in imminent danger.

- If the casualty is lying face up, slip a blanket or other supporting material under the arch of their lower back to help support the spine in a normal position (figure 13-28 step B on page 137). Take care not to place so much bulky padding under your casualty, as this will cause potential damage and cause undue pressure on the spine.

- If the casualty is lying face down, DO NOT put anything under any part of their body.

**Figure 13-28. The spinal column must maintain a normal spine position**

13-50. If the casualty must be transported to a safe location before medical personnel arrive and if the casualty is in a—

- Face-up position, transport them by litter or use a firm substitute, such as a wide board or a door longer than their height. Loosely tie the casualty’s wrists together over their waistline, using a cravat, gauze bandage, or a strip of cloth. Tie their feet together to prevent the accidental dropping or shifting of their legs. Lay a folded blanket across the litter where the arch of their back is to be placed. Using a four-person team (figure 13-29 on page 138), place the casualty on the litter without bending their spinal column or their neck. Avoid prolonged use, temporary if possible.

**Figure 13-29. Placing the casualty face-up with a fractured back onto the litter**

- The number two Service member positions themself at the casualty’s head. Their responsibility is to provide manual in-line (neutral) stabilization of the head and neck. The number three, and four Service members position themselves on one side of the casualty; all kneel on one knee along the uninjured side of the casualty.

- The number one Service member positions themself preferably to the opposite side of the casualty as the number three and four members. The number one member can be on the same side as number three and four, if needed. Numbers two, three, and four Service members gently place their hands under the casualty. The number one Service member on the opposite side places their hands under the injured part to assist.

- When all four Service members are in position to lift, the number two Service member commands, “PREPARE TO LIFT” and then, “LIFT.” On the command of “LIFT” all Service members, in unison, gently lift the casualty about eight inches off the ground.

- With the casualty lifted, the number one Service member recovers and slides the litter under the casualty, ensuring that the blanket is in proper position. The number one Service member then returns to their original lift position.

- When the number two Service member commands, “PREPARE TO LOWER” and then “LOWER.”. On the command of “LOWER” all Service members, in unison, gently lower the casualty onto the litter.

13-51. Casualties in a facedown position must be transported in the facedown position. The four-person team lifts the casualty onto a regular or improvised litter, keeping the spinal column in a normal spinal position. If a regular litter is used, first place a folded blanket on the litter at the point where the chest will be placed.

#### Immobilizing a Neck Fracture

13-52. A fractured neck is extremely dangerous. Bone fragments may bruise or cut the spinal cord just as they might in a fractured back. If the casualty is not to be transported until medical personnel arrive—

- Caution them not to move. Moving may cause permanent injury or death.

- Leave the casualty in the position in which they are found. If their neck and head (cervical spine) are in an abnormal position, immediately immobilize the neck and head.

- Keep their head still, if the casualty is lying face up, raise their shoulders slightly, and slip a roll of cloth that has the bulk of a bath towel under their neck (figure 13-30). The roll should be thick enough to arch their neck only slightly, leaving the back of their head on the ground. DO NOT bend their neck or head forward. DO NOT raise or twist their head.

- Immobilize the casualty’s head (figure 13-31). Do this by padding heavy objects (such as rocks or their boots filled with dirt, sand, gravel, or rock) and placing them on each side of their head. If it is necessary to use boots, after filling them, tie the top tightly or stuff with pieces of cloth to secure the contents.

**Figure 13-30. Casualty with a roll of cloth (bulk) under neck**

**Figure 13-31. Immobilization of the fractured neck with boots**

13-53. DO NOT move the casualty if they are lying face down. Immobilize the head and neck by padding heavy objects and placing them on each side of their head. DO NOT put a roll of cloth under the neck.

13-54. DO NOT bend the neck or head, nor roll the casualty onto their back. If the casualty must be prepared for transportation before medical personnel arrive at least two persons are needed to move the casualty’s head and trunk in unison.

13-55. Care is taken should a casualty have to be moved prior to medical personnel arrival. The casualty is prepared by—

- A wide board is placed lengthwise beside the casualty. It should extend at least 4 inches beyond the casualty’s head and feet (figure 13-32 step A on page 140). Avoid prolonged use, if possible.

- If the casualty is lying face up, the number one Service member steadies the casualty’s head and neck between their hands. At the same time, the number two Service member positions one foot and one knee against the board to prevent it from slipping. They then grasp the casualty underneath their shoulder and hip and gently slide the casualty onto the board (figure 13-32 step B on page 140).

- • If the casualty is lying face down, the number one member steadies the casualty’s head and neck between their hands, while the number two Service member gently rolls the casualty over onto the board (figure 13-32 step C on page 140).

- The number one Service member continues to steady the casualty’s head and neck. The number two Service member simultaneously raises the casualty’s shoulders slightly, places padding under their neck, and immobilizes the casualty’s head. Any improvised supports are secured in position with a cravat, gauze bandage, or strip of cloth extended across the casualty’s forehead and under the board (figure 13-32 step D on page 140).

- The board is lifted onto a litter or blanket in order to transport the casualty (figure 13-32 step E).
