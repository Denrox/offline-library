# Chapter 14: Expedient Techniques for other Secondary Injuries

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Secondary Injuries

14-1. Because medical personnel will not always be readily available, nonmedical personnel must rely on themselves and other Service members’ knowledge and fundamentals of first aid for secondary injuries until medical assistance is gained and initiated. This section will provide additional first aid, non-TCCC, procedures that nonmedical personnel can use in a garrison environment, or in combat situations.

## Abdominal Wounds

14-2. The most serious abdominal wound is one in which an object penetrates the abdominal wall and pierces internal organs or large blood vessels. In these instances, bleeding may be severe, and death can occur rapidly.

14-3. The first step in treating an abdominal wound is to position the casualty. Place and maintain the casualty on their back with their knees in an upright (flexed) position (figure 14-1). The knees-up position helps relieve pain, assists in the treatment of shock, prevents further exposure of the bowel (intestines) or abdominal organs, and helps relieve abdominal pressure by allowing the abdominal muscles to relax.

**Figure 14-1. The casualty positioned (lying) on their back with their knees (flexed) up**

14-4. Expose the wound by—

- Removing the casualty's loose clothing to expose the wound. However, DO NOT attempt to remove clothing that is stuck to the wound; removing it may cause further injury.

- Gently pick up any organs that may be on the ground. Do this with a clean, dry dressing or with the cleanest available material. Place the organs on top of the casualty's abdomen (figure 14-2).

**Figure 14-2. Protruding organs placed near the wound**

**_Note._** DO NOT probe, clean, or try to remove any foreign object from the abdomen. DO NOT touch with bare hands any exposed organs. DO NOT push organs back inside the body.

14-5. It may be necessary to wear improvised dressing (for example strips of cloth, a t-shirt, or the cleanest material available). Stabilize any protruding or impaled objects and apply a sterile abdominal dressing as follows:

**_Note._** Protruding abdominal organs should be kept moist to prevent the tissue from drying out. A moist sterile dressing should be applied if available.

- Using the sterile side of the dressing, or other clean material, place any protruding organs near the wound. (DO NOT try to push organs back inside the body.)

- Ensure that the dressing is large enough to cover the entire mass of protruding organs or area of the wound.

- If large enough to cover the affected area, place the sterile side of the plastic wrapper directly over the wound.

- Moisten the dressing with clean water prior to placing a dressing over the exposed organs.

- Place the dressing directly on top of the wound or plastic wrapper, if used.

- Tie the dressings loosely at the casualty’s side.

14-6. If two dressings are needed to cover a large wound, repeat steps in paragraph 15-5. If necessary, loosely cover the dressing with a cravat, elastic bandage, or gauze bandage. Tie them on the side of the casualty opposite to that of the dressing ties.

14-7. To avoid causing further injury to the casualty—

- Do not touch any exposed organs.

- Do not try to push any exposed organs back into the body.

- Do not tie the dressing tails tightly or directly over the wound.

- Do not give the casualty anything by mouth.

**_Note._** Continue to evaluate the casualty and provide necessary first aid.

14-8. Prepare the casualty for evacuation as follows:

- Place the casualty on their back (face up) with the knees flexed.

- Keep the patient warm, cover with a blanket as needed.

- If evacuation is delayed, check the casualty for shock every five minutes.

- Seek medical aid.

- Record the procedures applied on DD Form 1380.

14-9. The first responder continues to evaluate the casualties and is prepared to perform basic first aid measures. Always check for both entry and exit wounds. If there are two wounds (entry and exit), treat the wound that appears more serious first (for example, the heavier bleeding, protruding organs, larger wound, and so forth). It may be necessary to improvise dressings for the second wound by using strips of cloth, a t- shirt, or the cleanest material available.

14-10. Use the casualty’s dressing from the first aid kit, not your own. If the dressing is not large enough to cover the entire wound, the plastic wrapper from the dressing may be used to cover the wound first (placing the dressing on top). Open the plastic wrapper carefully without touching the inner surface. If necessary, other improvised dressings may be made from clothing, blankets, or the cleanest materials available.

14-11. Hypothermia prevention measures are taken with abdominal injuries due to the risk of rapid heat loss from the exposed abdominal cavity or organs. Moisten the dressing with clean water prior to placing a dressing over the exposed organs and seeking medical assistance.

### Warning

**If there is an object extending from the wound, DO NOT remove it. Place as much of the wrapper over the wound as possible without dislodging or moving the object. DO NOT place the wrapper over the object.**

### Warning

**When the dressing is applied, DO NOT put pressure on the wound or exposed internal parts, because pressure could cause further injury (such as vomiting or ruptured intestines). Therefore, tie the dressing ties (tails) loosely at the casualty’s side, not directly over the dressing.**

### Caution

DO NOT give casualties with abdominal wounds food or water (moistening the lips is allowed).

## Bandaging

14-12. Expedient bandaging techniques are of paramount importance for the U.S. military due to their ability to provide immediate and effective medical care in combat situations. In the fast-paced and unpredictable nature of military operations, Service members often face severe injuries that require immediate attention to stabilize the wounded and prevent further harm. Expedient bandaging techniques enable military personnel to apply life-saving measures to control bleeding and increase the chances of survival for injured personnel. These techniques not only buy valuable time until medical help arrives but also contribute to the overall mission readiness by ensuring that Service members can continue their duties with confidence and knowing that essential medical support is readily available when needed.

### Shoulder Bandage

14-13. To apply shoulder bandages attached to the field first aid dressing—

- Take one bandage across the chest, the other across the back, and under the arm opposite the injured shoulder.

- Tie the ends with a square knot (figure 14-3 on page 144).

- Ensure that the bandages are snug but not too tight, allowing for proper circulation and comfort for the casualty.

**Figure 14-3. The shoulder bandage**

14-14. To apply a bandage to the shoulder or armpit—

- Make an extended bandage by using two cravats or an elastic bandage (figure 14-4 step A on page 145); place the end of the first cravat along the base of the second one (figure 14-4 step B on page 145).

- Fold the two bandages into a single extended bandage (figure 14-4 step C on page 145).

- Fold the extended bandage into a single cravat (figure 14-4 step D on page 145). After folding, secure the thicker part (overlap) with tape or two or more safety pins (figure 14-4 step E on page 145).

- Place the middle of the cravat under the armpit so that the front end is longer than the back end and safety pins are on the outside (figure 14-4 step F on page 145).

- Cross the ends on top of the shoulder (figure 14-4 step G on page 145).

- Take one of the bandages, which ends across the back and under the arm on the opposite side and the other end across the chest. Tie the ends (figure 14-4 step H on page 145).

14-15. Be sure to place sufficient wadding in the armpit. DO NOT tie the bandage too tightly. Avoid compressing the major blood vessels in the armpit.

**Figure 14-4. The extended cravat applied to the shoulder or armpit**

### Elbow Bandage

14-16. To apply a cravat to the elbow—

- Bend the arm at the elbow and place the middle of the cravat at the point of the elbow bringing the ends upward (figure 14-5 step A on page 146).

- Bring the ends across, extending both downward (figure 14-5 step B on page 146).

- Take both ends around the arm and tie them with a square knot at the front of the elbow (figure 14-5 step C on page 146).

**Figure 14-5. The elbow bandage**

**CAUTION** If an elbow fracture is suspected, DO NOT bend the elbow; bandage it in the position found.

### Hand Bandage

14-17. To apply a bandage to the hand—

- Place the hand in the middle of the cravat or gauze bandage with the wrist at the base of the bandage (figure 14-6 step A). Ensure that the fingers are separated with absorbent material to prevent chafing and irritation of the skin.

- Place the apex over the fingers and tuck any excess material into the pleats on each side of the hand (figure 14-6 step B).

- Cross the ends on top of the hand, take them around the wrist, and tie them (figure 14-6 step C through step E) with a square knot.

**Figure 14-6. Bandage applied to the hand**

14-18. To apply a bandage to the palm of the hand—

- Lay the middle of the cravat or gauze bandage over the palm of the hand with the ends hanging down on each side (figure 14-7 step A).

- Take the end of the cravat or gauze bandage at the little finger across the back of the hand, extending this upward over the base of the thumb; then bring it downward across the palm (figure 14-7 step B).

- Take the thumb end across the back of the hand, over the palm, and through the hollow between the thumb and palm (figure 14-7 step C).

- Take the ends to the back of the hand and cross them; then bring them up over the wrist and cross them again (figure 14-7 step D).

- Bring both ends down and tie them with a square knot on top of the wrist (figure 14-7 step E through step F).

**Figure 14-7. Bandage applied to the palm of the hand**

### Leg (upper and Lower) Bandage

14-19. To apply a bandage to the leg—

- Place the center of the cravat or gauze bandage over the dressing (figure 14-8 step A on page 148).

- • Take one end around and up the leg in a spiral motion and the other end around and down the leg in a spiral motion, overlapping part of each preceding turn (figure 14-8 step B on page 148).

- Bring both ends together and tie them (figure 14-8 step C on page 148) with a square knot.

**Figure 14-8. Bandage applied to the leg**

127B **KNEE BANDAGE**

14-20. To apply a bandage to the knee as illustrated in figure 14-9, use the same technique applied in bandaging the elbow described in paragraph 14-16 on page 145.

### Caution

If a fracture of the kneecap is suspected, DO NOT bend the knee; bandage it in the position found.

**Figure 14-9. Bandage applied to the knee**

128B **FOOT BANDAGE**

14-21. To apply a bandage to the foot—

- Place the foot in the middle of the cravat, elastic bandage, or gauze bandage with the heel well forward of the base (figure 14-10 step A). Ensure that the toes are separated by absorbent material to prevent chafing and irritation of the skin.

- Place the apex over the top of the foot and tuck any excess material into the pleats on each side of the foot (figure 14-10 step B).

- Cross the ends on top of the foot, take them around the ankle, and tie them at the front of the ankle (figure 14-10 step C through step E).

**Figure 14-10. Bandage applied to the foot**

## Impalement Injuries

14-22. Depending upon where the impaled object is located on the body, extreme care must be exercised. Improper methods may cause severe injury and possess the potential for further disability and death.

14-23. Apply first aid for an impaled object as follows:

- Tell the casualty to remain still and not to move the impaled object.

- Expose the injury by cutting away or removing the clothing or equipment around the wound site.

- • If the impalement is on an extremity, check the pulse distal (situated away from the center of the body or injury) from the injury site.

**_Note._** If an assistant is available, one person should immobilize the object while the other applies the dressings and bandages.

- If necessary, apply direct pressure using gloved hands on either side of the object.

- Place several layers of bulky dressings around the injury site so that the dressings surround the object.

- Use additional bulky materials or dressings to build up the area around the object.

- Apply the support bandages.

- Apply the bandage over the bulky support material to hold it in place.

- Apply the bandage tightly but not so tightly as to impair circulation or breathing.

- Check circulation after applying the support bandages.

**_Note._** If a pulse was felt on an extremity and cannot be felt after the bandage has been applied, the bandage must be loosened until a pulse can be felt.

   - Immobilize the affected area with a splint or sling, if applicable.

   - Check for a pulse distal (far side of the wound) to the injury site.

   - Treat for shock, if necessary.

   - Seek medical assistance.

- 14-24. If the impalement is found in the cheek and both ends of the object can be seen, perform the following:

   - Remove the object in the direction it entered the cheek.

   - Position the casualty to allow for drainage.

   - If both ends of the object in the cheek cannot be seen, immobilize the impaled object.
