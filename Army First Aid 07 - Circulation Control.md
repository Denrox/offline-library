# Chapter 7: Circulation Control

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Circulation Control

7-1. Hemorrhage control assessment and management in TFC falls under the “C,” for Circulation, in the MARCH-PAWS sequence. The body requires good blood flow throughout, and a lack of good blood flow may cause the casualty to go into hypovolemic shock.

7-2. Hemorrhage control in the TFC phase takes place after massive bleeding control that occurs in the CUF phase. It is possible that it did not occur, and this may be the first opportunity to address massive bleeding as well, so there is significant urgency. The first step is to reassess all prior hemorrhage control interventions for effectiveness.

7-3. The casualty’s hemorrhage control interventions must be frequently reassessed to ensure continued hemorrhage control. This includes reassessments at frequent time intervals and any time the casualty is moved or the casualty’s status changes. The casualty must be continually watched for signs of shock.

7-4. Controlling shock is the primary method of circulation control. Shock is the inadequate blood flow to body tissues. Inadequate blood volume inside the circulatory system results in inadequate oxygen delivery to the body’s cells. As cells, tissues, and organs cease to function, eventually the whole body will fail, and death follows.

**_Note._** Hypovolemic shock can also result from blood loss due to internal bleeding into the abdominal or chest cavity. The first responder or CLS will not be able to control internal bleeding. This condition requires rapid evacuation of the casualty.

7-5. Shock is caused by a decrease in the amount of blood volume circulating in the casualty's blood circulatory system. Shocks can have many causes such as:

- Low blood volume or hypovolemia (such as dehydration or blood loss).

- Low blood pressure.

- Heart failure (pump failure).

- Neurologic damage (infection, allergic reaction).

- Obstructive causes (such as tension pneumothorax or pericardial tamponade causes obstruction of the heart).

### Indicators of Shock

7-6. Shock is usually caused by severe bleeding, but it can also be caused by severe burns classified as partial thickness burns (2<sup>nd</sup> degree) or full thickness burns (3<sup>rd</sup> degree) on 20 percent or more of the body surface. On the battlefield, assume shock is from severe blood loss (also called hemorrhagic shock). The signs and symptoms of shock include:

- Mental confusion.

- Weak or absent radial pulse.

- Rapid breathing.

- Nausea.

- Sweaty, cool, or clammy skin.

- Excessive thirst.

- Pale or gray skin.

- Previous severe bleeding

### Prevention of Shock

7-8. It is more effective to prevent shock through hemorrhage control than to treat it once it has developed. Even in cases where shock is already present, the most critical first action is to control any bleeding. Continuously reassess all bleeding control measures, such as tourniquets and pressure dressings, to ensure they remain effective and are adjusted tightly as soon as possible. This is a crucial step in treating shock.

7-9. Internal bleeding caused by blunt or penetrating trauma to the chest or abdomen may not be immediately controlled and can lead to shock later on. Continuous assessment of the casualty is essential. Even if a casualty is not currently in shock but has sustained serious injuries, they should be carefully monitored for signs of developing shock. It's important not to wait until signs and symptoms of shock appear. While medical personnel will provide comprehensive treatments, controlling any external bleeding early can save valuable time.

## Equipment Used for Circulation Control

7-10. At present, no specialized equipment has been designated exclusively for managing circulation control. The equipment mentioned in the earlier section of this publication serves to support and enhance circulation control measures.

## Tier 1 Skills for Circulation Control

7-11. The best way to prevent shock is to control bleeding. Responders should continuously monitor and reassess the effectiveness of bleeding control measures to ensure they are working, TQ application, wound packing with hemostatic dressing, a pressure dressing, or a combination of these.

7-12. After reassessing TQs and hemostatic dressings, the TCCC guideline on shock states: “Assess for hemorrhagic shock” (altered mental status in the absence of brain injury or weak and absent radial pulse). Reassess all bleeding control measures are still effective and TQs remain tight.

#### Basic Measures to Control Circulation

7-13. Control bleeding and then take steps to prevent shock by elevating the casualty’s legs higher than the casualty's heart if their airway tolerates lying flat on their back.

7-14. Conduct a bleeding re-check by:

- Reassess every TQ and dressing that may have been applied earlier.

- Expose the area and determine if bleeding is controlled.

- Expose and clearly mark all TQ sites with the time of TQ application if not previously done.

- When time and the tactical situation permit, check for further bleeding, and for pulses further out on the limb than the TQ.

### Position the Casualty for Shock

7-15. Move the casualty under a permanent or improvised shelter to shade them from direct sunlight. If the casualty is conscious, place the casualty in the shock recovery position (see figure 7-1), which is on their back with their feet elevated slightly above the level of their heart. This will help their blood circulation. A log, field pack, box, rolled field jacket, or other stable objects can be used to elevate their feet. If a sitting position makes it easier for the casualty to breathe, that is fine.

**Figure 7-1. Shock recovery position**

7-16. The following steps will also significantly help in the prevention of shock:

- Loosen clothing at the neck, waist, or anywhere is binding.

### Warning

**Do not loosen clothing if in a chemically contaminated area.**

- Prevent the casualty from getting chilled or overheated. Using a blanket or clothing, cover the casualty to avoid loss of body heat by wrapping the blanket completely around the casualty.

**_Note._** Ensure no part of the casualty is touching the ground, as this increases loss of body heat.

- Calm and reassure the casualty by—

   - Taking charge and showing self-confidence.

   - Assuring the casualty that they are being taken care of.

- Watch the casualty closely for life-threatening conditions and check for other injuries, if necessary.

- Seek medical aid.

- Record the treatment on DD Form 1380.

### Warning

**If the casualty has a fractured lower extremity, do not place the casualty in the shock position until the fracture has been splinted.**

### Food or Drink

7-17. When providing first aid for shock, DO NOT give the casualty any food or drink. If you must leave the casualty or if they are unconscious, turn their head to the side (see figure 7-2) to prevent them from choking, if they vomit.

## Tier 2 Skills for Circulation Control

7-18. Tier 2 skills for this section specifically address the crucial aspect of circulation control. Emphasizing the need for rapid and appropriate interventions to prevent further harm and optimize the chances of survival for wounded Service members. Circulation control involves techniques and procedures aimed at managing bleeding and vascular injuries. These are often encountered in high-stakes and dynamic combat environments.

### Pelvic Fractures

7-19. Pelvic fractures can be a cause of massive internal bleeding and impact circulation, which is the “C” in the MARCH-PAWS sequence. A pelvic fracture may be suspected if the casualty's injuries are a result of blunt force or blast trauma with one or more of the following:

- Pelvic pain.

- Major lower-limb amputation or lower-limb near amputations.

- Pelvic deformities, penetrating injuries, or bruising near the pelvis.

- Pelvic instability or crepitus, which is a crinkly, grating feeling, or sound under the skin.

- Unconsciousness or shock.

7-20. If a pelvic fracture is suspected, the casualty will require advanced evaluation by medical personnel, and you should notify medical personnel of the potential for a pelvic fracture as soon as possible.

### Pressure Bandage Reassessment

7-21. The regular reassessment of pressure bandages is crucial in managing wounds effectively. The CLS are tasked with ensuring that there is no ongoing bleeding from the wound site. Additionally, it's important to check for circulation below the pressure bandage by feeling for a distal pulse—that is, a pulse found below the level of the bandage.

7-22. If the skin below the bandage is cool, bluish, numb, or if the distal pulse is absent, these may be signs that the bandage is applied too tightly, potentially hindering blood flow. In such cases, the bandage should be loosened and then securely retied to ensure proper circulation is restored.

7-23. All dressings and bandages must be checked routinely, especially every time a casualty is moved. This practice helps to monitor the effectiveness of the initial treatment and to make timely adjustments as needed.

7-24. In situations where a pressure bandage or hemostatic dressing fails to control bleeding, a TQ should be applied approximately two to three inches above the site of bleeding, if feasible. If placing a TQ is not possible and the existing bandage is either ineffective or saturated with blood, replace it with a fresh hemostatic dressing. Ensure to pack the wound thoroughly with the hemostatic material, applying constant, direct pressure at the bleeding source for optimal effectiveness.

### Techniques for Non-medical Personnel

7-26. When medical personnel are not immediately available, non-medical personnel must depend on themselves and the knowledge of other Service members. The fundamentals of first aid for controlling circulation are crucial until professional medical help arrives. This section aims to provide essential, nonTCCC first aid procedures that non-medical personnel can use effectively to maintain circulation control.

7-27. Non-medical personnel are often the first responders in critical situations, making it essential for them to be familiar with basic first aid techniques. Understanding and applying these methods can be lifesaving when delays in professional medical treatment occur. Training in these expedient techniques empowers individuals to act confidently and efficiently, providing critical care in emergencies.

7-28. The guidance provided here includes several key first aid actions that non-medical personnel can take to manage circulation issues effectively. These include methods to minimize bleeding, stabilize the affected individual, and prepare for the arrival of medical professionals. Each technique has been chosen for its simplicity and effectiveness, ensuring that those with minimal medical training can apply them under pressure.

7-29. It is vital for all personnel, especially those in high-risk environments, to be trained in these techniques. Such training ensures that every member of a team can provide essential care in emergencies, enhancing overall safety and increasing the likelihood of positive outcomes in critical situations.

### Treatment for Anaphylactic Shock

7-30. Anaphylactic shock is a severe and life-threatening allergic reaction that significantly impacts the circulatory system. This medical emergency is marked by a swift release of histamines and other chemicals into the body, leading to vasodilation and increased permeability in blood vessels. Such changes can disrupt the adequate perfusion of vital organs and tissues, putting the casualty's life at great risk.

7-31. Vasodilation, a key feature of anaphylactic shock, involves the relaxation of smooth muscle cells within blood vessel walls, causing these vessels to widen and blood pressure to drop significantly. This physiological process can rapidly deteriorate the casualty’s condition.

7-32. The treatment of anaphylactic shock is urgent and time sensitive. Immediate actions include calling for medical help and, if available, using the casualty’s epinephrine auto-injector. Anaphylactic reactions can

manifest within seconds or minutes after exposure to an allergen, affecting the skin, respiratory, and circulatory systems.

7-33. Prompt recognition and response to anaphylactic shock are critical. Assessing both the signs and symptoms early on is essential for effective intervention. Quick and accurate identification of the condition allows for the necessary steps to be taken to mitigate its life-threatening effects.

7-34. To effectively check for anaphylactic shock, Service members should follow a systematic approach:

- Assess Symptoms:

   - Observe for sudden and severe symptoms like difficulty breathing, wheezing, or shortness of breath.

   - Check for a rapid or weak pulse.

   - Look for swelling of the face, lips, or tongue.

   - Note any hives or widespread skin redness.

   - Be alert to signs of confusion, dizziness, or loss of consciousness.

- Ask Questions:

   - Inquire about any known allergies or recent exposures to potential allergens.

   - Ask if they have ingested or come into contact with a known allergen recently.

- Observe Breathing:

   - Check for signs of respiratory distress, such as rapid or labored breathing.

   - Listen for wheezing or other abnormal breathing sounds.

- Check Circulation:

   - Assess the pulse to determine its rate and strength.

   - Look for signs of poor circulation, such as pale or bluish skin.

- Look for Other Symptoms:

   - Be aware of nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or a feeling of impending doom.

7-35. If anaphylactic shock is suspected, immediate medical assistance is crucial. The primary treatment involves administering epinephrine (adrenaline). If an epinephrine auto-injector is available, assist the affected individual in using it, or administer it yourself if you are allowed and trained. Dosage guidelines are typically provided with the injector—0.15 milligrams (mg) for children and 0.3 mg for adults weighing more than 66 pounds.

7-36. Further doses of epinephrine may be necessary, administered every 5 to 10 minutes if prescribed and symptoms persist. Additionally, provide supportive measures for treating shock, respiratory failure, circulatory collapse, or cardiac arrest:

- Position the casualty supine with legs elevated if injuries permit.

- Perform rescue breathing and external chest compressions if required.

- Monitor vital signs every three to five minutes until the casualty is stable.

7-37. Record all first aid actions on DD Form 1380 and request MEDEVAC immediately. Use CASEVAC only if MEDEVAC is unavailable and transport the casualty to the nearest MTF as soon as possible. Anaphylaxis is life-threatening, and immediate action is crucial. Seek professional medical help promptly and provide supportive care while awaiting arrival of medical personnel.
