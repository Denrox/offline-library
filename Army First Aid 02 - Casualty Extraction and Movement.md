# Chapter 2: Casualty Extraction and Movement

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Casualty Extraction

2-1. Transportation of casualties away from danger or to evacuation vehicles is a critical aspect of first aid, requiring careful attention to avoid exacerbating injuries. For comprehensive guidance on casualty transport and evacuation procedures, refer to Soldier Training Publication (STP) 8-68W13-SM-TG, ATP 4-02.13, and ATP 4-02.2. Risk assessment is essential during casualty movement, and for detailed information on this process, consult ATP 5-19.

### Warning

**In cases where a casualty is involved in a vehicle crash, there is a potential for spinal injury. Unless facing an immediate lifethreatening scenario (fire or explosion), they refrain from moving a casualty suspected of a back or neck injury. Seek guidance from medical personnel on appropriate transportation methods.**

2-2. Service members and CLS should adhere to the unit SOPs when extracting casualties from vehicles. Extraction methods will vary based on mission requirements and vehicle configurations in the operational area.

2-3. Service members should familiarize themselves with safety principles, casualty assessment, and proper training procedures. For instance, if a casualty is on fire, extinguish the flames, address life-threatening bleeding with a tourniquet (TQ) if necessary, and promptly seek cover.

2-4. To remove a casualty laterally from a vehicle—

- With the assistance of another Service member, grip the casualty's arms and legs.

- Stabilize the casualty's head and neck as much as possible.

- Lift them clear of the vehicle and transfer to a safe area on the ground.

**_Note_** . If available, medical personnel may use a specialized board or splint to stabilize the casualty's head, neck, and upper body.

2-5. To remove a casualty upward from a vehicle—

- Use a pistol belt or similar material to encircle the casualty's chest, aiding in their upward movement from the vehicle.

- With assistance from another Service member inside the vehicle, elevate the casualty using the pistol belt or by grasping their arms.

- While stabilizing the casualty's head and neck as much as possible, lift them free of the vehicle and position them on the topmost side.

**_Note._** If medical personnel are present, they may employ a specialized board or splint to stabilize the casualty's head, neck, and upper body.

- Depending on the circumstances, relocate the casualty from the topmost side of the vehicle to a secure area on the ground.

### Warning

**Avoid manual carries for casualties with suspected neck or spine injuries, unless immediate life-threatening hazards are present. Seek medical advice on appropriate movement and transportation protocols.**

## Casualty Movement

2-6. Timely evacuation of casualties from the operational environment is crucial not only for their safety and care but also for maintaining troop morale. Squad leaders bear the responsibility for CASEVAC from the POI to the platoon CCP. At the CCP, the senior medic assists the platoon sergeant or first sergeant in coordinating MEDEVAC by ground or air ambulance, or by CASEVAC. It's imperative for leaders to minimize the number of Soldiers needed for CASEVAC.

### Casualty Movement Route

2-7. Casualties with minor wounds might still be able to walk or help carry more seriously injured comrades. In the field, Soldiers can create improvised litters by threading poles—such as tent poles or antenna poles— through the sleeves of zippered combat uniform blouses or ponchos. Another option is to use a travois or skid, which allows a wounded individual to be strapped on and pulled by one person. Casualties can be evacuated from the POI to the CCP or directly to the BAS by litter teams. Depending on the situation, casualties may stay with the unit until transportation is available, or they might be left at a designated location for later retrieval. For more information on making and using improvised litter, refer to ATP 4-02.13.

2-8. From the platoon area, casualties are typically evacuated to the company CCP and then to the BAS. The company first sergeant, with support from the platoon sergeant, is usually tasked with moving casualties from the platoon CCP to the company CCP. Unit SOPs should cover this process, including casualty marking during limited visibility conditions. Small chemical lights can be effective for this purpose. Once casualties are gathered, assessed, and treated, they are transferred to the company CCP. Upon arrival, this process repeats until they are evacuated back to the BAS.

2-9. In cases of wide dispersion within the company, casualties may be evacuated directly from the platoon CCP using ground or air platforms. Aeromedical evacuation could be limited due to weather, terrain, or enemy presence. Sometimes casualties must be moved to the company CCP or battalion combat trains before rotary-wing aircraft evacuation becomes feasible. If there aren't enough battalion ambulances, supply vehicles may transport casualties to the BAS after delivering supplies. Urgent casualties are prioritized for MEDEVAC, while less critical cases may be moved by other means. Occasionally, the platoon sergeant may direct aid and litter teams to carry casualties to the rear.

### Casualty Handling

2-10. Casualties evacuated manually must be handled with care to avoid exacerbating their injuries. Rough handling could cause further harm, so movement efforts should be organized and methodical. Each movement should be deliberate and gentle, considering the casualties’ injuries and providing necessary first aid or TCCC as appropriate. Immediate movement may be necessary in certain safety-critical situations, such as a burning vehicle or enemy fire. However, manual carries should not be used for casualties with suspected neck or spine injuries unless there is an immediate life-threatening hazard, and medical advice should be sought.

2-11. Various methods for carrying and dragging litters can be used, depending on the casualty's condition, the terrain, and the level of enemy threat. When planning how to move a casualty, factors to consider include cover and concealment, the weight of the casualty versus the size and strength of the rescuer, nature of the casualty's wounds, the distance to cover, the carrying technique, environmental hazards, and enemy activity. Rescuers must be prepared to adapt their plans quickly based on the changing dynamics of the situation to ensure the casualty is moved swiftly and safely. Special care is also necessary when moving casualties near

different aircraft, vehicles, and ships, and responders should be knowledgeable about the specific hazards each platform presents.

2-12. Once a casualty has been treated with a hasty TQ, if the tactical situation allows, priority shifts to getting them to cover and out of enemy fire. Carries and drags enable responders or the CLS to accomplish this swiftly without worsening the casualty's condition. When providing first aid and considering casualty transport, responders must carefully assess the situation, particularly in scenarios like motor vehicle accidents where spinal injuries are a concern. In such cases, moving the casualty may only be advisable if there's an immediate threat to their life, with trained medical personnel ultimately directing transportation decisions.

#### Manual Carries

2-13. Manual carries pose physical strain on the bearers and carries the risk of exacerbating the casualty's injuries. However, they can be crucial in saving a casualty's life when other means of transport are unavailable or impractical due to terrain or tactical considerations. Depending on factors such as the bearers' strength and endurance, the casualty's weight, nature of injuries, and obstacles encountered, the distance a casualty can be manually carried varies. Carries can be employed for both conscious and unconscious casualties by one or two bearers. When executed correctly, carries offer the casualty greater protection from additional harm compared to drags and can facilitate movement over longer distances, typically ranging from 50 to 300 meters. For more information on manual carries refer to ATP 4-02.13.

#### Drags

2-14. Drags, on the other hand, are used when swift removal from dangerous or hostile environments is imperative. They allow the bearer and casualty to maintain a low profile, utilizing cover and concealment to evade hazards. Drags are generally employed for shorter distances, typically up to 50 meters. It's important to exercise caution during casualty handling to avoid causing further harm. For more information on drags refer to ATP 4-02.13.

### Litter Evacuation

2-15. Whenever possible, transporting a casualty on a litter is preferred over manual carries. Litter transport offers greater comfort to the casualty and reduces the risk of worsening their injuries. It facilitates easier and faster evacuation and enables the casualty to be transported over longer distances compared to manual carries. Ideally, a standard litter should be used whenever available. In cases where standard litter is not accessible, an improvised litter can serve as a suitable alternative until the casualty can be transferred to a standard one. It's crucial to ensure that standard litter is always maintained in a serviceable condition. For further guidance on litter evacuation, refer to ATP 4-02.13.

### Evacuation Platforms

2-16. In operational scenarios, ambulances may be insufficient in number to meet evacuation demands or may not be suitable for transporting patients over certain types of terrain. In such situations, many vehicles commonly used by units can be repurposed to transport casualties without significant modifications. Units should prioritize planning and training on how their existing vehicles can be used to transport casualties, both with and without litter. Leaders must assess not only their organic platforms but also explore the potential use of civilian, other Services, and coalition platforms. For detailed guidance on evacuation platforms and CASEVAC, refer to ATP 4-02.2, ATP 4-02.13, or STP 8-68W13-SM-TG.

2-17. TCCC is crucial for ensuring the safety and health of Service members in combatrelated situations. Specifically designed for battlefield conditions, TCCC provides a comprehensive approach to casualty care, focusing on immediate treatment and management. In the fast-paced environment of warfare, adherence to TCCC principles can significantly improve survival outcomes. It equips military units and leaders with the necessary knowledge and skills to provide timely and effective care to injured personnel, thereby reducing fatalities and enhancing survival rates. This publication highlights the vital importance of TCCC in military operations, emphasizing its essential role in saving the lives of Service members and enhancing the overall effectiveness of units.
