# Chapter 11: Head Injury

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Head Injuries

11-1. Head injuries are a common concern that can occur due to accidents, falls, or sports-related incidents. When the head experiences a forceful impact, it can result in different types of injuries. Understanding head injuries can help Service members take the necessary precautions to protect themselves or others from potential harm. It is critically important to report findings of a head injury to medical personnel at the scene since a lack of rapid, and appropriate medical care can worsen the head injury, and the eventual outcome. Head trauma can be either blunt or penetrating. Head injuries can be broadly classified as open or closed head injuries.

### Open Head Injury

11-2. An open head injury, also known as an open skull injury, is a type of injury that occurs when there is a break or penetration of the skull, exposing the brain to the external environment. It typically involves visible damage to the scalp, skull, or meninges (protective coverings of the brain). The signs and symptoms of an open head injury can vary depending on the severity and specific location of the injury. Open head injuries are considered medical emergencies. If an open head injury is suspected, it is crucial to seek immediate medical attention for evaluation, diagnosis, and appropriate treatment.

11-3. The common signs and symptoms associated with an open head injury are:

- Visible head wound. An open head injury often involves a visible wound or laceration on the scalp or skull. There may be bleeding, or an open fracture where the bone is exposed.

- Profuse bleeding. Open head injuries can cause significant bleeding due to damage to blood vessels. Blood may be visible on the scalp, face, or in severe cases, there may be profuse bleeding from the head wound.

- Skull deformity. In some cases, an open head injury may cause a visible deformity or depression in the skull, indicating a skull fracture or bone displacement.

- Loss of consciousness. Depending on the severity of the injury, loss of consciousness can occur, ranging from a brief period to an extended duration.

- Clear fluid or blood drainage. Leakage of clear fluid (cerebrospinal fluid) or blood from the nose, ears, or wound can indicate a more severe injury and should be treated as an emergency.

- Severe headache. An intense and persistent headache is common following an open head injury.

- Nausea and vomiting. Feelings of nausea and vomiting may be present after an open head injury.

- • Confusion and disorientation. The Service member may exhibit confusion, memory loss, difficulty concentrating, or may appear disoriented.

- Seizures. Seizures can occur after an open head injury, especially if the brain is directly affected.

- Changes in speech or vision. The person may have difficulties with speech, vision problems, double vision, or other sensory disturbances.

- Weaknesses or numbness. Weakness or numbness in certain body parts can be a sign of nerve damage resulting from the head injury.

### Closed Head Injury

11-4. A closed head injury refers to trauma to the head that does not involve an open wound or penetration of the skull. Instead, the injury occurs internally, within the skull. Closed head injuries are often the result of blunt force impacts, falls, or accidents, where the brain is subjected to sudden acceleration, deceleration, or rotational forces. Concussions are the most common type of a closed head injury. Closed head injuries may not be obvious or detectable.

11-5. Closed head injuries can range in severity from mild concussions to more severe forms, such as contusions, diffuse axonal injuries, or intracranial hemorrhages. It is essential to seek medical attention if a

closed head injury is suspected, as the impact on the brain can have serious consequences and requires appropriate evaluation and management.

11-6. The signs and symptoms of a closed head injury can vary depending on the severity of the injury. Mild cases may be present with subtle symptoms, while more severe injuries can have immediate and pronounced effects. The common signs and symptoms associated with a closed head injury are:

- Headache or pressure in the head.

- Nausea and vomiting.

- Dizziness and balance problems.

- Confusion and disorientation.

- Memory loss or difficulty concentrating.

- Mood and behavior changes.

- Sensory changes, such as blurred vision, or ringing in the ears.

- Sensitivity to light and sound.

- Loss of consciousness (in severe cases).

11-7. It is important to seek medical attention if any of these signs are present after a head injury, as they can indicate a range of severity from a mild concussion to more serious brain trauma. The above symptoms can occur immediately after the injury or may develop gradually over time. Symptoms may vary from person to person.

### Concussions

11-8. A concussion is a type of traumatic brain injury that occurs when the brain experiences a sudden and forceful movement within the skull. It is typically caused by a blow, jolt, or bump to the head or body, which can disrupt the normal functioning of the brain.

11-9. Concussions can occur in various situations, such as sports-related injuries, falls, motor vehicle accidents, or any other incident involving a significant impact to the head. Concussions can happen even without a direct impact to the head. The forceful movement or shaking of the body can still cause the brain to move inside the skull, leading to a concussion.

11-10. The primary characteristic of a concussion is a temporary disruption of brain function. Concussions may not always involve a loss of consciousness. In fact, most concussions occur without a person losing consciousness. Common symptoms of a concussion can include:

- Headache or pressure in the head.

- Dizziness or feeling lightheaded.

- Confusion or feeling mentally foggy.

- Memory problems or difficulty concentrating.

- Fatigue or feeling tired.

- Nausea or vomiting.

- Sensitivity to light or noise.

- Changes in sleep patterns.

- Irritability or changes in mood.

- Blurred vision or difficulty with balance.

11-11. It’s important to seek medical attention if a concussion is suspected, as health care professionals can evaluate the severity of the injury and provide appropriate guidance for recovery. The initial management of a concussion involves rest and avoiding activities that could further injure the brain. Physical and cognitive rest are recommended to allow the brain to heal properly. In some cases, individuals may need to temporarily limit activities like school or work to promote recovery. A gradual return to normal activities is typically recommended under the guidance of a health care professional.

11-12. Every concussion is unique, and the symptoms and recovery time can vary from person to person. With proper rest, most individuals recover fully from a concussion. However, in some cases, symptoms may persist or worsen, and a condition known as post-concussion syndrome may develop.

### Contusions Related to Head Injuries

11-13. A contusion refers to a type of injury characterized by damage to the tissue or blood vessels beneath the skin, usually resulting from a blunt force impact. It is commonly known as a bruise. When a contusion occurs, blood vessels are damaged, leading to bleeding and the subsequent discoloration of the skin.

11-14. In relation to head injuries, contusions can occur because of a direct blow or trauma to the head. When the head experiences a significant impact, the brain can collide with the inner surface of the skull, leading to contusions. These types of injuries are known as cerebral contusions.

11-15. Cerebral contusions can be classified as coup-contrecoup injuries, which means that contusions can occur both at the site of impact (coup) and on the opposite side of the brain due to the brain rebounding and hitting the skull (contrecoup). These injuries are concerning because they involve damage to the brain tissue itself.

11-16. The severity of a cerebral contusion can vary, ranging from mild to severe. In more severe cases, contusions can lead to increased pressure within the skull, swelling of the brain, and potentially lifethreatening complications.

11-17. It is crucial to seek immediate medical attention if a head injury is suspected, as prompt evaluation and treatment are essential to prevent further complications. Symptoms of a cerebral contusion may include:

- Changes in vision.

- Confusion.

- Difficulty speaking.

- Dizziness.

- Headache.

- Loss of consciousness.

- Nausea.

- Seizures.

- Vomiting.

- Weaknesses.

11-18. Treatment for cerebral contusions focuses on managing the underlying injury and preventing further damage. It may involve measures such as rest, pain management, observation, close monitoring of neurological status, and, in more severe cases, surgical intervention to relieve pressure and remove any blood clots or damaged tissue.

## Equipment Used for the Treatment of a Head Injury

11-19. Treating a head wound requires few essential pieces of equipment. Service members will need disposable gloves to protect themselves and the injured person from any potential infection. Sterile gauze pads or clean clothes are vital for covering and applying pressure to the wound to stop the bleeding. Adhesive bandages or medical tape are used to secure the gauze in place. Additionally, a mild antiseptic solution or wipes are important for cleaning the wound to prevent infection. Lastly, a cravat or gauze bandage can be used to create a makeshift dressing or sling to support the head if there is a suspected skull or neck injury.

## Tier 1 Skills for the Treatment of a Head Injury

11-20. Service members may not have the time or expertise to conduct extensive treatment for a head injury. Service members experiencing any of the symptoms explained in paragraph 11-17 should be evacuated as soon as tactically feasible.

## Tier 2 Skills for the Treatment of a Head Injury

11-21. Service members experiencing any of the symptoms explained in paragraph 11-17 should be evacuated as soon as tactically feasible. However, the CLS should complete the injury/evaluation/distance (commonly known as I.E.D.) checklist that is outlined in DODI 6490.11. This checklist is used to gather information, and to identify the signs and symptoms of a head injury.

11-22. Commanders or their representatives are required to assess all Service members involved in potentially concussive events, including those without apparent injuries, as soon as possible using the injury/evaluation/distance checklist (figure 11-1).

11-23. Service members who are involved in a potentially concussive event, as specified in DODI 6490.11, must be referred for a medical evaluation. This referral is mandatory if they answer "Yes" on the injury/evaluation/distance checklist or exhibit any symptoms associated with concussion at any point following the incident. Once the injury/evaluation/distance assessment is concluded, it is crucial to document the results for all individuals involved in the event. These records should then be submitted as part of the significant activities report, which is required for all blast-related incidents or other events outlined in DODI 6490.11. This procedure ensures that all potential concussions are promptly and effectively addressed, safeguarding the health and well-being of the Service members.

11-24. When dealing with trauma casualties suspected of having a head injury, it is crucial to report specific critical observations to medical personnel in accordance with the Military Acute Concussive Evaluation 2 (known as MACE 2). Immediate evaluation following the injury significantly enhances the effectiveness of the assessments. A traumatic brain injury should be suspected if the casualty exhibits any of the following signs:

- A deteriorating level of consciousness.

- Experiencing double vision.

- Exhibiting increased restlessness, combative, or agitated behavior.

- Repeated vomiting.

- Positive results from a structural brain injury detection device, if available.

- Seizures.

- Weakness or tingling sensations in the arms or legs.

- Severe or worsening headache.

11-25. If any of these signs or symptoms are present, they must be reported immediately to the arriving medical personnel. For comprehensive guidelines on reporting and treating head injuries, refer to DODI 6490.11. This protocol ensures that potential traumatic brain injuries are promptly identified and appropriately managed.

## Expedient Skills Used for the Treatment of Head, Neck, and Facial Injuries

11-26. Some wounds and burns will require special precautions and procedures when applying these measures. This section discusses specific first aid procedures for wounds of the head, neck, and facial injuries. It also discusses the techniques for applying dressings and bandages to specific parts of the body.

11-27. Head injuries range from minor abrasions or cuts on the scalp, to severe brain injuries that may result in unconsciousness and sometimes death. Head injuries are classified as open or closed wounds. An open wound is one that is visible, has a break in the skin, and usually has evidence of bleeding. A closed wound may be visible (such as a depression in the skull) or the first aid provider may not be able to see an apparent injury (such as internal bleeding). Some head injuries result in unconsciousness; however, a Service member may have a serious head wound and still be conscious. Casualties with blunt trauma should be treated as though they also have a spinal injury. The casualty should not be moved until the head and neck are stabilized unless they are in immediate danger (such as close to a burning vehicle).

11-28. Prompt first aid measures should be initiated for casualties with suspected head and neck injuries. The conscious casualty may be able to provide information on the extent of their injuries. However, because of the head injury, they may be confused and unable to provide accurate information. The signs and symptoms that a first aid provider might observe are:

- Nausea and vomiting.

- Convulsions or twitches.

- Slurred speech.

- Confusion and loss of memory. (Do they know who they are? Do they know where they are? Do they know what day it is?).

- Recent unconsciousness.

- Dizziness.

- Drowsiness.

- Blurred vision, unequal pupils, or bruising (black eyes).

- Paralysis (partial or full).

- Complaint of headache.

- Bleeding or other fluid discharge from the scalp, nose, or ears.

- Deformity of the head (depression or swelling).

- Staggering while walking.

### Unconscious Casualty

11-29. When encountering an unconscious casualty, it is essential to act swiftly and methodically, as their condition may be critical. Unconsciousness can result from various causes, such as trauma, medical emergencies, or underlying health conditions. The primary objective of first aid for an unconscious patient is to ensure their immediate safety, preserve their airway, and seek professional medical help. By following specific guidelines and employing basic life support techniques, first responders can play a vital role in providing initial assistance until medical professionals arrive. An unconscious casualty does not have control of all their functions and may choke on their tongue, blood, vomitus, or other substances resulting in:

- Loss of Breathing. The brain requires a constant supply of oxygen. A bluish (or an individual with dark skin-grayish) color of skin around the lips and nail beds indicates that the casualty is not receiving enough oxygen. Immediate action must be taken to clear the airway, to position the casualty on their side, or to initiate rescue breathing.

- Bleeding. Bleeding from a head injury usually comes from blood vessels within the scalp. Bleeding can also develop inside the skull or within the brain. In most instances visible bleeding from the head can be controlled by application of the field first aid dressing.

### Caution

DO NOT attempt to put unnecessary pressure on the wound or attempt to push any brain matter back into the head (skull). DO NOT apply pressure dressing.

- Convulsions. Convulsions (seizures or involuntary jerking) may occur even after a mild head injury. When a casualty is convulsing, protect them from hurting themselves. Take the following measures:

   - Ease them to the ground if they are standing or sitting.

   - Support their head and neck.

   - Maintain their airway.

   - Protect them from further injury (such as hitting close-by objects).

**_Note._** DO NOT forcefully hold their arms and legs if they are jerking because this can lead to broken bones. DO NOT force anything between the casualties’ teeth-especially if they are tightly clenched because this may obstruct the casualty’s airway. Maintain the casualty’s airway if necessary.

- Brain Damage. In severe head injuries where brain tissue is protruding, leave the wound alone. Carefully place a loose moistened dressing (moistened with sterile normal saline if available) and a first aid dressing over the tissue to protect it from further contamination. DO NOT remove or disturb any foreign matter that may be in the wound. Position the casualty so that their head is higher than their body. Keep them warm and seek medical assistance immediately.

**_Note 1._** If there is an object extending from the wound, DO NOT remove the object. Improvise bulky dressings from the cleanest material available and place this material around the protruding object for support, then apply the field dressing.

**_Note 2._** Unconscious casualties with open head injuries are unlikely to survive, regardless of how quickly they are evacuated.

### First Aid for Head Injuries

11-30. Provide direct manual stabilization of the head by placing your hands on each side of the casualty’s head, maintaining the position of the head in line with the body. Assess the casualty’s level of consciousness utilizing the alert, verbal, pain, and unresponsive (AVPU) method. AVPU is determined by asking—

- Is the casualty **alert** ? A.

- Does the casualty respond to **verbal** stimuli or commands? V.

- Does the casualty respond to **painful** stimulus? P.

- Is the casualty **unconscious** ? U.

11-31. The casualty with a head injury (or suspected head injury) should be continually monitored for the development of conditions that may require basic lifesaving measures. After initiating first aid measures, request medical assistance and evacuation. If dedicated MEDEVAC assets are not available, transport the casualty to an MTF as soon as the situation permits. The first aid provider should not attempt to remove a protruding object from the head or give the casualty anything to eat or drink. Furthermore, the first aid provider should be prepared to—

- Clear the airway.

- Control bleeding (external).

- Administer first aid measures for shock.

- Keep the casualty warm.

- Protect the wound.

#### Initiate Treatment for a Superficial Head Injury

11-32. Provide first aid for a superficial head injury by—

- Applying a dressing.

- Observing for abnormal behavior or evidence of complications.

- Documenting on the TCCC card.

#### Head Injury Involving Trauma

11-33. Provide first aid for a head injury involving trauma as follows:

- Maintain a patent airway using the jaw-thrust maneuver.

- Dress the head wounds.

- Control bleeding.

- Treat for shock.

- Seek medical aid.

- Monitor the casualty for convulsions or seizures.

11-34. Monitor unstable casualties for the following—

- Level of consciousness by using the AVPU indicators.

- Pupils respond to light and are equal in size.

- Motor functions, casualty’s strength, mobility, coordination, and sensation.

- Seek medical aid.

- Evacuate the casualty as soon as possible.

### Neck Injuries

11-35. Neck injuries may result in heavy bleeding. Apply pressure above and below the injury. Do not interfere with the breathing process and attempt to control the bleeding. Apply a dressing. When blunt trauma occurs, always evaluate the casualty for a possible neck fracture or spinal cord injury; if suspected, seek medical treatment immediately.

**_Note._** Establish and maintain the airway in cases of facial or neck injuries. If a neck fracture or spinal cord injury is suspected, immobilize the injury and, if necessary, perform basic life support measures.

### Facial Injuries

11-36. Soft tissue injuries of the face and scalp are common. Abrasions (scrapes) of the skin do not cause serious problems. Contusions (injury without a break in the skin) usually cause swelling. A contusion of the scalp looks and feels like a lump. Laceration (cut) and avulsion (torn away tissue) injuries are common. Avulsions are frequently caused when a sharp blow separates the scalp from the skull beneath it. The face and scalp are richly supplied with blood vessels (arteries and veins) and the wounds of these areas usually bleed heavily.

#### Ear Injuries

11-37. Lacerated (cut) or avulsed (torn) ear tissue may not be a serious injury. Bleeding, or the drainage of fluids from the ear canal, however, may be a sign of a head injury, such as a skull fracture. DO NOT attempt to stop the flow from the inner ear canal or put anything into the ear canal to block it. Instead, you should cover the ear lightly with a dressing. For minor cuts or wounds to the external ear, apply a cravat or gauze bandage as follows:

- Place the middle of the bandage over the ear (figure 11-2 step A).

- Cross the ends, wrap them in opposite directions around the head, and tie them (figure 11-2 step B and figure 11-2 step C).

**Figure 11-2. Applying a bandage to the ear**

- If possible, place some dressing material between the back of the ear and the side of the head to avoid crushing the ear against the head with the bandage.

#### Nose Injuries

11-38. Nose injuries generally produce bleeding. The bleeding may be controlled by placing an ice pack (if available) over the nose or pinching the nostrils together. The bleeding may also be controlled by placing torn gauze (rolled) between the upper teeth and the lip.

### Caution

DO NOT attempt to remove objects inhaled into the nose. An untrained person who removes such an object could worsen the casualty’s condition and cause permanent injury.

#### Jaw Injuries

11-39. Before applying a bandage to a casualty's jaw, remove all loose or free-floating foreign material from the casualty's mouth. If the casualty is unconscious, check for obstructions in the airway and remove if possible. If there is profuse bleeding in the oral cavity, the cavity may require loose packing with soft bandaging material prior to applying a bandage. Care should be taken to avoid occluding the airway.

11-40. When applying the bandage, allow the jaw enough freedom to permit passage of air and drainage from the mouth. Dress the wound, and apply the bandages by—

- Placing the bandage under the chin and pull its ends upward. Adjust the bandage to make one end longer than the other (figure 11-3 step A).

- Taking the longer end over the top of the head to meet the short end at the temple and cross the ends over (figure 11-3 step B).

- Wrapping the ends in opposite directions to the other side of the head and tying them over the part of the bandage that was applied first (figure 11-3 step C).

**Figure 11-3. Applying a bandage to the jaw**
