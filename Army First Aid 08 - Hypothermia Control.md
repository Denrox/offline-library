# Chapter 8: Hypothermia Control

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Hypothermia Control

8-1. The “H” in MARCH-PAWS stands for hypothermia prevention. Hypothermia begins when the body’s core temperature drops below 97° Fahrenheit (F). It can be mild (93-96°F), moderate (89-93°F), or severe (less than 89°F), depending on the temperature. To accurately diagnose hypothermia, the core temperature must be measured rectally with a special low-range thermometer, as mouth and ear measurements won't work well in cold environments.

8-2. Hypothermia can be caused by being in a cold environment or by losing a lot of blood, a condition known as hypovolemia. This is when there’s a significant drop in the volume of blood circulating in the body. Both environmental exposure and loss of blood can lead to dangerously low body temperatures, which can be life-threatening if not treated.

8-3. Environmental hypothermia happens when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it, often from being in cold weather for too long. Hypothermia from hypovolemic shock happens due to a large loss of blood or fluids, making it hard for the body to keep warm. Both causes can lead to serious health issues, including death, if not managed quickly.

8-4. Hypothermia can happen suddenly, like from falling into cold water, or slowly, if someone is exposed to cold conditions for a long time. It can even happen in mild temperatures if a person is wet, which makes it easier to lose body heat. Factors like wet clothing, not enough food, exhaustion, and drinking alcohol or using drugs can make hypothermia worse.

8-5. Massive bleeding can cause hypothermia, which in turn can cause more bleeding because cold temperatures can prevent blood from clotting properly. This is a problem even in warm weather, and especially for people in shock who can’t generate enough body heat. It’s crucial to stop this cycle to save the person’s life.

### Signs and Symptoms of Hypothermia

8-6. In the TFC phase of care, the focus has shifted from immediate life-threatening hemorrhage control during CUF phase, to the reassessment of all previous interventions. This is followed by the prevention and treatment of other injuries and complications such as hypothermia. It is important to identify the signs of hypothermia. Some easily recognizable signs of hypothermia are:

- Vigorous shivering is typically present.

- Shivering may decrease or cease as the core temperature continues to fall.

- Conscious, but usually apathetic or lethargic.

- Confusion.

- Sleepiness.

- Slurred speech or mumbling.

- Slow breathing and drowsiness.

- Weak pulse.

- Low or unattainable blood pressure.

- Change in behavior with or without poor control over body movements, and with or without slow reactions.

8-7. The signs and symptoms of hypothermia may vary depending on the severity and can be categorized into mild, moderate, and severe stages:

- Mild Hypothermia:

   - Shivering.

   - Cold and pale skin.

   - Numbness or tingling in extremities.

   - Fatigue and drowsiness.

   - Slurred speech.

   - Impaired coordination and fine motor skills.

- Moderate Hypothermia:

   - Intense shivering or shivering stops.

   - Difficulty in speaking and thinking clearly.

   - Clumsiness and lack of coordination.

   - Cold, pale, or blue-gray skin.

   - Slow breathing and heart rate.

   - Weak pulse.

- Severe Hypothermia:

   - Shivering may stop completely.

   - Confusion and disorientation.

   - Loss of consciousness.

   - Very cold, pale, or bluish skin.

   - Slow or irregular breathing.

   - Weak or absent pulse.

### First Aid for Hypothermia

8-8. The goals for first aid for hypothermia are to rescue, examine, insulate, and rapidly transport. If untreated, hypothermia is a true medical emergency and requires evacuation. Evacuate as soon as possible with the casualty lying down.

### Caution

Do not allow the casualty to use tobacco, consume alcohol, or caffeinated drinks.

8-9. Re-warming techniques include—

- Remove the casualty from the cold environment.

- Replace wet clothing with dry clothing.

- Cover the casualty with insulating material or blanket.

- Wrap the casualty from head to toe.

- Avoid unnecessary movement from the casualty.

- If the casualty is conscious, slowly give high caloric sweet warm fluids.

- Seek medical aid.

8-10. Hypothermia is a serious condition that requires professional evaluation and treatment. Attempting to rewarm a severely hypothermic person should be done by medical professionals using appropriate techniques. While waiting for help to arrive, focus on preventing further heat loss and providing support to the person.

### Prevention of Hypothermia

8-11. Preventing hypothermia is critical in reducing the effects of heat loss and preventing deaths from uncontrolled bleeding. Immediate action to prevent hypothermia should commence right after an injury occurs. Without preventative measures, there is a real risk that a casualty may succumb to hemorrhage from a wound that could otherwise be survivable. As blood loss itself can lead to hypothermia, it is vital for first responders and CLS to control bleeding and actively engage in both passive and active measures to prevent the casualty’s body temperature from dropping.

8-12. The quicker hypothermia prevention begins, the less severe its impact will be on the casualty’s bleeding and potential shock. Minimizing exposure to the elements is essential. Actions such as replacing wet clothing with dry ones and placing the casualty on an insulated surface should be prioritized. Environmental factors such as daily temperature fluctuations, nightfall, changes in altitude, and wind exposure must also be considered as they can significantly affect body heat retention.

8-13. Prevention of hypothermia is far more manageable than treating it once it has set in. Starting hypothermia prevention measures early is crucial because a decreased body temperature can adversely affect blood clotting processes, heightening the risk of continued bleeding—even in warm conditions.

8-14. CLS personnel must be proactive in assuming that every casualty is at risk of developing hypothermia, utilizing all known prevention techniques. If a casualty is already hypothermic, the response should be even more aggressive, treating the condition as a direct threat to the casualty’s life.

### Management of Hypothermia

8-15. To prevent hypothermia, it is essential to keep the casualty warm, even in very hot environments. This is particularly crucial when a casualty is suffering from hemorrhagic shock due to blood loss, as they are at an extreme risk for developing hypothermia. Both active and passive methods should be employed to ensure the casualty's body temperature is maintained. This involves proactive measures such as providing thermal blankets, using heat packs, and minimizing exposure to cold or windy conditions. Maintaining warmth not only aids in preventing hypothermia but also helps stabilize the casualty’s overall condition.

## Equipment Used for Hypothermia Control

8-16. The hypothermia control equipment falls into either active warming (external source of heat) or passive warming blankets, or a poncho liner to treat or prevent hypothermia. The most common equipment seen is the hypothermia prevention and management kit, blankets, or ponchos.

### Active Hypothermia Management and Material

8-17. Medical personnel will distribute active hypothermia blankets based on the unit mission and load. Active hypothermia blankets are activated when their heating elements are exposed to air and can produce temperatures reaching 104° F for up to eight hours. Active hypothermia blankets are applied to a casualty who cannot generate their own heat, but not directly on their skin, because the activated blankets can cause burns.

8-18. Apply the active warming blanket from the active hypothermia materials to the casualty’s torso, not directly on the skin, and cover the casualty with the passive hypothermia shell. Continue to reassess the casualty to determine if additional methods are needed to prevent or treat hypothermia. The heat reflective shell will help to retain the heat produced by the ready-heat blanket. It has an incorporated hood and Velcro closures down each side to allow exposure of an arm or a leg. Such exposure allows the medic to attend to IVs and TQs.

8-19. If an active hypothermia device is not available, a combination of the passive warming blanket and active warming blanket may be used. Active hypothermia treatment uses heating sources such as the readyheat blanket to warm the casualty. This requires a chemical reaction with oxygen, so at higher altitudes there may not be enough oxygen to sustain the chemical reaction required to generate heat.

8-20. You must determine based on the environmental conditions whether the casualty needs both the outer shell and the heating blanket. It is sufficient to just keep them covered with the shell, because it is already hot outside.

8-21. If no active rewarming equipment is available, then use passive warming devices that will retain heat and keep the casualty dry. The casualty should be assessed for hemorrhagic shock and ensure bleeding is controlled.

**WARNING:**

**Active warming is contraindicated in a casualty who is showing signs and symptoms of hyperthermia from heat related illness.**

### Passive Hypothermia Management and Material

8-22. Passive hypothermia blankets are designed to retain the casualty’s body heat by enclosing them within a thermal barrier, keeping them off the cold ground. While passive strategies prevent further cooling, they do not actively warm the casualty. Therefore, it's critical to avoid placing the casualty directly onto cold, wet, or damp surfaces; instead, a blanket or poncho should be positioned underneath them to provide insulation. In the absence of specialized re-warming equipment, dry blankets, poncho liners, sleeping bags, or similar items should be used to retain heat and keep the casualty dry and elevated from the ground.

8-23. Key considerations include recognizing that blood loss can significantly reduce body temperature, even in warmer climates. When using a blanket or any passive heating material, it is crucial to wrap the casualty completely, including their head, but ensure the face remains uncovered to prevent breathing obstructions. Leaving any part of the body exposed can result in heat loss and worsen hypothermia. These measures are vital in managing a hypothermic casualty, especially in the absence of active warming tools.

## Tier 1 Skills Used for Hypothermia Control

8-24. The first responder must prevent, and address hypothermia compounded by massive blood loss. This is not hypothermia due to cold weather, but can be prevented by—

- Keeping clothing or uniform on the casualty.

- Replacing extremely wet clothing if possible and keeping the casualty dry.

- Keeping the casualty off the ground, by placing a barrier between the casualty and the ground or lifting them off the ground, if possible.

**_Note._** Contact with the ground increases loss of body heat.

- Using dry blankets, poncho liners, sleeping bags, or anything that will retain heat.

- Minimizing the casualty’s exposure to the environment.

## Tier 2 Skills Used for Hypothermia Control

8-25. The Tier 2 TCCC skills emphasize advanced yet practical approaches to managing battlefield casualties under hypothermic conditions. Personnel trained in Tier 2 TCCC are equipped with the knowledge and skills to effectively use available medical equipment to prevent and treat hypothermia. This includes the application of specialized gear such as hypothermia prevention and management kits, which are designed to maintain body temperature and prevent further heat loss in injured soldiers. In addition, Tier 2 personnel are trained to monitor vital signs closely, ensuring that the core body temperature is maintained within a safe range, thereby reducing the risk of hypothermia-related complications.

## Expedient Skills Used for Hypothermia Control

8-26. In situations where standard medical supplies are not available, field expedient techniques for hypothermia control become crucial. Soldiers are taught to use their environment and available resources to prevent and manage hypothermia. For instance, using space blankets or even regular blankets to wrap the victim can significantly reduce heat loss. If these are not available, utilizing materials such as leaves, grass, or even newspapers as insulation can be a life-saving improvisation. Creating a barrier between the victim and the cold ground, such as with a sleeping pad, backpack, or any available material, can also prevent further body heat loss. These improvised methods are essential skills in TCCC, enabling soldiers to provide effective hypothermia care even in resource-limited settings.
