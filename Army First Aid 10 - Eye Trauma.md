# Chapter 10: Eye Trauma

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Eye Trauma

10-1. The eye is a vital sensory organ, and blindness is a severe physical handicap. Timely first aid for eye injuries may relieve pain and may help prevent shock, permanent eye injury, and possible loss of vision. The eye is very sensitive: any injury can be easily aggravated if it is improperly handled. Injuries in the eye may be quite severe. Cuts of the eyelids can appear to be very serious, but if the eyeball is not involved, a person's vision usually will not be damaged. However, lacerations (cuts) of the eyeball can cause permanent damage or loss of sight.

10-2. Exposure to intense light can burn an individual. Infrared rays, eclipse light (if the casualty has looked directly at the sun), or laser burns cause injuries of the exposed eyeball. Ultraviolet (UV) rays from arc welding can cause a superficial burn to the surface of the eye. These injuries are generally not painful but may cause permanent damage to the eyes. Immediate first aid is usually not required. Loosely bandaging the eyes may make the casualty more comfortable and protect their eyes from further injury caused by exposure to other bright lights or sunlight. Eyesight is one of our most important senses. Damage to an eye can be irreversible if not treated correctly and as early as possible. The following signs should make you suspect a penetrating eye injury:

- Bleeding surrounding the eye, inside the eyeball, or coming from the eyeball.

- Obvious penetration of shrapnel or debris into the eyeball or eye socket.

- Protruding objects from the globe of the eyeball.

- Swelling or lacerations of the globe of the eyeball.

- Protrusion of the globe of the eyeball from the eye socket.

- Reduced vision and swelling of the eye area.

- Misshapen or distorted parts of the eye from normal.

## Equipment Used for the Treatment of Eye Trauma

10-3. The rigid eye shield is a barrier to environmental hazards and to protect the eye by providing a safe enclosure, until additional medical attention is available. The rigid eye shield is normally plastic or metallic, with a domed shape to enhance the protection of eye injuries, without applying pressure. It may be selfadhering or require tape. If a rigid eye shield is not available, or already used, the following may be used as an improvised or expedient rigid eye shield:

- Protective glasses.

- Clean cup.

- Dip can.

10-4. The Military Acute Concussion Evaluation 2 Card is an acute assessment tool for all medically trained personnel who treat Service members involved in a potentially concussive event. The Military Acute Concussion Evaluation 2 incorporates current state-of-the-science traumatic brain injury information, which looks at the systems in charge of integrating balance, vision, and movement.

## Tier 1 Skills Used to Treat Eye Trauma

10-5. If a penetrating eye injury is seen or suspected, the first responder must shield the injured eye. The casualties injured eye must be covered with a rigid eye shield, not a pressure patch. In typical situations, a concave shield is placed over the injured eye, (not both eyes), and taped in place. If there is a protruding object in the eye, bandage in place — do not remove the object. Do not waste critical time by conducting further treatment. The casualty should be evacuated as soon as possible.

### Warning

#### DO NOT apply pressure to the injured eye.

10-6. The rigid eye shield is secured over the injured eye with tape at a 45-degree angle across the forehead and cheek. Rigid eye shields should be placed over both eyes only when you are sure and at least suspect that both eyes have been injured. If a rigid eye shield is not available, tactical eyewear may be used to protect the eyes from further trauma.

10-7. When only one eye has been injured, do not place a shield over the uninjured eye to prevent eye movement. Movement has not been shown to worsen the outcome for the injured eye. Blindness, resulting from placing eye shields over both eyes unnecessarily, makes an otherwise ambulatory casualty a litter casualty, and is psychologically stressful. The first responder should be prepared to render first aid for lacerations of the eyelid, foreign bodies in the eye, extrusions of the eye, and chemical burns of the eye.

### Initiate First Aid for Lacerations of the Eyelid or Eyeball with Eye-shield

10-8. Lacerated or torn eyelids may bleed heavily, but bleeding usually stops quickly. Cover the injured eye with a sterile dressing. DO NOT put pressure on the wound because you may injure the eyeball. Handle torn eyelids very carefully to prevent further injury. Place any detached pieces of the eyelid on a clean bandage or dressing and immediately send them with the casualty to the medical facility.

10-9. Lacerations or cuts to the eyeball may cause serious and permanent eye damage. Cover the injury with a loose sterile dressing. DO NOT put pressure on the eyeball because additional damage may occur.

### Caution

DO NOT apply pressure when there is a possible laceration of the eyeball. The eyeball contains fluid. Pressure applied over the eye will force the fluid out, resulting in permanent injury. APPLY A PROTECTIVE DRESSING WITHOUT ADDED PRESSURE.

10-10. Survey the situation; if the injury is from chemical exposure, immediately wash the eye. Otherwise, DO NOT put anything on the eye ITSELF, especially if you think the eye is cut.

10-11. If necessary, remove the casualty’s headgear. Position and treat the casualty as follows:

- The conscious casualty will be placed in a seated position.

- The unconscious casualty will be placed in a supine position with their head slightly elevated.

- Gently clean any dirt or blood from the affected area around the eye.

### Warning

**DO NOT put pressure on the eye or remove anything from the eye surface. This could cause additional harm, such as loss of eyesight or loss of the eye.**

10-12. Examine the eyes for the following:

- Objects protruding from the eyes.

- Look for foreign objects in or on the eyes and damage to the eyes.

- Swelling or lacerations of the eyes.

- Bloodshot appearance of the white of the eyes.

- Bleeding—

   - Surrounding the eye.

- From inside the eyeball.

- Coming from the eyeball.

10-13. Ask the casualty if they are wearing contact lenses, but DO NOT force the eyelids open. Record and communicate to medical personnel (first responders, emergency medical technicians, or combat medics) that contact lenses are being worn.

10-14. Categorize the injury as follows:

- Injury to the tissue surrounding the eye (lacerations and contusions).

- Injury to the eyeball.

- Extrusion (the eye is protruding out of the eye socket).

- Foreign bodies.

- Protruding (impaled) objects.

**_Note._** Torn eyelids should be handled carefully. Wrap any fragments in a separate moist dressing and evacuate with the casualty.

10-15. Provide first aid for the injury as follows:

- Control bleeding with light pressure from a dressing.

### Warning

**Pressure is used for orbital injuries, DO NOT use any pressure if injury to the eyeball itself is suspected. Injuries to the eye itself require an immediate covering with an eye shield.**

- Gently clean any dirt or blood from the affected area around the eye.

- Cover the eyelid with the rigid eye-shield provided in the first aid kit, or anything that provides a hard cover over the eye such as—

   - Eye protective gear, ballistic glasses, or goggles.

   - The bottom of a disposable cup for example, one made of extruded polystyrene (foam), plastic, or paper.

**_Note._** This helps prevent any undue pressure or further damage to the eye.

- Secure the shield by applying tape from the cheek to the forehead (figure 10-1 on page 100). If no tape is available, use gauze, cloth dressing, shoelace, or string to secure in place.

**Figure 10-1. Rigid eye-shield or cup properly secured over the injury**

### Caution

ALWAYS use a rigid eye-shield to provide a barrier to prevent further damage. NEVER place anything under the rigid eye-shield or directly on the eye.

10-16. Preserve any avulsed (detached) skin or tissue in a clean sterile gauze soaked in saline (if available) and transport it with the casualty for possible grafting.

10-17. While seeking CASEVAC or MEDEVAC:

- Do not cause further injury.

- Limit movements that could cause further injury to the eye.

- Do not administer medication to prevent coughing, sneezing, or vomiting.

10-18. Consider giving the entire combat pill pack if medical treatment takes more than three hours from the time of injury and if giving the medication to the casualty does not cause nausea.

10-19. Record first aid administered on the DD Form 1380. Evacuate the casualty:

- On their back, with the head elevated and immobilized.

- With their corrective eyeglasses, even if they are broken.

- Ensure the casualty's personal belongings, such as identification and any critical medications, accompany them during evacuation.

10-20. Do not cause further injury to the eye or casualty while waiting for evacuation. Provide assurance to the casualty and stay with the casualty until relieved by medical personnel or competent authority.

### Initiate First Aid for Foreign Bodies in the Eye with an Eye-shield

10-21. With impaled objects or significant sized foreign bodies, both eyes are usually bandaged to help secure the foreign body in the injured eye. In a battlefield environment, leave the uninjured eye uncovered so that the casualty can see.

10-22. Survey the situation, if the injury is from chemical exposure, immediately wash the eye. Otherwise, DO NOT put anything on the eye ITSELF, especially if you think the eye is cut. Examine the eyes as follows:

- Look at the eyes — are the pupils equal and round?

- Look for bleeding or foreign bodies in the eye.

### Warning

**DO NOT put pressure on the eye or remove anything from the eye surface. This could cause additional harm, such as loss of an eye.**

- Foreign bodies in the eye are treated by appropriate medical personnel or by the casualty if it can easily be removed (for example, a surface non-embedded material such as an eyelash or speck of dirt).

10-23. Use the rigid eye-shield provided in the first aid kit, or anything that provides a hard cover over the eye, such as—

- Eye protective gear, ballistic glasses, or goggles.

- The bottom of a disposable cup, for example one made of extruded polystyrene (foam), plastic, or paper.

**_Note._** This helps prevent any undue pressure or further damage to the eye. DO NOT put any pressure on the eye.

- Secure the rigid eye-shield by applying tape from the cheek to the forehead. If tape is unavailable, use gauze, cloth dressing, shoelace, or string to secure in place.

### Caution

Removing foreign bodies from the eye is NOT a first aid procedure. Seek medical treatment.

**_Note._** In hazardous conditions (such as combat or unsure footing), leave the good eye uncovered long enough to ensure the casualty’s safety.

### Caution

DO NOT attempt to remove a foreign object stuck to or sticking into the eyeball. A medical officer (physician) must remove such objects.

10-24. Apply dry sterile dressings to build around and support the object. This will help prevent further contamination and minimize movement of the object. Cover the injured eye with a paper cup or cardboard cone.

10-25. Seek further medical aid immediately.

10-26. Obtain details about the injury—

- Source and type of foreign bodies if known.

- Whether the foreign bodies were windblown, or by what type of high-velocity mechanism.

- Time of onset and length of discomfort.

- Any previous injuries to the eye.

10-27. Do not cause additional injury to the eye—

- Do not probe for foreign bodies.

- Do not put pressure on the eyeball.

- Do not remove the impaled object.

- Limit movements that could cause further eye injury.

- Do not administer medication to prevent coughing, sneezing, or vomiting.

10-28. Consider giving the entire combat pill pack if medical treatment takes more than three hours from the time of injury, and if giving the medication to the casualty does not cause nausea.

10-29. Record the first aid administered and other information as required on the DD Form 1380 (provided in the first aid kit).

10-30. Do not cause further injury to the eye or casualty while waiting for evacuation. Provide assurance and stay with the casualty until relieved by medical personnel or competent authority.

### Initiate First Aid for Extrusions of the Eye with an Eye-shield

10-31. Service members may encounter casualties with severe eye injuries that include an extruded eyeball (eyeball out-of-socket). In such instances, you should gently cover the extruded eye with a loose moistened dressing and cover the unaffected eye. DO NOT bind or exert pressure on the injured eye while applying the dressing. Keep the casualty quiet, place them on their back, treat for shock, and evacuate them immediately.

10-32. Survey the situation and remove or avoid hazards.

10-33. Remove the casualty’s headgear, if necessary.

10-34. Position a—

- Conscious casualty in a seated position.

- Reassure and inform the conscious casualty of the first aid steps that you are providing.

- Unconscious casualty lying on their back with their head slightly elevated.

### Warning

**DO NOT put pressure on the eye or remove anything from the eye surface. This could cause additional harm, such as loss of eyesight or loss of the eye.**

10-35. Examine the eyes for—

- Objects protruding from the eye or eyes.

- Swelling or lacerations of the eyes.

- Bloodshot appearance of the eyes.

- Bleeding—

   - Surrounding the eye.

   - From inside the eyeball.

   - Coming from the eyeball.

- Contact lenses. Ask the casualty if they are wearing contact lenses, however, do not force the eyelids open.

- Extrusion (the eye is protruding from the socket).

### Caution

DO NOT attempt to reposition the eyeball or replace it in the eye socket.

10-36. Provide appropriate first aid for the injury as follows:

- Position the casualty face up.

- Cut a hole into several layers of dressing material and then moisten it. Use sterile or the cleanest water if available, preferably potable.

- Gently place the dressing so the injured eyeball protrudes through the hole, with minimal contact. Surround and protect the eyeball, minimizing the movement of the dressing. The dressing should be built up higher than the eyeball.

**_Note._** If available, place a paper cup or cone-shaped piece of cardboard over the eye. This material should not touch the eye. Do not apply pressure to the injury site. Apply roller gauze to hold the cup in place.

- The provided rigid eye-shield will not be deep enough to properly protect the eye extrusion injury.

10-37. Call for medical aid immediately and arrange for MEDEVAC as soon as possible.

10-38. While calling for CASEVAC or MEDEVAC —

- Limit movements that could cause further eye injury.

- Do not administer medication to prevent coughing, sneezing, or vomiting.

- Consider giving the entire combat pill pack if medical treatment takes more than three hours from the time of injury, and if giving the medication to the casualty does not cause nausea.

10-39. Record first aid administered on the DD Form 1380. Evacuate the casualty:

- On their back, with the head elevated and immobilized.

- With their corrective eyeglasses, even if they are broken.

- Ensure the casualty's personal belongings, such as identification and any critical medications, accompany them during evacuation.

10-40. Do not cause further injury to the eye or casualty while waiting for evacuation. Provide assurance and stay with the casualty until relieved by medical personnel or competent authority.

### Initiate First Aid for Chemical Burns of the Eye with an Eye-shield

10-41. Injuries from chemical burns require immediate first aid. Mainly acids or alkalis cause chemical burns. The first aid measures consist of flushing the eyes immediately with large amounts of water for at least 5 to 20 minutes, or as long as necessary to flush out the chemical and, once flushed, bandaging the eyes. If the burn is an acid burn, you should flush the eye for at least 5 to 10 minutes. If the burn is an alkali burn, you should flush the eye for at least 20 minutes. After the eye has been flushed, evacuate the casualty immediately.

10-42. When an individual suffers burns of the face from a fire, the eyes will close quickly due to extreme heat. This reaction is a natural reflex to protect the eyeballs; however, the eyelids remain exposed to possibly being burned. If a casualty receives burns of the eyelids or face—

- DO NOT apply dressing.

- DO NOT touch.

- SEEK medical assistance immediately.

### Warning

#### DO NOT attempt to force open the eyes with thermal burns.

10-43. Reassure the casualty and quickly survey the situation.

10-44. Quickly identify the substance that the casualty was exposed to such as—

**_Note._** Do not delay first aid to perform this step. Time is critical and the eyes must be irrigated as soon as possible.

- Alkali substances, which are the most dangerous of all substances are due to the penetrating factor. Common substances that contain the hydroxides of ammonia, lye, potassium, magnesium, and lime are—

   - Fertilizers.

   - Cleaning products such as drain cleaners, and oven cleaners.

   - Plaster and cement.

- Acid substances are usually less severe than alkali burns. Common acids contain sulfuric, hydrochloric, nitric, acetic, chromic, and hydrofluoric acids are—

   - Glass polish, vinegar, and nail polish remover.

   - Automobile battery acid.

- Irritants and substances that have a neutral power of hydrogen tend to cause more discomfort to the eye. Examples of irritants that may cause this damage are—

   - Most household detergents.

   - Pepper spray.

   - Smoke from burning chemicals or rubber.

### Warning

**DO NOT put pressure on the eye or remove anything from the eye surface. This could cause additional harm, such as loss of eyesight or loss of the eye.**

10-45. Check for the following signs and symptoms:

- Irritation.

- Pain and redness.

- Watering and tearing.

- Possible erosion of the corneal surface.

- Inability to keep the eye open.

- Swelling of the eyelid.

- Blurred vision.

10-46. Initiate first aid for the chemical burn as follows:

- IMMEDIATELY flood the eye with water.

- Keep irrigating the eye with running water from a faucet, low-pressure hose, bottle, cup, or intravenous setup (if available).

- Hold the irrigating tip 1 to 1½ inches away from the casualty’s eye or eyes, direct the irrigating solution gently from the inner eye (nearest the nose) to the outer eye (towards the ear) from the casualty’s unaffected eye.

- Start the transport and continue washing out the eye or eyes for at least 20 minutes or until the casualty’s arrival at the MTF.

- Dry the area around the eye or eyes by gently patting with gauze sponges or other appropriate clean material. Do not touch the casualty’s eye.

- Cover the injured eye or eyes with the rigid eye-shield provided in the first aid kit, or an appropriate device that provides a hard cover over the eye such as:

   - Eye protective gear, ballistic glasses, or goggles.

   - The bottom of a disposable cup made of extruded polystyrene (foam), plastic, or paper.

- Secure the rigid eye-shield by applying tape from the cheek to the forehead. If tape is unavailable, use gauze, cloth dressing, shoelace, or string to secure in place.

**_Note 1._** In hostile environments (such as combat or unsure footing), the eyes may have to remain uncovered so the casualty can see getting away from danger.

**_Note 2._** Burned eyelids swell to protect the underlying eyes. The eyes should be protected with the rigid eye-shield to prevent further injury.

### Caution

ALWAYS use a rigid eye-shield to provide a barrier to prevent further damage. NEVER place anything under the shield or directly on the eye.

10-47. Seeking MEDEVAC for the casualty.

10-48. Do not cause further injury to the eye by limiting movement that could cause further eye injury. Do not administer medication that prevents coughing, sneezing, or vomiting.

10-49. Consider giving the entire combat pill pack if medical treatment takes more than three hours from the time of injury, and if giving the medication to the casualty does not cause nausea.

10-50. Record the first aid given on the DD Form 1380 (provided in the first aid kit).

10-51. Evacuate the casualty without causing further harm.

## Tier 2 Skills Used to Treat Eye Trauma

10-52. If a penetrating eye injury is noted or suspected, four steps must be taken:

- Perform a rapid field test of visual acuity. Rapid visual acuity testing includes:

   - Read print.

   - Count fingers.

   - Identity hand motion.

   - Sense light perception.

- Cover the affected eye with a rigid eye shield, NOT a pressure patch. A pressure dressing could result in permanent loss of vision. Place a rigid eye shield on only one eye, unless both eyes are injured. Covering both eyes turns an otherwise ambulatory casualty into a litter casualty. Tactical eyewear is always a good way to prevent eye issues and can be used for protection if no eye shield is available.

- Administer the casualty’s complete CWMP, including all the medications contained in it. One of the medications is 400mg of moxifloxacin, which can be administered to prevent infection.

- Document all findings and treatments on the casualty’s DD Form 1380.

10-53. Pressure on the eye could force the interior contents of the eye out of the eyeball through a cut or laceration. For protruding or impaled objects extending past the eye shield, cut a hole in the eye shield to allow the object to fit through, and secure it in place. If the eye shield cannot be applied around the impaled object, then use an improvised eye shield to avoid pressure on the eye.

## Expedient Skills Used to Treat Eye Trauma

10-54. There are several expedient skills that can be employed to provide immediate care for eye trauma. Eye trauma should always be evaluated and treated by a qualified medical professional as soon as possible. The following are some skills or techniques that may be used in the initial management of eye trauma:

- Protecting the eye: If there is a foreign object, such as debris or a penetrating object, near or embedded in the eye, it’s crucial to prevent further injury. Avoid touching or rubbing the eye, as this can exacerbate the damage. Place a protective shield or cup over the eye, if available, to prevent accidental contact.

- Flushing the eye: If there are chemicals or irritants in the eye, use immediate and gentle irrigation with clean water, or saline solution can help flush out the substance. Tilt the head to the side and pour a gentle stream of fluid over the eye from the inner corner to the outer corner. Use a clean container or a commercially available eye wash bottle.

- Covering injuries: If there is a cut or laceration around the eye, apply a sterile dressing or clean cloth to cover the wound. Avoid applying pressure directly on the eye. Secure the dressing in place with a bandage or adhesive tape.

- Immobilizing the eye: If there is a suspected or evident fracture of the eye socket or orbit, it may be necessary to immobilize the eye to prevent further injury. Place a protective shield or a cup over the eye and use a bandage or tape to secure it in position. This can help stabilize the injured area until medical attention is available.

- Applying cold compresses: In cases of eye trauma accompanied by swelling or bruising, a cold compress can be applied gently over the closed eyelid. This can help reduce swelling and alleviate pain. Use a clean cloth or ice pack wrapped in a towel. Avoid placing ice directly on the skin.

10-55. The above expedient skills are meant to provide initial first aid and should not replace professional medical care. The casualty should be evacuated as soon as possible to supporting MTF to ensure a thorough evaluation and appropriate treatment for eye trauma.
