# Chapter 12: Burns

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Burns

12-1. A burned trauma casualty should be treated for all other life-threatening injuries first, following the MARCH-PAWS sequence, before addressing burns. Even if the skin is burned, trauma treatments can still be performed on or through it.

12-2. Burns can result from various causes:

- Friction Burns. These are common in situations where skin is rubbed off against a hard surface, often seen in road accidents where motorcycle riders, cyclists, or skateboarders slide across pavement, getting what's known as "road rash." Carpet burns occur from skin scraping against carpet, often during indoor sports or play. Rope burns are common during activities like climbing or tug-of-war, and sliding burns frequently happen in sports like baseball when sliding into bases.

- Cold Burns. Also known as frostbite, these burns damage the skin by freezing it. Frostbite can occur from prolonged exposure to freezing temperatures or direct contact with extremely cold objects.

- Thermal Burns. These occur when the skin touches something very hot, like hot metals, scalding liquids, flames, or steam, raising the skin temperature high enough to kill skin cells.

- Radiation Burns. Sunburn is a typical example. Other sources include X-rays or radiation therapy for cancer.

- Chemical Burns. These happen when strong acids, solvents, or detergents come into contact with the skin.

- Electrical Burns. These burns are the result of contact with electrical currents.

12-3. Burns are classified based on how deeply they damage the skin:

- Superficial or First Degree Burns (figure 12-1 on page 116): Superficial burns affect only the outer layer of the skin, causing redness, pain, and mild swelling. They are like mild sunburn and usually heal within a few days without leaving scars.

- Partial thickness or Second Degree Burns (figure 12-2 on page 116): Partial thickness burns reach the second layer of the skin and can cause blisters, severe pain, redness, and swelling. Healing times vary, and these burns may leave scars.

- Full thickness or Third Degree Burns (figure 12-3 on page 116): Full thickness burns are the most severe, these burns go through the entire thickness of the skin and can damage muscles or bones underneath. The skin may look white, blackened, or charred, and there might be no feeling in the area because of nerve damage. These burns require immediate medical attention and often need skin grafts to heal.

12-4. The size of or percentage of the burn area will be documented on DD Form 1380. The “Rule of Nines” is used to determine or estimate the burn percentage. Figure 12-4 shows the approximation for each area of the body. The body is divided into areas with each having a percentage of the total body surface area as follows:

- Head – 9 percent.

- Torso (front) – 18 percent.

- Torso (back) – 18 percent.

- Arms – 9 percent each.

- Legs – 18 percent each.

- Pubic area – 1 percent.

- Buttocks – 1 percent.

**Figure 12-4. Burn area percentage “Rule of Nines”**

**_Note._** First degree or superficial burns are not part of the burn percentage calculation and are rarely of medical significance.

12-5. General guidelines are that the size of the casualty’s palm of the hand represents approximately 1 percent of the burned area. When estimating, it is easiest to round up to the nearest 10 percent. If half of the front or rear area is burned, the area would be half of the area value. For example, if half of the front upper leg or front lower leg were burned, it would be half of 9 percent, or 4.5 percent. If half of the front torso were burned, say either the upper or the lower part of the front torso, then it would be half of 18 percent or 9 percent. Remember, the higher the percentage burned, the higher the chance is for hypothermia.

## Equipment Used for the Treatment of Burns

12-6. The CoTCCC-recommended compressed gauze and or gauze rolls are 6-ply sterile cotton that has been compressed. They are used to stop minor bleeding, cover wounds or burns, pack wounds, act as bulky material for pressure dressings, or pad pressure points in splinting.

12-7. The CoTCCC-recommended elastic bandage is a stretchable bandage. They are used to hold dressings or splints in place or can be applied more tightly to apply localized pressure on a wound. Elastic or restrictive bandages are normally not applied to a burn casualty. The application of these types of bandages are very painful and swelling secondary to the injury may restrict circulation.

12-8. The cravat is a sterile triangular shaped bandage. It can be used as both a bandage and a sling. It is commonly used to secure a splint, create a sling, or swath for an injured limb.

## Tier 1 Skills for the Treatment of Burns

12-9. Burn care in TCCC is very basic. Eliminating the source of the burn should be the initial priority. Providing any treatment prior to removing the casualty from the source of the burn or extinguishing the cause of the burn will be ineffective and harmful to the casualty and casualty response personnel.

12-10. Electrical burn care consists of—

- Eliminating the source of electrical burns.

- Turning off any sources of electricity that have been exposed to the casualty (if applicable).

- Using nonconductive material to drag the casualty away from the electrical source (if it cannot be turned off).

**_Note._** Types of nonconductive material include rope, clothing, and dry wood.

- Moving the casualty to a safe place.

### Warning

**Do not touch the casualty or the electrical source with your bare hands. You will be injured too! High voltage electrical burns from an electrical source or lightning may cause temporary unconsciousness, difficulties in breathing, or difficulties with the heart (irregular heartbeat).**

12-11. Thermal burn care consists of—

- Stopping the source of the thermal burn and remove the casualty from the source of the burn.

- • Extinguishing any flames on the casualty, cut the clothing around the burned area, and gently lift away.

### Caution

If clothing is stuck to the burn, ensure you cut around the clothing and leave the stuck clothing in place.

- Avoid grabbing the burned area while moving or picking up the casualty.

12-12. Chemical burn care consists of—

- Eliminating the source of chemical burns.

- Brushing off dry chemicals (carefully) with a clean, dry cloth.

- Flush the burned area with copious amounts of flowing water to remove any liquid chemicals.

- Applying a wet barrier (water-soaked gauze, clothing, or mud) with an occlusive dressing.

- Advising medical personnel immediately.

### Warning

**Do not submerge a chemical burn casualty in water. This will greatly contribute to hypothermia and spread the irritation.**

12-13. All burn care consists of—

- Monitoring the casualty for life-threatening conditions.

- Checking for additional injuries.

- Treating for shock (if applicable).

- Seeking medical aid.

- Annotating all treatment and care on DD Form 1380.

12-14. Close attention to the casualty’s airway, breathing is required and always assess for secondary injuries and shock.

## Tier 2 Skills for the Treatment of Burns

12-15. Along with the treatment measures outlined in paragraph 13-9 through 13-14 extra emphasis should be placed on heat loss prevention methods. Burn patients are particularly susceptible to hypothermia. Casualties should be kept off the ground and placed onto an insulated surface as soon as possible.

12-16. For extensive burns, those with greater than 20 percent of the area burned place the casualty in the Heat Reflective Shield to cover the burned areas and prevent hypothermia. Preventing and managing hypothermia for burn patients is important and occurs regardless of the ambient temperature in the environment.

12-17. Facial burns, especially those that occur in closed spaces, may be associated with an inhalation injury. These casualties should be monitored closely for potential airway issues. Do not place an NPA in a casualty with signs of inhalation burns. Notify medical personnel as soon as possible if an inhalation injury is suspected.

12-18. Analgesia may be administered to treat burn pain. Antibiotic therapy is not solely indicated for burns; it should also be given to prevent infection in penetrating wounds. Consider giving the entire combat pill pack if medical treatment takes more than three hours from the time of injury, and if giving the medication to the casualty does not cause nausea.

12-19. Interventions for warm and cool weather should be considered. The addition of blood loss can cause the body’s temperature to drop even when it is hot outside. The TQs should not be covered; keep them visible so medical personnel can easily see it.

## Expedient Skills Used for the Treatment of Burns

12-20. In emergency scenarios, especially in remote or combat zones, effectively managing burn injuries with limited resources is essential. Basic materials such as gauze bandage, clean clothing, and cravats can be used to cover burns and control bleeding. Cooling and cleaning burns should be done with available clean water, avoiding ice to prevent further skin damage. For thermal burns, it's crucial to remove the casualty from the heat source and cut away clothing gently around the burn area. In cases of electrical burns, disconnecting the power and using non-conductive materials to move the casualty is vital, while chemical burns require immediate decontamination by brushing off dry chemicals and flushing the area thoroughly with water.

12-21. Preventing hypothermia is critical in burn care due to the high risk posed by skin damage. Using emergency blankets, reflective heat shields, or makeshift insulation from natural materials can help maintain body temperature. Pain management, when possible, should include safe administration of over-the-counter pain relievers, and continuous monitoring for shock is essential. These field expedient techniques are pivotal in stabilizing burn victims and preventing further injury until more advanced medical help can be accessed, underscoring the importance of training and preparedness in these emergency interventions.
