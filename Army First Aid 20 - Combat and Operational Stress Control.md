# Chapter 20: Combat and Operational Stress Control

> **Note:** U.S. Army manual written for soldiers; parts of it assume combat conditions (Tactical Combat Casualty Care). Figures are not included. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## Overview of Combat and Operational Stress Control

20-1. Psychological first aid is just as crucial as physical first aid and is a familiar concept to many. Think back to when a simple comforting gesture from a parent soothed you as a child, or how supportive conversation with a friend helped you navigate through disappointment or grief. These actions are natural responses to emotional distress, similar to how we instinctively want to help someone who is physically hurt.

20-2. Just as we use bandages to cover wounds, we use supportive words and actions to help heal emotional wounds. Psychological first aid involves assisting those who are emotionally distressed, whether due to physical injuries, illness, or overwhelming stress. Signs of emotional distress, like overexcitement, severe fear, excessive worry, deep depression, or anger, can sometimes be less visible than physical injuries but are equally important to address.

20-3. These emotional reactions are indicators that stress is impacting someone's ability to cope effectively. When these symptoms become more pronounced, the need for psychological support becomes more urgent. Knowing how to offer this support is essential, as it can significantly aid in someone's recovery process, just as much as treating a physical ailment.

## Psychological First Aid

20-4. You must know how to give psychological first aid to be able to help yourself, your team members, and your unit to keep performing the mission. Psychological first aid measures are simple and easy to understand. Your decision of what to do depends upon your ability to observe the Service member and understand their needs. Making the best use of resources requires ingenuity on your part. A stress reaction resulting in poor judgment can cause injury or even death to yourself or others on the battlefield. It can be even more dangerous if other personnel are affected by the judgment of an emotionally upset Service member. If it is detected early enough, the affected Service member stands a good chance of remaining in their unit as an effective member. If it is not detected early and if the Service member becomes more emotionally upset, they may become a threat to themselves and to others.

### Situations Requiring Psychological First Aid

20-5. Psychological first aid (buddy aid) is most needed at the first sign that a Service member cannot perform the mission because of emotional distress. Stress is inevitable in combat, in hostage and terrorist situations, and in civilian disasters such as floods, hurricanes, or industrial accidents. Most emotional reactions to such situations are temporary, and the Service member can still carry on with encouragement. Painful or disruptive symptoms may last for minutes, hours, or days. However, if the stress symptoms are seriously disabling, they may be psychologically contagious and endanger not only the emotionally upset Service member but also the entire unit.

20-6. Sometimes people continue to function well during a disastrous event, but suffer from emotional scars, which impair their job performance or quality of life later. Painful memories and dreams may recur for months and years and still be considered a normal reaction. However, an individual needs treatment if the memories are so painful that the person must avoid all situations which arouse them, becomes socially withdrawn, show symptoms of anxiety, depression, or substance abuse. Experience with police, firefighters, emergency medical technicians, and others who deal with disasters have proved that the routine application of psychological first aid to all the participants, including those who have functioned well, greatly reduces the likelihood of future serious post-traumatic stress disorder(s).

### Interrelationship of Psychological and Physical First Aid

20-7. Psychological first aid should go together with physical first aid. The discovery of a physical injury or cause for an inability to function does not rule out the possibility of a psychological injury (or vice versa). The person suffering from pain, shock, fear of serious injury, or fear of death does not respond well to joking, indifference, or fearful-tearful attention. Fear and anxiety may take as high a toll of on the Service member's strength as does the loss of blood.

### Goals of Psychological First Aid

20-8. Psychological First Aid is aimed at supporting Service members in managing immediate stress reactions effectively, ensuring both the well-being of the individual and the operational readiness of the unit. The primary goals of Psychological First Aid are multifaceted, focusing on immediate mental health support, safety, and a swift return to duty.

20-9. The first and foremost goal of Psychological First Aid is to provide empathetic support to Service members experiencing stress reactions. By offering a supportive presence, responders can help individuals navigate their emotional responses effectively. This involves active listening, acknowledging their feelings without judgment, and providing reassurance. This supportive approach is crucial in helping Service members feel understood and less isolated in their experiences.

20-10. Another critical aim of Psychological First Aid is to prevent and control behaviors that could be harmful to the individual or others. This includes identifying signs of distress that might lead to harmful actions and implementing strategies to mitigate these risks. The ability to intervene early and effectively can prevent the escalation of stress reactions into more serious mental health issues, thereby maintaining the safety and cohesion of the unit.

20-11. Finally, Psychological First Aid seeks to return the Service member to duty as efficiently as possible after addressing their immediate psychological needs. This goal underscores the importance of resilience and rapid recovery within military operations, ensuring that personnel are mentally fit to resume their roles. Facilitating a quick return to duty not only helps maintain unit effectiveness but also promotes a sense of normalcy and purpose for the individual, which can be therapeutic in overcoming stress reactions.

### Respect for Others’ Feelings

20-12. Accept the Service member you are trying to help without censorship or ridicule. Respect their right to their own feelings. Even though your feelings, beliefs, and behavior are different, do not blame or make light of them for the way they feel or act. Your purpose is to help them in this tough situation, not to be their critic. A person does not want to be upset and worried. When they seek help, they need and expect consideration of their fears, not abrupt dismissal, or ridicule.

20-13. Realize that people are the products of a wide variety of factors. All people do not react the same way to the same situations. Everyone has complex needs and motivations, both conscious and unconscious, that are uniquely their own. Often the one thing that finally causes the person to become overloaded by a stressful situation is not the stressor itself, but some other problem.

### Emotional and Physical Disability

20-14. Accept emotional disability as being just as real as physical disability. If a Service member’s ankle is seriously sprained in a fall, no one expects them to run right away. A Service member’s emotions may be temporarily strained by the overwhelming stress of battle or another traumatic incident. Do not demand that they pull themselves together immediately and carry on without a break. Some individuals can pull themselves together immediately, but others cannot. The Service member whose emotional stability has been disrupted has a disability just as real as the Service member who has sprained their ankle. There is an unfortunate tendency in many people to regard as real only what they can see, such as a wound or bleeding. Some people tend to assume that damage involving a person’s mind and emotions is just imagined, they are not sick or injured, and that they could overcome their trouble by using their will power.

20-15. The phrases “it is all in your head”, “snap out of it”, and “get control of yourself” are often used by people who believe they are being helpful. These terms are expressions of hostility because they show lack

of understanding. They only emphasize weakness and inadequacy. The above stated phrases are of no use in psychological first aid.

20-16. Every physically injured person has some emotional reaction to the fact that they are injured. It is normal for an injured person to feel upset. The more severe the injury, the more insecure and fearful they become, especially if the injury is to a body part that is highly valued. For example, an injury to the eyes or the genitals, even though relatively minor, is likely to be extremely upsetting. An injury to some other part of the body may be especially disturbing to an individual for their own reason. For example, an injury to the hand may be a terrifying blow to a surgeon, or an injury to the eye of a pilot.

20-17. An injured Service member can sometimes feel less secure, more anxious, and more afraid not only because of what has happened to them but also what they imagine may happen because of their injury. This fear and insecurity may cause them to be irritable, uncooperative, or unreasonable. As you help the Service member, always keep in mind that such behavior has little or nothing to do with you personally. They need your patience, reassurance, encouragement, and support.

## Combat and Operational Stress Reaction

20-18. Stress reaction is a temporary emotional disorder or inability to function, experienced by a previously normal Service member as a reaction to the overwhelming or cumulative stress of combat. Stress reaction gets better with reassurance, rest, physical replenishment, and activities that restore confidence. All Service members are likely to feel stressed under conditions of intense or prolonged stress. They may even become stress reaction casualties and are unable to perform their mission for hours or days. Other combat and operational stress reactions may result in negative behavior, but are not termed stress reaction, as they need treatment that is more intensive. These negative combat and operational stress reactions may result in misconduct and stress behaviors such as drug and alcohol abuse, criminal acts, looting, desertion, and selfinflicted wounds. These harmful combat and operational stress reactions can often be prevented by good psychological first aid. Service members who commit misconduct stress behaviors may require disciplinary action rather than medical treatment.

### Reactions to Stress

20-19. Most Service members react to stressful incidents after the situation has passed. Service members feel some fear. This fear may be greater than they have experienced at any other time, or they may be more aware of their fear. In such a situation, they should not be surprised if they feel shaky or become sweaty, nauseated, or confused. These reactions are normal and are not a cause for concern. However, some reactions, either short- or long-term, will cause problems if left unchecked.

#### Emotional Reactions

20-20. The most obvious combat and operational stress reaction is inefficient performance. This can be demonstrated by—

- Slow thinking (or reaction time).

- Difficulty recognizing priorities and seeing what needs to be done.

- Difficulty getting started.

- Indecisiveness and having trouble focusing.

- Tendency to do familiar tasks and be preoccupied with familiar details. (This can reach the point in which the person is very passive, sitting or wandering, and not knowing what to do).

20-21. A less common reaction may be uncontrolled emotional outbursts; this can be demonstrated by crying, screaming, or laughing. Some Service members will react in the opposite way. They can show signs of being very withdrawn, silent, and try to isolate themselves from everyone. These Service members should be encouraged to remain with their assigned unit. Uncontrolled reactions may appear by themselves or in any combination. The person may be crying uncontrollably one minute and then laughing the next. In this state, the person is restless and cannot keep still. They may run about, apparently without purpose. Inside, they feel a great rage or fear and their physical acts may show this. In their anger, they may indiscriminately strike out at others.

#### Loss of Adaptability

20-22. In a desperate attempt to get away from the danger, which has overwhelmed them, a Service member may panic and become confused. Their mental ability may be so impaired they cannot think clearly or even follow simple commands. Their judgment may be faulty, and they may not be aware of their actions, such as standing up in their fighting position during an attack.

20-23. In other cases, overwhelming stress may produce symptoms that are often associated with head injuries. For example, the Service member may appear dazed or be found wandering around aimlessly. They may appear confused and disoriented and may seem to have a complete or partial loss of memory. In such cases, especially when no eyewitnesses can provide evidence that the Service member has NOT suffered a head injury, it is necessary for them to be rapidly evacuated. DO NOT allow the Service members to expose themselves to further personal danger until the cause of the problem has been determined.

#### Sleep Disturbance and Repetition of Dreams

20-24. A person who has been overwhelmed by stress, often has difficulty sleeping. The Service member may experience nightmares related to the stressors. Remember that nightmares, in themselves, are not considered abnormal when they occur soon after a period of intensive stress. As time passes, the nightmares usually become less frequent and less intense. In extreme cases, a Service member, even when awake, may think repeatedly of the incident, feel as though it is happening again, and act out parts of their stress repeatedly.

20-25. For some individuals, this repetitious re-experiencing of the stressful event may be necessary for eventual recovery; therefore, it should not be discouraged or viewed as abnormal. For the person reexperiencing the event, such reaction may be disruptive. The Service member needs to be encouraged to ventilate about the incident. Ventilation is a technique where the Service member is given the opportunity to talk extensively and often repetitiously about the experience.

#### Severe Stress or Stress Reaction

20-26. You do not need specialized training to recognize severe stress or stress reaction that will cause problems for the Service member, the unit, or the mission. Reactions that are less severe, however, are more difficult to detect. To determine whether a person needs help, you must observe them to see whether they are doing something meaningful, performing their duties, taking care of themselves, behaving in an unusual fashion, or acting out of character.

### Application of Psychological First Aid

20-27. The emotionally disturbed Service member has built a barrier against fear. They do this for their own protection, although they are probably not aware that they are doing it. If they find that they do not have to be afraid and that there are normal, understandable things about them, they will feel safer in dropping this barrier. Persistent efforts to make them realize that you want to understand them will be reassuring, especially if you remain calm. Nothing can cause an emotionally disturbed person to become even more fearful than feeling that others are afraid of them. Try to remain calm. Familiar things, such as a cup of coffee, the use of their name, attention to a minor wound, being given a simple job to do, or the sight of familiar people and activities, will add to their ability to overcome their fear. They may not respond well if you get excited, angry, or abrupt.

#### Ventilation

20-28. After the Service member becomes calmer, they are likely to have dreams about the stressful event. They also may think about it when they are awake or even repeat their personal reaction to the event. One benefit of this natural pattern is that it helps them master the stress by going over it just as one master the initial fear of parachuting from an aircraft by doing it repeatedly. Eventually, it is difficult to remember how frightening the event was initially. In giving first aid to the emotionally disturbed Service members, you should let them follow this natural pattern. Encourage them to talk. Be a good listener. Let them tell you, in their own words, what happened. If home front problems or worries have contributed to stress, it will help them to talk about these problems. Your patient listening will prove to the Service member that you are

interested in them, and by describing their personal problem, they can work at mastering their fear. If they become overwhelmed, suggest a cup of coffee or a break. Whatever you do, assure them that you will listen again as soon as they are ready. Try to help put the Service member's perception of what happened back into realistic perspective; but DO NOT argue about it.

#### Activity

20-29. A person who is emotionally disturbed as the result of a combat action is a casualty of anxiety and fear. The person may feel as though they are disabled because they have become temporarily overwhelmed by their anxiety. A good way to control fear is through activity. For example, almost all Service members experience a considerable sense of anxiety and fear while they are poised, awaiting the opening of a big offensive; but this is normally relieved, and they feel better once they begin to move into action. They take pride in effective performance and pleasure in knowing that they are a good Service member, perhaps being completely unaware that overcoming their initial fear was their first major accomplishment.

20-30. Useful activity can be very beneficial to Service members who are emotionally disturbed and not physically incapacitated. After you help a Service member get over their initial fear, help them to regain some self-confidence. Help them realize their job continues by finding them something useful to do. Encourage them to be active. Get them to help load trucks, clean up debris, or dig fighting positions. If possible, get them back to their usual duty. Seek out their strong points and help them apply these attributes. Avoid having them just sit around. You may have to provide directions by telling them what to do and where to do it. The instructions should be clear, simple, and should be repeated. A person who has panicked is likely to argue. Respect their feelings, but point out more immediate, obtainable, and demanding needs. Channel their excessive energy and, above all, DO NOT argue. If you cannot get them to become interested in doing profitable work, it may be necessary to enlist aid in controlling their over-activity before it results in more panic. Prevent the spread of such infectious feelings by restraining and segregating if necessary.

20-31. Involvement in an activity helps the Service member in three ways; they—

- Place their thoughts on something more productive.

- Have an outlet for their excessive tensions.

- Prove to them that they can do something useful.

#### Rest

20-32. There are times, particularly in combat, when physical exhaustion is a principal cause for emotional reactions. A unit sleep plan should be established and implemented. When possible, Service members should be given a safe and relatively comfortable area in which to sleep. Examples would be an area away from heavy traffic, noise, congestion, a place that is clean, dry, and protected from environmental conditions. The more uninterrupted sleep a Service member gets the better they will be able to function in the tactical environment.

#### Hygiene

20-33. Field hygiene is an important ingredient in a Service member's morale. A Service member who is lacking personal hygiene will not function as well as others who have had the opportunity to bathe and put on clean, dry clothing. During combat, unit leaders should stress the importance of personal hygiene. Good personal hygiene not only improves morale, but it is also a preventive measure against disease and disease and nonbattle injury.

#### Group Activity

20-34. You have probably already noticed that a person who works, faces danger, and handles serious problems better if they are a member of a closely-knit group. Each Service member in the team supports the other team members. Esprit de corps is built because the Service members have the same interests, goals, and mission. As a result, they are more productive; and are less worried because everyone is involved. This is one of the most effective tools you have in your psychological first aid bag. Getting the Service member back into the team or squad activities will re-establish their sense of belonging and security. This will go far toward making them a useful member of the unit.

### Reactions and Limitations

20-35. Up to this point, the discussion has been primarily about the feelings of the emotionally distressed Service member. What about your feelings towards them? Whatever the situation, you will have emotional reactions (conscious or unconscious) toward this Service member. Your reactions can either help or hinder your ability to help them. When you are tired or worried, you may very easily become impatient with them if they are unusually slow or exaggerated. You may even feel resentful toward them. Physical wounds can be seen and easily accepted. Emotional reactions are more difficult to accept as injuries. On the other hand, you tend to be overly sympathetic. Excessive sympathy for an incapacitated person can be as harmful as negative feelings in your relationship with them. They need strong help, but not your sorrow. To overwhelm them with pity may make them feel even more inadequate. You must expect your buddy to recover, to be able to return to duty, and to become a useful Service member again. This expectation should be displayed in your behavior and attitude as well as in what you say. If they can see your calmness, confidence, and competence, they will be reassured and will feel a sense of greater security. ### Supporting At-risk Service Members

20-36. Taking care of fellow Service members is a fundamental duty within the DOD, emphasizing the critical role of teamwork and the inherent value of each individual. Essential to this dynamic is the implementation of the battle-buddy concept, particularly when addressing COSR. Vigilance, appropriate intervention, and thorough follow-through are key elements in fostering a supportive environment.

20-37. The optimal initial response to Service member stress involves the caring and observant presence of battle buddies and leaders. The DOD supports this approach through a robust framework that extends from individual Service members to the highest levels of leadership, facilitating COSC. It is imperative that Service members recognize signs of distress among their peers, seek help, and provide consistent support.

20-38. In the context of handling COSRs, the "ACE" acronym—standing for Acknowledge, Care, and Escort—serves as a concise and memorable strategy. This method is crucial for both Service members and leaders in managing stress reactions effectively:

- Acknowledge: The first critical step is recognizing potential stress reactions in colleagues. This requires alertness to behavioral and emotional shifts that may suggest a Service member is struggling. Trusting one’s instincts and taking any signs of distress seriously are essential in this initial phase.

- Care: After recognizing a colleague's distress, providing immediate support is vital. This support can include offering reassurance, listening with empathy, and creating a safe, non-judgmental environment for the individual to share their experiences. It’s important to affirm that their reactions are normal responses to the stressful situations they have encountered.

- Escort: The final step involves guiding the affected Service member to suitable professional resources. This could mean directing them to a mental health expert, a chaplain, or another unit leader who can offer further support and advice. Ensuring the individual receives appropriate help is crucial and helps to mitigate any stigma associated with seeking mental health support.

20-39. The Acknowledge, Care, and Escort framework underscores the importance of timely intervention and the need to connect distressed Service members with suitable resources. It offers a practical guide for those who may lack extensive mental health training but are committed to assisting their colleagues.

20-40. The Acknowledge, Care, and Escort approach is a preliminary measure and does not substitute for professional mental health evaluation and treatment. Involving mental health professionals is crucial for a comprehensive assessment and care of Service members experiencing COSRs, ensuring they receive the full spectrum of necessary support.
